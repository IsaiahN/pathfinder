# Identity, isomer or functional group? A supporting derivation for "same basic forms"

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The phrase "same basic forms of reasoning" is ambiguous in a way that matters. It could mean three different figure relations. This derivation separates them so the empirical question becomes testable rather than rhetorical.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for the argument: a shared set of reasoning operations (whatever the ground finds; the framework does not name them). Bonds held: the seven, including identity ≡ (symmetric), sequence → (ordered), and comparison ⋛ (ordered). No new primitive is assumed. Anchor: human judgment (Fig 2 :37), measured with a form/familiarity discriminator.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What would "same form" even mean under the bond set?

- **Identity (≡).** Operators table :24-27 and Fig 12 :65-69: *"two names, one referent"*, test *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."* Under identity, culture A's form and culture B's form are one term with two names. Swapping the cultural labels loses nothing. This is the strongest reading of "same basic forms."
- **Isomer.** Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** Under isomer, both cultures hold the *same operations* but join them with different bonds (one uses sequence → where the other uses comparison ⋛). They share atoms, differ in bond, and are therefore different reasoning substances. This is a genuine middle position: "same basic forms" at the atom level, different forms at the molecule level.
- **Functional group.** Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** Both cultures share a pattern such as `problem → operation → answer`, with different operations in the middle slot. Same kind, different member.

These are not rhetorical distinctions. Fig 12 :95-96: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."** A cross-cultural result that reports only "both cultures use analogy" has recorded the ingredient list and not the bond. It cannot distinguish any of the three.

### Q2 — Does order matter?

- Fig 12 :73: **"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."** This is the test for the ordered bonds. If reasoning form A is `perceive → infer → act` and form B is `perceive → act → infer`, they are different terms under →, not the same form applied differently.
- Fig 12 :59 for the symmetric case: **"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."** If the operations combine under a symmetric bond, order carries no information and the apparent cultural difference is a spelling difference, which Fig 12 :23 warns a search will overcount: *"this bond is symmetric, so the four are one term and nothing consults the bond to find that out."*
- **Conclusion (`specified`).** Part of the cross-cultural variation may be an artefact of writing a symmetric relation as an ordered sequence. The framework's own notation rule (Fig 12 :27, *"→ bigger space, not a bigger vocabulary"*) says that charges the sequence when the bond is symmetric. This is a measurement warning, not an empirical finding.

### Q3 — Are the two cultures isomers, identical, or a functional group?

- Under identity: substituting one culture's form for the other loses nothing (Operators table :27). This is a one-pass check (Fig 9 :29, *"matching is a one-pass check, not a search"*).
- Under isomer: the ingredients match but the bond does not. Fig 12 :97-98: *"This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation."* The framework's own example says the notation had to change to see the difference. So a test that records only which operations are present will call two isomers "the same form."
- Under functional group: the substructure matches, the qualifier differs. This is the weakest claim and the one most easily mistaken for identity.
- **Verdict:** the framework supplies the three-way distinction and the tests. It does **not** supply a finding about any culture. Applying the tests requires the ground (Fig 2 :37). Triangulated for the definitions (Fig 12 plus Operators table); single-source for whether any culture instantiates any of them.

### Q4 — Dependency order of the bonds

- Fig 13 :75: **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."** For reasoning, the analogous constraint is that operations do not compose directly with operations without an operand between them. This is the cross-kind rule, invoked only as a general composition constraint (F7 of `01` quarantines the stronger substrate claim).
- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."** A reasoning form once settled against the ground becomes an atom for the next level of comparison. So the cross-cultural comparison is **second order**: it compares settled forms, not raw behaviours. This is why the first loop (is this a form at all?) must precede the second (is it shared?).
- **Strain, named:** the operand type of ⋛ is defined as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* (Operators table :48). Whether settled reasoning forms count as quantities at the comparison level is not decided by the figure. It needs an instrument. This is the same live strain recorded in the exemplar's `06` and in `01` F7.

### Q5 — Falsifier, written before the run

The three-way distinction is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. A cross-cultural comparison can be made in which swapping the cultural labels changes the term under a **symmetric** bond, so the symmetry test fails to separate identity from difference.
2. Two forms are shown to differ with the **same atoms and the same bond**, which contradicts Fig 12 :95-96 (the difference must be carried by the bond or the atoms).
3. A reasoning form transfers between cultures with no habitat dependence, contradicting Fig 11 :40, so the functional-group reading collapses into identity with no anchor condition.

If any fires, the supporting derivation is withdrawn. Until one is run, this remains `specified` and ungrounded.

## CONCLUSION

"Same basic forms" is not one claim but three distinguishable figure relations: identity, isomer, and functional group. The framework supplies the tests (substitution, bond inspection, substructure) and a measurement warning (a symmetric bond written as a sequence manufactures a bigger space). It does not tell us which relation any pair of cultures instantiates. That is owed to the ground, and the honest verdict on the empirical question is `gap`.
