# Outreach draft: check the output before deciding about the framework

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover note attached to a submission through a public venue in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send. Record the recipient's verdict on stage 1 before any theory is shown.

---

**Subject:** One checkable claim about information and causal structure, before any theory

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The question in effect terms:** how do information and causal structure constrain each other? Two things are being asked at once, so I am treating them as a union, not one problem: (1) what an information measure rules out about admissible causal structures; (2) what a causal structure determines about what can be registered as information at all. I am not averaging the two into one principle.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):**
> 1. An information quantity constrains causal structure by **exclusion**, not by generating it. Pawlowski et al., *Information Causality as a Physical Principle*, Nature 461, 1101 (2009), state that if Alice communicates m bits, the information Bob gains about her data is at most m bits, and that the principle is violated by correlations stronger than the quantum bound. It rules theories out; it does not construct them.
> 2. A causal structure constrains information by fixing what can reach a record. Chaves, Majenz and Gross, *Information-Theoretic Implications of Quantum Causal Structures*, Nature Communications 6, 5766 (2015): *"The correlations that can be observed between a set of variables depend on the causal structure underpinning them."* They show the information-causality principle emerges from a causal graph framework and strengthen it.
> 3. The two currencies must be kept apart. A bit cost prices a description; an action cost prices finding out whether it holds. Collapsing them into one "information explains causation" figure is a named error in the framework I am using, not a step I am asking you to accept on trust.
>
> **What this does not claim:** that there is a single correct physical information measure; that a specific causal structure (causal sets, or any named one) is the answer; that information is more fundamental than causation or the reverse; that anything here is settled by experiment beyond the two cited results. The framework returns a shape, not a name. This is a checkable constraint, not a theory of everything.
>
> **What would refute it, written before this message:**
> 1. A single information reading that discriminates the causal structures which could produce it, with no extra instrument.
> 2. A closed principle deriving both the measure and the order inside one frame, with provenance showing no import.
> 3. An information measure shown to generate, not merely exclude, an admissible causal structure.
> 4. Two causally distinct structures yielding the same complete record for every possible instrument, with no residual anywhere.
> 5. A common derived unit pricing both description bits and the actions needed to test a law.
>
> If any of these holds, the relevant claim is withdrawn. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figures, not my summary, are the authority.
> - Information Causality as a Physical Principle (arXiv abstract): https://arxiv.org/abs/0905.2292
> - Information-Theoretic Implications of Quantum Causal Structures (arXiv abstract): https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.3800
> - Causal sets, including Sorkin's "Order + Number = Geometry" and Malament's theorem: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_sets
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula and the figures are public. Run them on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and label the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> [your name]
