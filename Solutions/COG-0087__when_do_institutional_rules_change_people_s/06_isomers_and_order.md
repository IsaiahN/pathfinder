# Isomer or functional group? Union, order and the compliance/internalization split

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The two observed effects of a rule (behavior change with adoption, behavior change without adoption) can look identical from outside. Two questions are owed: (1) is the split a partition or a union, and (2) what is the dependency order of the conditions that produce each effect? A third, structural question follows: are the two effects isomers under Fig 12's definition, or merely members of one functional group?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :54 "when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again"
- Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
- A person can comply with one rule and internalize another; a single rule can be partly internalized. Effect A and Effect B overlap in the same person-rule slot across time.
- ⇒ The split is a **union**. A single lever (enforcement) clears the region where only compliance holds and leaves the intersection standing.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if every person-rule pair is determinately either full internalization or mere compliance, with no overlap and no mixed state, the union reading fails and the split is a partition.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order of the conditions?

- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- Conditions in order: (1) the rule is declared as a convention (Domain A); (2) the person's frame contacts a frame whose closure differs and the reason crosses as an atom (Domain B, Fig 6 :23); (3) the atom settles against the ground (Formula step 5); (4) only then can the discriminator observe persistence when the contingency is removed (Domain C).
- Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." Step (4) is where the field has the least instrument: normative belief is weakly correlated with behavior (SEP, Social Norms, 2011/2023; Bicchieri 2016).
- ⇒ Effort spent on (1), for example writing a clearer rule or a more inspiring justification, cannot rescue a break at (2) or (4). "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not" (Fig 3 :19).
- **Falsifier:** if a discriminator can reliably identify internalization before settlement (step 3), the dependency order is wrong and steps 3 and 4 commute.

### Q3 - Isomer or functional group?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Both effects have the same observable ingredient set: a rule, a person, a behavior that tracks the rule. Their internal bond differs. Effect A is a **cycle** (Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction") with a grounded atom that survives its use (Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use."). Effect B is an open chain ending at the external contingency; when the input stops, the behavior stops.
- ⇒ They are **isomers** at the person-rule level: same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference invisible in the behavior reading alone. They are also members of one **functional group** at the population level: both are `rule → person → behavior` molecules with different qualifiers (grounded reason versus authored constant).
- **Falsifier:** if the observable behavior together with its stated reason determines the internal bond, so that two readers using the same instrument must agree on which effect they are seeing, the isomer claim fails. The metamerism case in `05_what_shape_means.md` section 4 predicts the opposite.

### Q4 - What is the order across scales, and what is the round-trip gap R_T?

- Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."
- Symbols table :75-77: R_T is "The gap between what was sent up and what came back down: gap(x, (T_E ∘ T_A)(x)). Measures what the coarser description cannot hold."
- Sent up (x): the band-spanning observation that rules sometimes coincide with changed values and sometimes not. Abstracted (T_A): the shape claim, a crossing from declared meaning to grounded atom with w shifted. Concretized (T_E): a specific person, rule, contingency and behavior.
- **R_T is nonzero.** The abstraction drops which frame supplied the atom, whether the atom settled, the sign of the cycle, and the slot-level residual. A report that treats "values changed" as a single population variable sets R_T to zero and is the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."
- ⇒ What crosses between a cohort and the next is a method for producing and testing the value, never a recording of one person's compliance (Fig 4 :25: a recording carried upward "is a description of one occasion").

## CONCLUSION

The two effects form a union, not a partition, and must be unbundled before searching. Their dependency order is declare, contact/import, settle, discriminate; effort above a break cannot rescue the break. Structurally they are isomers at the person-rule level (same atoms, different bond, different substance) and members of one functional group at the population level. The cross-scale round trip loses the provenance and the sign of the cycle, so R_T must be reported as an enumerated set of dropped constraints rather than assumed zero. All four conclusions are `specified`, ungrounded, and carry their falsifiers above.
