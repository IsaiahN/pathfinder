## DERIVATION

### Q1: Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :54, "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55, "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."
- Fig 9 :57, "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

Apply to the question. Let M = the method condition (does the reading register against a non-updating ground?), C = the concept condition (are the categories declared and provenanced?), E = the evidence condition (do the agreeing frames draw on different evidence?). A larger, more diverse sample affects M directly. It does not by itself fix C, because a borrowed category can be administered to a larger sample. It does not by itself fix E, because more sites can still share one instrument and one analysis pipeline. The intersections M and C, M and E, C and E survive the single lever. So this is a **union**, and each intersection is a specific compound, not a harder case of a single condition.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single intervention is demonstrated to remove the residual in M, C and E simultaneously in a registered, multi-site study, with no remaining compound, the union reading fails and a partition reading is owed.

### Q2: Are two cultural applications isomers or functional-group members?

- Fig 12 :117, "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123, "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Fig 12 :94, "The arrangement is the substance."
- Fig 12 :5, "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Take two applications, A and B, of the same measurement programme. As usually reported, the ingredient lists differ: A uses sample s1, task t1 and scale q1; B uses s2, t2 and q2. They do not share an atom set in the figure's sense, so as reported they are **not isomers**. They are two members of the functional group "instrument returns a reading about a mind", with different qualifiers. The ingredient-list comparison is the flat formula: it records the components and not the bond.

The stronger reading: hold the atom set fixed (the same sample frame, the same task family, the same scale family, the same ground, the same analysis pipeline) and vary only the bonds. Let A be `{sample → scale → ground}` with the bond being direct comparison, and B be `{sample → task → ground}` with the bond being an effect description ranged against the ground. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. That is a genuine isomer claim under Fig 12 :117. It is also the claim worth testing, because it isolates what the figure says carries the information: the relation, not the ingredient list.

This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: Fig 12 :97-98 records that "silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties", which forced structure into the notation, named isomerism by Berzelius 1830. The point for a cross-cultural science is the same: "the difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Two studies can report the same sample sizes, the same scales and the same p values and still be different substances because the bond to the ground differs.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the arrangement to the ground can be recovered from the ingredient list alone, so that two applications with identical atoms and different bonds behave identically, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3: What is the dependency order, and where does the chain break?

Fig 3 :20, "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." The five links for this question, in dependency order:

1. **Perception.** Can the study tell what is in front of it, and tell it apart from what was there before? Fig 3 :5. In cross-cultural terms: can the researcher discriminate a construct difference from a response-style difference at all?
2. **Vocabulary.** Does it have terms for what the situation affords, in that culture? Fig 3 :7. This is the emic/etic seam, where a borrowed category can name a state but not a route (Fig 12 :111, "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one.").
3. **Objective.** Can it say what would count as progress here, in its own terms? Fig 3 :10. This is the pre-registered refuter and the stated mode.
4. **Planning.** Is there a route to that objective under incomplete information? Fig 3 :13. This is the choice of independent frames and the instrument improvement path.
5. **Learn and carry.** Can it name what worked, verify it and carry it to the next problem? Fig 3 :15. This is the method that crosses the membrane, not the recording (Fig 4 :22, "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down.").

Fig 3 :24-27 says the chain usually breaks between links 2 and 3: "The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments." Fig 3 :27, "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

This predicts where the field's criticism lands. The Cross-cultural psychology article records "serious shortcomings in the theoretical and methodological bases used impede, rather than help, the scientific search for universal principles in psychology"; the Cultural psychology article records over-reliance on self-report, which "can lead to relatively unstable and ultimately misleading data". Both are link 2 and link 3 failures, not failures of subject recruitment. Optimising the sample (link 1) cannot rescue them: Fig 3 :19, "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a programme with no stated local vocabulary and no pre-committed objective still produces ground-paid predictions in a new culture, the dependency order is wrong and the chain does not break at link 2 to 3.

### Q4: What do the analogues say?

- **Chemistry, isomerism.** Fig 12 :117 (quoted in Q2). Same atoms, different arrangement, decisive behavioural difference. Governs the fixed-atom comparison in Q2.
- **Animal behaviour, zone of latent solutions.** The Animal culture article: "many behaviours can be individually reinvented, while social learning influences their frequency or distribution without transmitting the behavioural form itself." This is the low-overlap analogue of convergent derivation: the same reading can arise by separate routes, and only the record of origin separates convergence from transmission (Fig 8 :51, "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them").
- **Metascience, registered reports.** The Registered report article: the design and analysis plan are "peer-reviewed before data are collected or analyzed", and "manuscripts are not rejected on the basis of null or statistically non-significant results". This is a world instance of link 3: an objective fixed before the observation arrives. It is corroboration, not a figure-derived standard.
- **Psychometrics, measurement invariance.** A candidate discriminator (Measurement invariance article): "a statistical property of measurement that indicates that the same construct is being measured across some specified groups". It is disputed within the field (see `01` S4), so it is recorded as an owed instrument with a live conflict, not a settled answer.

None of these analogues is cited as a figure line. They are world corroboration, opened and recorded in `10_source_ledger.csv`.

## CONCLUSION

The union reading survives Q1 with a stated falsifier. Two cultural applications are **not** isomers as usually reported, because their ingredient lists differ; over a fixed atom set with only the bond to the ground varied, they are a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier (Q2). The work is ordered by dependency, and the chain most likely breaks between vocabulary and objective, which is where the field's own methodological criticism lands (Q3). The analogues corroborate the shape from chemistry, animal behaviour and metascience, but none supplies the specific technique (Q4). The specific technique remains a `gap`: Fig 9 :32, "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself".
