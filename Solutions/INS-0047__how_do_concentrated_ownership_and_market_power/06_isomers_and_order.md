# Union, order, isomers, and the ecology analogue

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The primary audit left two structural questions open. First, are competition rules and labor rules one question or a union, and if a union, in what order are the loops read? Second, what kind of bond is the concentration comparison, and are the two rule systems isomers or members of a functional group? A third question is whether a genuinely low-overlap frame predicts the same shape, which is what the ecology analogue is for.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation are role bindings, not new primitives: a state-like condition (concentrated ownership), a structure (a rule, contract, licensing regime, bargaining institution, or enforcement network), and a state-like outcome (wages, entry, prices). Bonds held are the seven plus negation: sequence `→`, production `⇒`, threshold comparison `⋛`, subtraction `-`, conjunction `+`, identity `≡`, disjunction `∥`, and refusal `¬`. No new primitive is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Union before partition

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the" (continued :56: "intersections standing, because the second condition still binds").
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."

Ownership as seller in the product market and as buyer in the labor market are two conditions that overlap inside the firm. A pure antitrust lever clears the product-market region and leaves the labor intersection standing. Three loops are owed, not two: competition rules, labor rules, and the compound.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** show that one lever clears both the product-market region and the labor intersection without a separate loop for the compound. If it does, the union reading fails.

### Q2. The comparison is a threshold, not a merge

- Fig 12 :87: "a threshold relation between two quantities"
- Operators table :51: "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"
- Operators table :49: "a threshold relation between two quantities"

A concentration comparison asks which side is larger, not whether both operands are present. It is `⋛`, not `+`. This matters because 'concentrated ownership plus market power' written with a conjunction would record only ingredients and lose the threshold that makes the question about power at all. Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- **Falsifier:** express the concentration question as a symmetric conjunction with no threshold and show that it still predicts which side dominates. If it does, the `⋛` reading is unnecessary.

### Q3. Isomers or a functional group

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

As the two loops are normally written, competition rules and labor rules do not share the same atoms: one is built over price and output, the other over wages and job access. By the figure's definition they are **not** isomers. They share a **functional group**, the `state-like condition / structure / state-like outcome` arrangement, with different atoms in the slots. The stronger form, worth testing, writes both rule systems over a shared atom set of ownership, rule structure, and market or labor outcome, differing only in which structure mediates which pair. In that form they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance.
- **Falsifier:** show that either rule system requires an atom the other cannot be written with, under the shared-set form. That kills the isomer claim and leaves the functional-group claim standing.

### Q4. Order by dependency

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step" (continued :21: "that cannot use it.").
- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"

The ownership condition is established first, then first-order outcomes in product and labor markets are read, then the second-order rule change is read, then feedback closes onto the ownership condition. A rule change is composable over a firm only if that firm has settled at first order. Fig 3 :17: "not reached, so not assessable". A reading taken before the first-order outcomes have arrived is a reading of nothing.
- **Falsifier:** show a rule change that can be settled and composed before the market outcomes it consumes have arrived. If it can, the dependency order is wrong.

### Q5. Low-overlap analogue: ideal free distribution

This is the one world frame in the packet that does not draw on the economics pool. In ecology, the ideal free distribution model (Fretwell and Lucas, 1969; Fretwell, 1972, as recorded in the source fetched at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_free_distribution) predicts that animals distribute among resource patches in proportion to the resources in each, but that dominant individuals can keep weaker ones from reaching the ideal patch and that unequal competitors change the match.

- The matching prediction is an instance of contact: actors go where the residual is smallest and the resource largest, which mirrors Fig 11 :40 "A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."
- The unequal-competitor result is an instance of relative valence. Fig 11 :49: "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries." A dominant competitor is not a fixed evil; its effect depends on which residual is tracked.
- The habitat is enumerated, not composed. Fig 11 :61: "The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not." A seat can add an actor to a habitat and cannot subtract the habitat.

The analogue corroborates the shape from a different evidence pool. It does not decide the verdict and adds no figure atom. Its agreement with the figures is a shadow, not a second witness for the institutional claim.
- **Falsifier:** show a low-overlap frame in which a dominant actor's valence is fixed across residuals, or in which contact can be removed rather than substituted. That would break the analogy, not the figure verdict.

### Open strain: operand type at second order

The Operators table and Fig 12 :87 define `⋛` as a threshold relation between two quantities. At second order the operands are settled rule systems now serving as atoms. Fig 12 :104 permits the demotion to atom, but whether an atom counts as a quantity at that level is a type question. It is named here and not smoothed over. Fig 13 :139 itself allows that a frame can say what something would have to look like and still not name it: "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself".

## CONCLUSION

The question is a union of three loops read in dependency order, not one hard problem and not a list of two. The comparison is a threshold, not a conjunction. Competition rules and labor rules share a functional group as normally written and are a real isomer claim under a shared atom set. The ecology analogue corroborates the contact and valence claims from a genuinely low-overlap pool. One type strain remains, the operand type of `⋛` at second order, recorded rather than hidden.
