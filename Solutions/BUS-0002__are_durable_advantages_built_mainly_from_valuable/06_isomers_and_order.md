# Are the four sources isomers? Order, structure and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading
(see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The primary audit concludes that the four candidates form a union of low-overlap frames and that
capability is a property of firm-and-habitat. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. Under Fig 12's definitions, are the four candidates **isomers**, a **functional group**, or
   unrelated frames?
2. What is the **dependency order** among them, and where does a chain of explanation break?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is
invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Applied strictly: the four candidates do **not** share the same atom set. A resource stock is not the
same atom as an institutional rule; a position is a relation to rivals, a tie is a relation to a
partner. They are not isomers under the figure's definition, because the first condition (same atoms)
fails.

Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says
what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

All four belong to one functional group: "a contributor to a slot's persistent residual whose valence
is relative to that residual" (Fig 11 :49). The group says what kind (a habitat actor in contact with
R); the qualifier says which member (resource, routine, position, tie, rule). This is the weaker and
correct reading.

The stronger isomer claim is available at a different level: for a **single firm**, the same bundle
of atoms (patents, people, capital, partners) can be arranged under different bonds and produce
different substances. A patent held outright (`+` with the firm) and a patent licensed under a
relation-specific contract (a bond to a partner) are the same atom under different bonds. Fig 12 :96:
**"Two entries can share every ingredient and be inverses of one another."** So isomerism is a claim
about **arrangements within a firm**, not about the four schools as wholes.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two firms with identical atom sets under different bonds
  show no systematic difference in the duration of their residual, the within-firm isomer claim fails
  and only the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 :20-21: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one
above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**

Working chain for BUS-0002:

```
1 field and structure conditions  ->  2 terms for what the situation affords
        ->  3 a stated objective (what counts as a persistent small residual)
                ->  4 planning / reconfiguration
                        ->  5 carry and verify, then the ground-facing return
```

Fig 3 :6-7 names link 2: *"it has terms for what things afford: what can be moved, opened, joined,
blocked"*. Fig 3 :22-23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor
can. Which is why a reading taken below the break is not a weak reading. It is a reading of nothing."*

Consequence for the four candidates: adding resources or capabilities (a lever at link 4 or 5) cannot
rescue a firm whose vocabulary for its habitat (link 2) is missing. This is why "mainly resources"
fails even in its own terms for some slots. Resource accumulation is effort above the break.

Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a firm with a demonstrably broken affordance vocabulary
  still achieves a long small residual purely by adding resource stock, the dependency order fails.

### Q3 - Catalysts, cycles and what each school quietly assumes

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave
differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

- A **reputation** or a **licensed brand** is catalyst-like: it survives its own use. Dyer and Singh's
  relation-specific assets and knowledge-sharing routines are catalyst-like too, because repeated use
  can regenerate them. The RBV's physical capital is mostly spent.
- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes,
  and a chain cannot close by construction."** Dynamic capabilities are a cycle: reconfiguration
  produces a new asset position, which becomes the input to the next reconfiguration (Teece, Pisano
  and Shuen, 1997). A stock-only resource list is a chain and cannot close by construction.
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome."** An industry
  with high entry barriers is a functional-group qualifier: it fixes what kind of advantage is
  available without naming which firm has it.

So the four schools each implicitly assume a different bond type. That is Fig 12 :5: *"A set of
ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was
recording."*

### Q4 - Two currencies, and why the debate does not settle

Fig 12 :125-126: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."** *"Bits price the
description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."*

- VRIN-style resource lists are **cheap in bits** (four adjectives) and **expensive in actions**
  (showing a resource is rare, inimitable and non-substitutable in a given slot over a given duration).
- A variance decomposition is **moderately cheap in bits** and **moderately expensive in actions**
  (a long panel of business-segment data), but it prices a different quantity.
- A claim about *durable* advantage is expensive in both: a long description of the contact
  conditions and a long observation window to settle them.

Fig 12 :127: *"They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them
and will not notice the shortfall."* The schools debate as if one currency would decide it. It will
not.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single measure in one currency ranks all four candidates
  consistently across sectors and time, the two-currency objection fails.

### Q5 - Negation and the refusal the question owes

Operators table :57-58: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without
it can only accumulate."*

The question "mainly X?" is an accumulation question. The derivation owes a refusal first: refuse the
premise that there is one main source, then mint the union shape. This is Formula step 2's REFUTED
bin - a term held is false, what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation. Binding the sought shape as
an ordinary term (Formula "Direction") is what makes that refusal possible.

## CONCLUSION

The four schools are **not isomers** over a shared atom set. They are members of one **functional
group** whose valence is relative to the residual (Fig 12 :124, Fig 11 :49). The genuine isomer claim
lives one level down, inside a single firm's arrangements of the same atoms under different bonds,
and has a stated falsifier.

The dependency order runs from field and structure conditions, through a vocabulary of affordances, to
reconfiguration and the ground-facing return. A lever applied above a broken link cannot rescue it
(Fig 3 :19-23). Each school also assumes a different bond type (cycle, catalyst, functional group,
stock), which is why their union has surplus and a single winner is not composable.
