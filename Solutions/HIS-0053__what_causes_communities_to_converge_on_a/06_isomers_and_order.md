# Union, order and the bond analogue for convergence with interpretive disagreement

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the order rule, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed after `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`:

1. Are the three convergence bonds a partition or a union, and what follows for a case that satisfies two of them at once?
2. In what order must the diagnostic run, and is the "same conclusion, different interpretation" pattern an isomer, a spelling under a symmetric bond, or neither?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for the diagnostic: a non-updating anchor, a shared evidence pool, influence ranking, a shared term C, and an interpretation relation. Bonds held: the three convergence relations from Fig 2, the semantic convention relation from Fig 10, and (as an analogue only) the symmetric bond from Fig 12. No new primitive is needed. The verdicts stay in D1 through D4 of the primary census.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Union or partition? UNION, with a specific residue

Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."** Fig 9 :55-56: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."**

The three Fig 2 panels can overlap. A community can share one evidence pool *and* update on each other's conclusions. Clearing the pool condition does not clear the case, because the influence condition still binds. Fig 9 :57-58: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."**

Consequence: there is no single "cause of consensus." Each case is a compound with its own loop, and the compound is named by which conditions are jointly present. This rescues the question from the either/or frame without inventing a fourth mechanism.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** show that the three conditions never co-occur in any documented case, and that all observed convergences fall cleanly into one panel. Then the union call is unnecessary and a partition would be simpler.

### Q2: What order does the diagnostic run? Dependency, not importance

Fig 3 :20-21: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** Fig 3 :22-23: **"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can. Which is why a reading taken below the break is not a weak reading. It is a reading of nothing."**

Applied here, the links are:

| link | question | what it consumes | what breaks it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 perception | what actually converged, and on which slot? | nothing | calling a whole field "the consensus" without naming C |
| 2 vocabulary | what terms do the frames have for C and its interpretations? | link 1 | treating an interpretation as a synonym rather than a term |
| 3 objective | what would count as a verdict here rather than a convention? | link 2 | using agreement itself as the criterion |
| 4 planning | which frames and which anchor will be ranged? | link 3 | booking a room of people who already agree |
| 5 learn and carry | what method survived, with provenance? | link 4 | carrying the conclusion instead of the diagnostic |

The break in this field is at link 3. Most published effort lands above it: counting agreements, measuring modularity, timing consensus. Those are measurements of link 1 and link 2 quantities. Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

**Falsifier:** exhibit a case where the objective was decided without any vocabulary for the interpretation, and the resulting verdict was later confirmed by an unmoved ground. That would show link 3 is not where the chain breaks in this field.

### Q3: Is "one conclusion, many interpretations" a symmetric-bond spelling?

Fig 12 :23: **"this bond is symmetric, so the four are one term"**; :24: **"and nothing consults the bond to find that out"**; :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**

The temptation: under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term. A shared conclusion C with many local interpretations looks like many spellings of one term, and the different interpretations look like operand orderings that "nothing consults."

This is an **analogue, not a derivation**, and it is marked as strained:

- Fig 12's operands and bonds are terms inside a library Γ. A community's interpretations are not framework atoms and cannot be enumerated as `k` known operands.
- The social "bond" between a frame and C is not one of the seven typed bonds in the Operators table. Calling it symmetric smuggles a type decision.
- The analogy is still useful as a *test*: if interpretations are genuinely permutations of the same held atoms, then "same conclusion" is one term and disagreement is spelling noise. If they are different compositions, they are different terms.

**Falsifier:** show that two interpretations of the same conclusion cannot be mapped to each other by any permutation of a shared operand set, that is, one requires an atom the other does not hold. Then the symmetric-bond analogue fails and the isomer reading below takes over. Status: `single-source` and strained. It does not appear in any verdict in `01` or `07`.

### Q4: Are two communities holding C isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** Fig 8 :50-52: **"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them, and the surplus lives only where the closures differ."**

Two communities that converge on the same C can be related in three ways:

1. **Same atoms, different bond, different substance (isomer).** Both hold the shared evidence and the anchor, but one ranges against the anchor and the other updates on influence. The contents (C) match; the behaviour under a new datum differs sharply. This is a real isomer claim.
2. **Functional group.** Both are `frame(s) -> bond -> C` arrangements, but with different atoms in the slots. They share a functional group, not an atom set, so they are not isomers under Fig 12's definition. This is the weaker and safer reading.
3. **Same frame wearing two names.** Both draw on the same pool and the same influence ranking. Then there is one substance with two labels, and the surplus is zero (Fig 8 :47-48). "Two communities converged" is an artefact of counting names.

The discriminator is provenance, not contents. Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."** A settled conclusion can serve as an atom at the next level; the level at which it became settled decides what is irreducible, not the conclusion itself.

**Falsifier:** find two communities holding the same C whose behaviour under an identical new datum is indistinguishable *and* whose provenance records show different bonds. That would break the isomer claim's "decisive in the behaviour" condition and leave only the functional-group reading standing.

### Q5: Which of the three questions in step 7 applies?

Formula step 7 asks SEARCHED, INWARD, OUTWARD, in that order, and keeps them distinct:

- **SEARCHED:** is the answer already in this community's closure and merely unfound? Fig 8 :39: **"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."** Spending budget on a region already swept is a filter failure, not a reason to import a new theory of consensus.
- **INWARD:** sharpen the instrument. Fig 6 :32: **"An instrument is not built from a description. It is improved from a worse instrument already returning something."** Shwed and Bearman's modularity is such an instrument, improved from black-boxing and community detection.
- **OUTWARD:** find a frame whose closure already predicts the shape. This is how Star and Griesemer's boundary object and Galison's trading zone entered the derivation: the residual was described first (Fig 9 :27-28), then those frames were searched. They are imports with a source gate and a shadow test, debited against independence.

The three are opposed in the sense that INWARD and OUTWARD are not to be read against each other (formula step 7). Buying a better consensus score does not replace finding a frame that predicts interpretive disagreement, and finding such a frame does not improve the score.

## CONCLUSION

The three convergence conditions are a **union** with a specific compound residue, not a partition. The diagnostic runs in dependency order, perception before vocabulary before objective, and the chain breaks at the question "what would count as a verdict here?" The "one conclusion, many interpretations" pattern is a **strained symmetric-bond analogue** at best, and a genuine **isomer claim** only when two communities hold the same atoms under different bonds and behave differently under a new datum. The safer structural statement is that they share a functional group. Every claim here is `specified` or `general`, none is `grounded`, and all three falsifiers were written before the comparison.
