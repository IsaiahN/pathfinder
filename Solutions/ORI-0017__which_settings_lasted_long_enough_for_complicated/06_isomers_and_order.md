## Q1 — Are two accumulating settings isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Write a hot spring as `{contact: seasonal, concentration: wet-dry, gradient: thermal, cycling: diurnal}` and a closed basin lake as `{contact: perennial, concentration: evaporative/closed-basin, gradient: pH/redox, cycling: seasonal}`. Both use the same four condition atoms. They differ in which bond (which cycle, which gradient source) holds between them. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, not variants of one setting. The difference is invisible in the ingredient list and decisive in the behaviour: one concentrates by drying, the other by closed-basin retention.

### Falsifier (written before the run)

If either setting can be written without an atom the other requires (for example, a lake that accumulates with no gradient, or a spring with no re-entrant cycle), the isomer claim fails and the weaker **functional-group** reading stands instead. This is the same falsifier structure as F1 of `01`.

**Result:** the isomer claim is `specified` and unmeasured. The falsifier has not been run.

## Q2 — Do the settings share a functional group?

Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

The four-condition set is a functional group. Its presence fixes the *kind* of outcome (accumulation is possible); the qualifiers (which gradient, which cycle, which duration) fix *which member* (vent, pond, ice, spring, crust). This is the honest weaker claim when the isomer falsifier cannot be run: settings are members of one functional group, and the question "which setting" asks which qualifier pays for the outcome.

World corroboration: the Abiogenesis page enumerates deep-sea vents, hot springs, temperate surface waters, icy surface waters, and the continental crust as distinct settings, each with a different qualifier. That is an enumeration of the functional group, not a ranking (Fig 11 :43).

## Q3 — Which part is a catalyst, and which is a spent part?

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

A mineral surface or a pore that survives many wet/dry cycles is catalyst-like: it is not consumed by the condensation it hosts. A deliquescent salt that is washed away by an uncontrolled rain event is a spent part. Campbell et al. 2019 give the measured contrast: regulated rehydration by deliquescence (catalyst-like, regenerating) gave a glycine oligomer yield of 16.0 percent; simulated heavy rain (spent, destructive) gave 1.9 percent. The figures' own warning applies: nothing in the recipe records which a term is. The world source supplies the record.

This is a genuine point of contact between Fig 12 :119 and the lab literature. It is `specified`, triangulated (Fig 12 :119 plus a measured lab contrast).

## Q5 — Does a settled result cross to the next level?

Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."** Once a setting is ground-settled (a rock, a date, a concentration assay pays for it), it becomes an atom for the next question, for example the transition from accumulation to replication. Until then it is a candidate, not an operand. This is why the list is a gap now and not a permanent silence: a settlement would put a new atom in the library without moving the wall (Fig 6 :21).

## Ordering caveat: production is not sequence

The Operators table distinguishes `→` (sequence, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*) from `⇒` (production, *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, test: *"is there a B before A fires? No."*). Accumulation is `⇒`: polymers did not exist before the cycling produced them. A wet/dry cycle is a loop (Fig 12 :121), not a straight sequence. Writing the four conditions as a flat chain would record the ingredients and lose the arrangement (Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information"*). Kept as a live notation constraint, not hidden.

## CONCLUSION

Over the fixed four-condition atom set, accumulating settings are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117) with a stated falsifier; at minimum they are members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :123). Mineral surfaces and pores are **catalysts** where they survive their use, **spent parts** where they do not (Fig 12 :119), and the lab literature supplies the record the notation does not. The chain breaks at the objective link (Fig 3), which is why the question cannot yet yield a ranked list. All claims are `specified`, none are `grounded`, and the isomer falsifier has not been run.
