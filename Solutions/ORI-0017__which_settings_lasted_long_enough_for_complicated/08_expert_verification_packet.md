## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

The question "which settings lasted long enough for complicated chemistry to build up" is a union of four conditions, not a contest between named settings. A qualifying setting must keep contact with its chemistry across enough time (Fig 13 :12, Fig 11 :40), concentrate building blocks faster than dilution removes them (Fig 6 :21), supply a spendable gradient (Fig 13 :22), and run a re-entrant cycle with the right sign (Fig 12 :121). Vents, ponds, ice, hot springs, and crustal pores are different arrangements of the same four conditions, so they stand as isomers (Fig 12 :117) or, at minimum, members of one functional group (Fig 12 :123). The framework returns that shape and its refuters; it does not return the list, because which setting actually qualified is owed to rock and experiment. ### C2 — geology-facing; checkable against the setting literature

- **Statement:** concentration and persistence are separate necessary conditions, and the setting literature treats them separately. A vent supplies gradient and persistence but dilutes; a closed basin or wet-dry pond concentrates and cycles but has uncertain persistence.
- **What to check:** the Abiogenesis page states *"Vents do not concentrate prebiotic materials, due to strong dilution by seawater. This open system cycles compounds through vent minerals, leaving little residence time to accumulate."* The Last Chance Lake page states *"It is a closed-basin lake, which allows it to maintain high salinity and carbonate alkalinity."* Campbell et al. 2019 report *"an average total yield of glycine oligomers of 16.0%"* under regulated deliquescent cycling versus *"1.9%"* under added water. Are those characterizations faithful to the underlying literature? Is the vent concentration claim still current?
- **Refuter:** demonstrate multi-step accumulation in a well-mixed open setting where dilution exceeds input, or show that a single lever predicts which settings accumulate (which would collapse the union).

### C3 — chemistry-facing; checkable against cycling and energy experiments

- **Statement:** accumulation requires a spendable gradient and a cycle whose output re-enters with the right sign; one-pass flow cannot close, and the sign can reverse the outcome.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 13 :22 *"a difference that can be spent."* Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Operators table :39 (production) *"is there a B before A fires? No."*
- **What to check:** in the Campbell et al. data, added water reduced yield by favoring hydrolysis; in Deamer & Weber 2010, repeated hydrated-to-dry cycling *"can lead to the accumulation of more complex products."* Does the sign-of-loop claim hold across other chemistries, or is the hydrolysis/condensation reversal specific to glycine oligomerization?
- **Refuter:** show multi-step accumulation in a setting with no spendable gradient, or accumulation compounding through a single pass with no output re-entry.
