## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are the before and after configurations isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :118-119: under a symmetric bond, *"this bond is symmetric, so the four are one term"*; under an ordered bond, *"A then B"* is a different thing.
- Read the two configurations over the same atom set `{b, a, o′, R, φ, Γ, ground}`:
  - **Before:** observation and explanation are joined by agreement in the same closure. The figure for this is Fig 2 :13-15, *"no anchor; each updates on the others"*, or Fig 2 :18-21, *"one evidence pool."* The effective bond is `≡` identity (two names for one reading) or `+` conjunction (both held at once), read symmetrically.
  - **After:** the chain is `a → o′ → R → φ`, each stage ordered, with Γ ranged separately against a non-updating ground (Fig 2 :45-46).
- Same atoms, different bond, different substance. Both are **isomers** in Fig 12's sense. This is why the question is not "did experiments add a new faculty" but "how did the arrangement change."
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the before configuration has no action atom at all, or the after configuration requires a new primitive not in the library, the isomer claim fails and the relation is composition plus import (Fig 6 :23). The claim would then be a different one: an atom was added, not a bond changed.
- **Triangulated** for the definition (Fig 12 :117 and the Fig 2 panels are low-overlap routes). The historical instantiation is `single-source` and unmeasured; that is F8's gap.

### Q2. What is the order, and does it carry part of the claim?

- Fig 12 :70: **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"** is the `→` sequence bond; the test is *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*
- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Ordered chain for this question: anchor → instrument returning something → intervention → observation → per-slot residual → term minted and settled → method carried up.
- Fig 6 :32: **"An instrument is not built from a description. It is improved from a worse instrument already returning something."** So the instrument step cannot be moved after the description of the result.
- Formula step 2: refuse before mint. An explanation that should be refused is not repaired by a new term. This is the one direction the loop cannot check on its own: formula "Direction" says *"a frame cannot check the direction of its own question."* Writing the order down as an ordinary term, as here, makes it refusable.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a working tradition in which the explanation is settled before the reading is produced, and the reading is still used as a verdict against that explanation. That breaks the `→` order and reduces the triangle back to a symmetric bond.
- **Triangulated** (Fig 12 and Fig 3, different figures and different quantities).

### Q3. A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot name a route

- Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**
- Fig 12 :112-113: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."**
- A list of the three terms, observation, intervention, explanation, is an ingredient list. It cannot say which came first, which is paid in which currency, or which one a frame may not reconstruct. The bond carries that information and is *"the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording"* (Fig 12 :5).
- This is the reason F2 and F5 are not optional embellishments. Without the ordered bond and the verifier restriction, the three-term list is the flat formula that Fig 12 rejects.
- **Falsifier:** show a description of the three terms in which the bond information is recoverable from the operands alone. Then Fig 12 :5 does not govern here.

### Q4. The four structures as they appear here

Fig 12 :116-124 names four structures a compositional system exhibits. Each has a reading in this question:

| structure | verbatim line | reading here |
|---|---|---|
| isomer | Fig 12 :117 *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance."* | before and after configurations (Q1) |
| catalyst | Fig 12 :119 *"A part that survives its own use."* | an instrument or protocol survives a run and pays again in the next; a single result does not |
| cycle | Fig 12 :121 *"The output re-enters as input."* | the residual from one run sharpens the next instrument (Fig 6 :33); the sign of the loop decides whether it closes |
| functional group | Fig 12 :123 *"A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* | `act → read → revise` is the group common to all experimental domains; the qualifier names the domain (optics, chemistry, medicine) |

The functional-group reading matters because it blocks a category error. The shape is the group, not a standing list of instruments or a named discipline. Fig 11 :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*

### Q5. The two currencies, again

- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- Fig 12 :128: **"The two readings come apart in both directions. A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute."**
- An explanation can be cheap to state and expensive to settle. A protocol can be long to describe and cheap to refute. Because the currencies do not add, a history of experimental method cannot be told as a single story of "more and better observation." The two ledgers must be kept apart or the budget silently decides the verdict (Fig 9 :60).
- **Falsifier:** a domain where both prices are paid in the same unit and experimenters actually compare them. Then the two-currency claim is false there.
