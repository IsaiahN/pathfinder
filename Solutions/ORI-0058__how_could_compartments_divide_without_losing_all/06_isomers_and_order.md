# Structure, order and isomers of the division event

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The audit established the shape (a lossy cross-scale handoff carrying a seed). Two supporting questions remain. (1) What is the **order** of the division event: a mere sequence, a production, or a closed cycle? (2) Are the observed division modes (shear fission, budding, extrusion) **isomers** over a shared atom set, or merely different members of a functional group?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the world frame: membrane material, aqueous contents, a genetic record, a catalyst or replicase, external energy or shear. Bonds available: the seven in Fig 12 plus negation. No new primitive is assumed. Currency: viable daughters per parent. Anchor: the retention and evolution assays in the opened papers.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: sequence, production, or cycle?

Fig 12 :71 defines sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*. Fig 12 :77 defines production: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*. Fig 12 :121 defines the cycle: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*

A division described as "grow, then split, then grow again" is a sequence, which is a chain, and the figure says a chain cannot close by construction. The correct structure is production closed into a cycle: the parent **produces** daughters that did not exist before, and the daughters re-enter as the parents of the next round. This is why the coupling of growth and division is structural, not a scheduling detail. Szostak 2011 (opened) supplies the world-side instance: division is coupled to growth because filaments divide and spheres do not.

**Falsifier (Q1):** a system that undergoes repeated division where daughters never serve as parents for a further division. That breaks Fig 12 :121 and :104, and the production reading is withdrawn.

### Q2: why symmetric halving is the wrong bond

Fig 12 :92–93: *"Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term"*. A symmetric split treats the two daughters as interchangeable spellings of one term. That is correct for membrane area, which can be roughly halved, and wrong for a single genome, which cannot be split into two functional copies by the same operation.

The information partition therefore requires either duplication before the split or a high-variance stochastic allocation in which some daughters receive copies and others do not. The world side supports the second: Chen and Walde 2010 (opened) describe the stochastic corrector, where *"An occasional parasitic sequence would be separated from most of the active polymerases during vesicle division and could not poison the entire system"*. The asymmetry is not noise to be engineered away at the origin; it is the condition for selection between daughters.

**Falsifier (Q2):** a demonstrated case where a single genome copy is split into two functional daughter genomes by the physical division itself, with no prior duplication and no stochastic allocation. That breaks the symmetric-bond analysis above.

### Q3: are the division modes isomers?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Consider three modes over the same atom set {membrane material, contents, genome, external energy}:

| mode | what is held | arrangement (bond) | behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| shear fission of filaments | contents retained, RNA redistributed | growth into filament, then fragile crossing under mild shear | many viable daughters, trace leakage (Zhu and Szostak 2009) |
| chemically fuelled budding | contents retained, shrinking parent | partial conversion to oil, hydrolysis, budding | heterogeneous daughters, external fuel required (Zambrano et al. 2024) |
| extrusion through pores | contents partly lost | forced size reduction | about 40 percent RNA leaked (Zhu and Szostak 2009) |

They share the atom set and differ in arrangement, and the behaviour differs decisively (retention versus loss). By Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**, not variants of one thing. This matters because the question "how could compartments divide without losing all their contents" is partly a question about which arrangement, not which ingredients. Ingredient-only accounts cannot tell the three apart, which is exactly the flat-formula failure Fig 12 names.

**Falsifier (Q3):** show that two modes with the same atom set and different mechanisms produce indistinguishable retention and heredity distributions. The isomer claim then collapses into a functional-group claim.

### Q4: the functional group underneath

Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

The minimal substructure that fixes the outcome is {a boundary, a record that survives its own use, an energy or shear source}. The group says *what kind*: a division-capable compartment. The qualifier says *which member*: fatty acid vesicle, coacervate, emulsion droplet, mineral pore. The group is figure-derived; the member is a provenanced import from the world frame. This is the precise sense in which the framework returns a shape, not a name.

### Q5: order of derivation versus order of explanation

Fig 13 :88–89: *"The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no two passes begin from the same place."* Each division changes what the next division composes against: daughters are smaller, contents redistributed, the habitat altered. So the order of explanation cannot be a single template applied identically; it is a chain whose turns begin from different remainders. This is the structural reason a "one mechanism fits all generations" account is incomplete.

## CONCLUSION

The division event is production closed into a cycle, not a sequence. Its information partition is asymmetric: a symmetric bond is right for membrane area and wrong for a single genome. The candidate physical modes are isomers over a shared atom set, with retention as the decisive behavioural difference. Underneath them sits a functional group (boundary, surviving record, energy source) whose qualifier is the specific chemistry, which the figures do not name.

This rescues what is checkable in the field's observations without pretending the framework selected a carrier. `metric tensor = Gradient`-style over-bindings are replaced by: *a record that survives its own use* and *a cross-kind arrangement whose isomers differ in retention*.
