# Isomers, functional groups and order in the replace/modify question

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed after `01`: (1) if standard expected utility and prospect theory are written over a shared set of atoms, are they *isomers* under Fig 12, or merely members of a functional group; and (2) what is the dependency order of a correct answer, so that effort is not spent above a broken link (Fig 3 :19)?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in D1 and D3: outcomes, probabilities or weights, a value or utility function, a time index, a reference point (status disputed: held or imported), and a computational-cost term (status disputed: held or imported). Bonds available: the seven, including `+`, `→`, `⋛`, `−`. No further primitive is licensed without import. Actors, currency and anchor are as in `01` §2. Question type: union, with the isomer claim applying only to D3 and the order claim applying across D1 to D4.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are the standard model and its behavioral replacement isomers?

Fig 12 :117:

> "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :94-96: "The arrangement is the substance. Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

Camerer 1999 supplies the world-side statement: a good behavioral principle should include the rational principle as a mathematical special case. Expected utility `Σ p_i u(x_i)` and prospect theory `Σ π(p_i) u(x_i − r)` share outcomes, weights and a value function, and differ in the bond (reference dependence and nonlinear weighting). Over that fixed atom set they are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different behavioural substance. This is a structural claim, not a claim that either model is true.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either model requires an atom the other does not (for example a reference point with no counterpart in the standard frame, or a computational-cost term that is not a preference), the isomer claim fails and the relation becomes composition plus import. This is exactly the live type question in `01` F4. `01` §8 strain 5 keeps it visible.

### Q2: If not isomers, what binds them?

Fig 12 :123:

> "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Both models are members of the functional group "constrained optimisation over a value function under a belief". The qualifier (expected utility, probability weighting, hyperbolic discounting, social preference) says which member. Fig 12 :124 confirms. The practical consequence: a debate conducted at the level of the functional group ("does optimisation still describe choice?") cannot settle a debate at the level of the member ("which value function and which weights?"). A single lever aimed at the group leaves the member differences standing (Fig 9 :55-56). This is the structural reason a global "replace" or "modify" answer cannot be right.

### Q3: What is the order of the answer?

Dependency order from Fig 3 and the formula:

1. **Fact first.** Fig 9 :4-5: "Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."
2. **Describe before looking.** Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit." A choice deviation must be described in effect terms (manipulation, anchor, replication) before prospect theory or bounded rationality is named as its source.
3. **Refuse before mint.** Formula step 2: REFUTED means "a term held is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation". If an axiom is false, a new complicated model composed over it inherits the error.
4. **Reachability, then novelty.** Fig 5 :22-24: "can we even build this term? it composes from what we already have" and "is it actually new? it is not already in the library". A "new" behavioral model that is a re-arrangement of held atoms is not a new primitive (Fig 6 :22).
5. **Settle before composing.** Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." A model that has not paid against the ground cannot serve as an atom for a welfare or policy conclusion. Fig 5 :69-72 says settling is necessary and not sufficient; a library that grows is not a reach that grows.
6. **Ground decides, not agreement.** Fig 2 :45: "Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."

The broken link in the current debate is step 3 to step 4. The field has terms for deviations and instruments for effects, but no shared term for "which held term is false and what would refuse it" (Fig 3 :25-26: "The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments." Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."). Effort spent building ever-richer behavioral models (above the break) cannot rescue a missing refusal criterion (below it), per Fig 3 :19.

### Q4: What does the recursion do at the next level?

Fig 12 :103-104: "Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible. A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."

A behavioral model, once settled against held-out data, becomes an atom for a welfare or policy composition in D4. It does not thereby become a final frame: Fig 7 :6 names "the limit: no last room", and Fig 13 :116-118 says a frame can "search without end and still not arrive: infinite effort inside a finite reach". So a successful modification is not a terminal theory, and a successful replacement is not either. The debate's "end of rational choice" framing is the ToE error in another vocabulary.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed atom set, the standard model and its behavioral replacements are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117) sharing a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123). The isomer claim is conditional on the reference point and any computational-cost term being re-arrangements rather than imports, and its falsifier is stated above. The correct order is: split, describe, refuse, check reach and novelty, settle, then let the ground decide. The link most likely broken is the refusal criterion, not the modelling machinery. This framing rescues what is defensible in both camps without choosing a winner: type and order are the variables, not loyalty to a model.
