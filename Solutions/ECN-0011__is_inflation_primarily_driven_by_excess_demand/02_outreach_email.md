# Outreach draft: check the shape before you decide which inflation story to believe

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover note attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the pre-registered refuter in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 4 has been re-read and the sender is willing to have it fired. If the shape claim does not survive, there is nothing else to offer.

---

**Subject:** One checkable claim about "what primarily drives inflation"

> Hello,
>
> I have one specific claim about how the question "is inflation primarily driven by demand, supply, money, expectations, or a combination" is framed. I am not asking you to endorse a school of thought, and I am not asking you to read a theory first. If the claim fails, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`general`, structural, not a measurement of any country):**
>
> The word "primarily" asks for one number over a vector. That operation loses the signal. The residual a researcher actually needs is indexed by price sub-index, by country and by episode. Headline averages hide which slot is live. You can see this in practice in the Federal Reserve's own guidance, which tells policymakers to examine the subcategories of a broad index to decide whether a rise is temporary or unique, rather than reading the headline alone.
>
> Second, the four listed candidates are not four rival boxes. They overlap. A demand impulse and a supply bottleneck can be present in the same quarter; expectations can amplify either; fiscal and monetary stimulus can hit a constrained supply chain. The question is a union of conditions, not a list of alternatives. So "some combination" is not an answer. It is a restatement of the problem.
>
> Third, expectations are not a fourth linear driver. They are a loop: expected prices enter wage and price decisions, which produce the prices that confirm or disconfirm the expectation. Whether that loop amplifies or damps is an empirical question about the sign of the feedback.
>
> **What the claim does not say:** it does not say demand is unimportant, that money never matters, that supply shocks are imaginary, or that expectations are merely psychological. It says none of these can be ranked from an aggregate, and the figures from which the claim is derived do not name a dominant driver at all. Where the ground has not settled a name, the honest report is a gap, not a guess.
>
> **What would refute it, written before this message:**
> 1. Show that one aggregate driver predicts every price sub-index residual as well as a per-slot decomposition, against an anchor that does not change when a school of thought changes its mind.
> 2. Show that the four conditions are mutually exclusive, so a single lever clears every overlap.
> 3. Show that expectations feedback has negative sign at the measured scale, so the loop closes and cannot amplify a shock.
> 4. Show that causal attribution is invariant to the choice of price index, window and subcategory.
>
> If any of these holds, I will withdraw the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the public sources. The figures, not my summary of them, are the authority.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Canonical usage rule (local copy): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`
> - Federal Reserve, "What is inflation, and how does the Federal Reserve evaluate changes in the rate of inflation?": https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14419.htm
>
> **If you want to reuse the method:** the loop is in the local `THE_FORMULA.md`. Write down what would refute your own result *before* you run it, and state whether your output is `general`, `specified` or `grounded`. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> Thank you for your time.
