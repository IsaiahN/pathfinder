## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the two cores first-order?

A first-order molecule crosses once: `state - structure - state`. Geometry's core reads `Space - structure - Time` (something between space and time, Fig 13 :75: *"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."*). Entanglement's core, as a relation over degrees of freedom, reads `Energy - structure - Time` when written in the same vocabulary (a spectrum-like ordering over energy, with a structure connecting). Both are legal at first order.

Fig 13 itself licenses more than one molecule of this kind: *"A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."* So two distinct first-order molecules are permitted by the figure without a new primitive.

Mode: `specified` for the binding of each physics core to a first-order form; `general` for the legality. Verdict: triangulated (Fig 13 :75 and the gradient-over-space/time line).

### Q2 - Are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Written as the audit currently writes them, D1's core uses Network and D2's uses Space, so their atom sets are not identical. Under Fig 12's strict definition they are **not isomers**; they are two members of the same **functional group**, `state - structure - state`, differing in which atoms fill the slots.

The stronger form: if both are written over the full shared atom set `{Space, Time, Energy, Gradient, Network, Spectrum}`, differing only in which structure mediates which state pair, then they **are** isomers. Same six atoms, different arrangement, different substance. This is the claim worth testing, because it is checkable and it needs no new primitive.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either frame can be written without an atom the other requires (for example, if entanglement needs Network while geometry can be written with no Network, or geometry needs Space while entanglement needs no Space), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. That is a real outcome, not a failure of the exercise.

### Q3 - What order is the bridge?

Fig 12 :103: *"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."* Fig 12 :104:

> **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"**

Both first-order cores are settled against their own grounds (decades of experiment on entanglement and on spacetime geometry). Each can therefore serve as an atom at the next level. The bridge is a **second-order composition**: `(settled entanglement molecule) BOND (settled geometry molecule)`. This recursion is exactly what Fig 12 permits; it is not an import and it creates no seventh member.

Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."* The entropy-to-area route and the wormhole route can arrive at one composed substance without being the same primitive. This is the level-relativity result of audit F7, restated in composition terms.

**Type caveat, still open.** The Operators table defines `⋛` (comparison) as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*. At second order the operands are settled molecules now serving as atoms. Whether an atom counts as a *quantity* at that level is not decidable from the figure; it needs an instrument, not an argument. Recorded as a strain, not hidden.

Mode: `general` for the recursion; `specified` for the second-order reading of these two frames. Verdict: triangulated for the recursion (Fig 12 :103-104, Fig 13 :121); strained-for-now on the operand type.

### Q4 - Does order permit identity?

No. Over an ordered bond, different orders are different terms (Operators table: *"Under an ordered bond, different orders are different terms"*). Even if the two cores are isomers over the full atom set, their difference is carried by the bond and the arrangement (Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*). A literal identity `entanglement ≡ geometry` would require `≡` (identity, *"two names, one referent"*) to hold between two things whose difference is the whole point of the isomer claim. Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*

So order gives a composition and an isomer test, not an identity. Identity is refused at the level where the two are distinguishable, which is the only level we can check.

Mode: `specified`. Verdict: single-source for the bond definitions (Fig 12 :94-96 with the Operators table), same framework pool.

### Q5 - Analogues from other fields (secondary, not decidable)

- **Swingle 2009** (condensed matter) organizes quantum-state information by scale and *"defin[es] a higher dimensional geometry from this structure"*. This is a constructive analogue of Q3: a derived geometry from state structure. It corroborates the **shape** (geometry as a composition residue) and says nothing about literal identity.
- **Krioukov et al. 2010** (network science) finds that *"if a network has some metric structure, and if the network degree distribution is heterogeneous, then the network has an effective hyperbolic geometry underneath."* This is genuinely low overlap with the framework and is the strongest external witness that network structure and effective geometry can be related without being the same thing. It corroborates F2 and F1.
- **Falsifier for the analogue:** if either work were shown to require a new geometric primitive rather than deriving an effective one, the composition shape would lose a witness. It would not refute the figure verdict, which stands on Fig 13 alone.

## CONCLUSION

The two cores are first-order molecules of the six-term set. As the audit writes them they share a **functional group**, not an isomer relation; over the full shared atom set they are a genuine **isomer** claim with a stated falsifier. Their bridge is second-order, licensed by the settled-molecule rule (Fig 12 :104), and produces an isomer distinction rather than an identity. Geometry as a residue and entanglement as a network relation are two distinct atoms whose arrangement, not their ingredients, carries the substance (Fig 12 :94). The operand type of the comparison bond at second order remains a live strain. Everything here is `specified` or `general`; nothing is `grounded`.
