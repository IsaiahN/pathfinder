## RESIDUAL

The question asks whether dark matter is cold, warm or a mixture. Three sub-questions are owed about the *structure* of those options:

1. Are cold and warm different kinds, or different arrangements over the same atoms?
2. Is a mixture a third same-kind option, or a union of two conditions that must be unbundled?
3. What is the order of the discriminator, and which bond writes it?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are cold and warm isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 13 :20 "ordering, so more and less can be said." Fig 13 :22 "a difference that can be spent."
- Cold and warm are not different atom sets. Both are composed over Energy (state) bonded to Spectrum (ordering of perturbations) producing a Gradient (directed suppression) over Space and Time. What differs is the **scale** of the gradient, i.e. which region of the ordering is suppressed, and **which structure mediates** the pair.
- Over the full shared atom set the two are therefore isomers in Fig 12's sense: same atoms, different arrangement, different observable behaviour. As the question phrases them ("cold" versus "warm") they share the whole six-term set but differ in bond scale, so the isomer claim is stronger than the exemplar's GR/QM case, where only Time was shared.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either option requires an atom the other does not, or a seventh primitive, the isomer claim fails and they must be re-censused as different kinds. A warmth that required a new state beyond Energy would do it.

### Q2 — Is a mixture a third option?

- Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55-56 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."
- Fig 9 :57-58 "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."
- A mixture is U1 (a suppression exists) and U2 (its source) both binding. It is not a third same-kind label on the ordering. The residue after unbundling is smaller and categorically different: a two-component dark sector with a warm fraction and a cold fraction, whose residuals are the sum of two condition-specific residuals plus whatever new interaction the overlap creates.
- Operators table :19 "either suffices; not both required" describes ∥ (disjunction), and the table warns that this is not concurrency. A mixture is not ∥ in the naive sense; both components are present, so the correct reading is the union compound of Fig 9 rather than a single bond. The warning prevents a cheap ∥ spelling.
- **Falsifier:** if a mixed model's residual is exactly the sum of the cold and warm residuals with no interaction term, the "specific compound" claim is too strong and plain union (Fig 8) is the correct reading. That would simplify, not refute, the need to unbundle.

### Q3 — What is the order of the discriminator, and which bond writes it?

- Spectrum is "ordering, so more and less can be said" (Fig 13 :20). The matter power spectrum is the ordered thing.
- Gradient is "a difference that can be spent" (Fig 13 :22). Free-streaming spends the difference in power on small scales.
- Comparison ⋛ is "a threshold relation between two quantities" (Operators table :49). The discriminator is a threshold on the ordering: which side of `k_fs` does a given scale fall on?
- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." The `state → structure → state` form is the functional group shared by all three options; the cutoff scale is the qualifier that says which member.
- **Falsifier:** if the cutoff is not a threshold on an ordering but an unstructured feature (no `k` ordering, no redshift scaling), the comparison bond is mistyped and the discriminator must be re-censused.

### Q4 — What is the dependency order of the probes?

- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
- Chain: perceive a cutoff (Lyman-alpha at z greater than 4) → name the ordering (spectrum/gradient vocabulary) → state the objective (redshift-scaling discrimination) → plan the probe (21 cm at z greater than 15) → carry the transfer functions forward.
- The high-redshift link currently stops at the objective because the needed precision is not yet delivered. It is not a working link and cannot be read as evidence of absence. Fig 8 :39 "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."
- **Falsifier:** an instrument that delivers percent-level `P(k)` at z greater than 15 and finds no scaling difference would break the objective and force a re-census of the whole chain.

### Q5 — Analogue check: what does the isomorphism *not* carry?

- Fig 12 :97-98 "This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation." The figure uses chemistry as the *source* of the isomer concept, not as a loose metaphor.
- The analogue transfers: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. It does **not** transfer the specific masses, bonds or circuit details of chemistry into dark matter. Fig 6 :30 "The description travels. Fails by being sharpened into terms only we can read. Cast it in effect terms."
- The statistics analogue (MacKay 2003, read this session) transfers the idea that a complex model must pay for its parameters: "Simple models tend to make precise predictions. Complex models, by their nature, are capable of making a greater variety of predictions." It does not transfer Bayes factors as a substitute for the ground. A mixed model with more parameters can be penalised by an evidence calculation, but only a measurement settles which model nature chose.

## CONCLUSION

Cold and warm are **first-order arrangements** over the six-term set, differing in the scale of a gradient over a spectrum, so they are isomers in Fig 12's sense. A mixture is **not a third option of that kind**: it is a union compound (Fig 9) whose conditions must be unbundled and whose residue is a specific compound with its own loop. The discriminator is a comparison ⋛ written on the spectral ordering at a threshold, and the chain of probes is ordered by dependency, with the high-redshift link currently unbuilt rather than negative. Falsifiers for all four claims were written before the run and are listed above.
