**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The global question offers two mechanisms as alternatives. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) are the two mechanisms **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or something weaker, and (2) what bond structure and dependency order does a checkable answer require?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (carried from `01`, not repeated in full)

Domain 1, price discovery: actors dispersed investors, arbitrageurs, issuing firms; currency capital and compressed information; anchor real productive outcome. Domain 2, speculation and short horizons: actors speculators, managers, lenders; currency near-term price change and reputation; anchor the same real outcome plus observable horizon mismatch. Domain 3, verification: researchers and reviewers; currency evidence; anchor out-of-sample real outcomes. Union, not partition. Dependency order: perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, carry.

## Q1. Are the two mechanisms isomers?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

Name the atoms available in the finance frame: **capital, dispersed information, second-order belief, price, horizon, incentive, real productive use**.

- Mechanism A (signal): `dispersed information -> price -> capital allocation`. Its distinguishing atom is dispersed information. In the opened world source, Hayek's arbitrageur acts on "special knowledge of circumstances of the fleeting moment".
- Mechanism B (distortion): `second-order belief -> price -> capital allocation`. Its distinguishing atom is a belief about what average opinion will believe, which Keynes describes as "anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be".

The two mechanisms share the substructure `signal -> price -> allocation` and differ in what fills the signal slot. They do **not** share the full atom set: A needs dispersed information, B needs second-order belief. By Fig 12 :117 they are therefore **not isomers**. By Fig 12 :124 they are two members of the same **functional group**, where the group is `signal -> price -> allocation` and the qualifier is which signal occupies the first slot.

This matters for the verdict. An isomer claim would let one theory be derived from the other by rebonding the same atoms, which would be a much stronger and cheaper result. The functional-group reading says the two mechanisms must be checked separately against the ground, which is exactly the per-slot verdict F1 in `01`.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if both mechanisms can be written over an identical atom set and differ only in the bond between those atoms, the isomer claim holds and this finding is retracted. If either mechanism can be written without the price atom at all, then the shared functional group fails and the two mechanisms are unrelated kinds.

## Q2. What bonds does a checkable answer need?

Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."*

Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*

A list of states (price level, return, valuation, capital stock) cannot answer a question about **where capital goes**, because that is a route. The Operators table gives the candidates:

| bond | meaning | role in this question |
|---|---|---|
| `->` sequence | "A then B; B needs A to have happened" | signal precedes price precedes allocation |
| `=>` production | "A produces B; B did not exist before" | allocation produces new capacity, which is the real outcome to test |
| `⋛` comparison | "a threshold relation between two quantities" | allocation is compared with a productive-use threshold |
| `-` subtraction | "A with B removed or absent" | a short horizon is the absence of the future payoff from the decision |

The question as normally posed uses only `+` (conjunction) language: "markets and speculation both affect investment". A flat formula records ingredients and not arrangement, so it cannot distinguish the two mechanisms. The derivation in `01` adds the ordered bonds, and that addition is what makes a per-slot test possible.

Fig 12 :103: *"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."* A settled first-order allocation (one signal, one price, one allocation) becomes an atom at the next level, where sectoral or economy-wide questions compose over it. Nothing here requires a new primitive; the recursion is inside the existing closure (Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."*).

## Q3. The cross-field analogue, and its limit

Fig 11 :50 gives the analogue directly: *"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."* The ecology vocabulary is not an external analogy imported from outside the framework; the figure itself applies it domain-agnostically. The world sources make it concrete:

- Hayek 1945 treats the arbitrageur trading on fleeting local price differences as useful, because the residual is the dispersal of knowledge.
- Keynes 1936 treats the same short-horizon activity as harmful when enterprise becomes "the bubble on a whirlpool of speculation", because the residual is long-run yield over the asset's whole life.

The limit of the analogue: a habitat can be enumerated (Fig 11 :43 says "The habitat is enumerated, never composed"), but the enumeration is never complete. "And contact cascades" (Fig 11 :52), so a second-order effect such as a funding shock reaching real capacity three steps out can be missed by a direct-contact scan. The analogue licenses a method of enumeration, not a claim that all actors have been listed.

## Q4. Dependency order, restated as a chain

Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

1. **Perception.** Can the allocation and the real outcome be read per slot? Without this, no residual arrives.
2. **Vocabulary.** Are there terms for horizon, signal type, mispricing and productive use? The functional-group result in Q1 supplies part of this.
3. **Objective.** What counts as progress? This is the break identified in F4 of `01`: "most productive uses" is usually replaced by a price-based proxy, so the link has no independent instrument.
4. **Planning.** Only now can a design isolate A from B.
5. **Learn and carry.** The method, never a recording, crosses the membrane (Fig 4 :22).

Effort spent refining return-based tests (step 1 and 2 tools) cannot rescue step 3. This is why the recommendation in `01` puts the instrument before the test.

## CONCLUSION

The two mechanisms are **not isomers** under Fig 12 :117 because they do not share the full atom set. They are members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), `signal -> price -> allocation`, with different signal atoms. Answering the question requires ordered bonds, not a flat list of factors, and the dependency chain breaks at the objective link because "most productive uses" has no instrument independent of price. The ecology analogue supports residual-relative valence but does not complete the habitat enumeration. Falsifiers for both the isomer question and the functional-group claim were written before this conclusion.
