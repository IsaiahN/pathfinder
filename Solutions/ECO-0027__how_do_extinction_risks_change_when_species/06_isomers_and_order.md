# Community isomers and dependency order

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading
(see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two communities can hold the same species and differ only in which species depends on which. Does the
figure set license treating them as different objects with different extinction risk, and does the
order of the dependency chain decide which loss propagates? Two questions are owed: (1) community
isomerism under Fig 12, and (2) dependency order under Fig 3.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Operands: species. Bonds: typed dependency relations (food, pollination, habitat provision, reproduction).
Available figure atoms: the seven bonds and negation in Fig 12 / Operators table, but no ecological
bond is derived from them. An ecological reading of a bond is a **binding** from the ecology frame
onto a held operator, not a new primitive and not an import that moves the wall (Fig 6 :23).

Dependency: settle first-order contacts before composing second-order cascades (Fig 12 :104); refuse
an invariance claim before minting a mechanism (Formula step 2).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are two communities with the same species but different bonds isomers?

- Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :95-96 "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond. Two entries can share every ingredient and be inverses of one another."
- ⇒ Under Fig 12's definition, community A and community B with identical species sets but different
  dependency bonds are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. Their
  extinction risk is not a function of the species list.
- **Scope note.** Fig 12's seven bonds are formal typed relations. I do not assert that predation *is*
  subtraction or that pollination *is* conjunction. Those are bindings that would need a source gate
  and a shadow test (Fig 8). The isomer claim only needs the weaker, figure-given fact that which bond
  holds is not recoverable from the operands.
- **Mode:** `specified`. Fig 12 supplies the definition; the ecological instantiation is unmeasured.

### Q2 - What if the species sets differ?

- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- If the two communities share a recurring substructure such as `provider -> consumer` but not all
  species, they are members of the same **functional group**, not isomers. The weaker claim stands:
  the shared substructure fixes the kind of risk change; the particular species fix which case it is.
- **Triangulated** for the definition (Fig 12 :117 and :123 are two distinct figure definitions),
  single-source for the ecological reading.

### Q3 - What decides which loss propagates?

- Fig 3 :20-21 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- ⇒ A dependency chain is ordered from provider to dependent. A break at an upstream provider cannot
  be compensated by effort at a downstream consumer, because the downstream link's input never
  arrives. Fig 3 :22-23 says a reading taken below the break "is a reading of nothing."
- **World corroboration, low overlap:** the coextinction sources report that the practical first step
  is to conserve the host species on which affiliates depend, and the cascading-failure literature
  describes load shifting from a failed component onto neighbours (Motter and Lai's overload model).
  Neither draws on the framework author.
- **Mode:** `specified`, triangulated for the ordering rule; single-source for the particular
  ecological mapping.

### Q4 - Is the cascade first order or higher order?

- Fig 11 :43-44 "The habitat is enumerated, never composed. List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."
- Fig 11 :52-53 gives the worked case: "And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."
- Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." (full line continues "and there is no bottom")
- ⇒ The first-order contact list is necessary but not sufficient. A direct-partner-only scope is the
  DDT error. Settled first-order losses become operands for the next order, which is the recursion
  Fig 12 names, not a new ecological primitive.
- **Mode:** `general` for the rule; the number of orders in any given community is unmeasured.

### Q5 - Is the dependency graph a cycle, and does that change the verdict?

- Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."
- Mutual dependence (a pollinator and a plant each needing the other) can be read as a cycle. Fig 12
  says the sign of the loop decides whether it closes: a positive feedback loop can amplify a small
  loss into a collapse; a negative one can buffer it. This is the structural reason behind the finding
  in `01` F4 that the sign of the risk change is not fixed.
- **Binding, not derivation.** Calling a mutualism a cycle is a mapping onto Fig 12's structure, and
  must be recorded as such. The figure does not derive which ecological relations are cycles.

## FALSIFIER, WRITTEN BEFORE THE RUN

1. If two communities with identical species sets and different dependency arrangements have
   identical per-species risk trajectories under the same perturbation, the isomer claim (Q1) fails.
2. If a downstream intervention can fully prevent an upstream-caused loss without touching the
   upstream contact, the dependency-order claim (Q3) fails.
3. If a mutual dependency behaves identically under positive and negative perturbations, the cycle
   reading (Q5) fails.

Any firing sends the claim back to the domain census; it does not soften the reading.

## CONCLUSION

Under Fig 12, communities with the same species and different bonds are isomers, so species lists
alone cannot carry extinction risk. Where species sets differ but a provider-consumer substructure is
shared, the weaker functional-group claim applies. Fig 3 orders the cascade from provider to
dependent, and Fig 11 requires enumeration outward through second and third order. Mutual dependence
can be read as a signed cycle, which explains why the risk change has no single sign. All of this is
`specified` and ungrounded: no community was measured in this derivation.
