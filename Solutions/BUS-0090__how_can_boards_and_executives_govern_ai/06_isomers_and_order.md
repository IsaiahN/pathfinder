# Governance modes as isomers, and the order of the oversight chain

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

`01` fixed the shape: per-slot residual, non-updating anchor, renewed reach, a refusal seat, an enumerated habitat. Two further questions are owed:

1. Are two governance modes that use the same ingredients different things, or two spellings of one thing? If they are different things, what carries the difference?
2. In what order do the links of the oversight chain break, and which link has no instrument?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are two governance modes isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
Fig 12 :93: **"The arrangement is the substance."**

Consider two modes over the same atom list:

- **Mode A, serial approval.** Management produces a checklist score; the board's gate releases a deployment when the score is green; incidents are recorded in the same system the score uses; the board reviews monthly.
- **Mode B, per-slot ground contact.** Management brings a characterised residual per deployment; independent rooms are booked against the residual the last room surfaced; the reference is an incident feed the audited team cannot update; the board holds a refusal with provenance.

Read the ingredient lists side by side: both have a board, a score, a control set, an incident record and a review cadence. By the flat formula they are the same governance system. They are not. In A the checklist score is bonded to the decision by a convention that decides an outcome (Fig 10 :47), and the incident feed is bonded to the same frame that produced the score, so the reference updates. In B the score is bonded to *the budget* (Fig 9 :60), the incident feed is bonded to a frame whose closure differs (Fig 6 :23), and the decision is bonded to a refusal (Operators :58). Same atoms, different bonds, different substance. They are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if Mode A can be written without an atom that Mode B requires, or vice versa (for example, if the independent incident feed cannot be introduced into A at all, so it is not a shared atom), the isomer claim fails and the relation is composition plus import or subtraction instead. This is a live condition, not a rhetorical one: importing a new atom is available to a seat (Fig 11 :61) but costs independence (Fig 6 :24), so the shared-atom condition has to be checked rather than assumed. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 :117); single-source for the application to boards.

### Q2 - Which part survives its own use?

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

The oversight seat is a catalyst when each refusal returns it to the ground with its independence intact. It is a spent part when it has authored the standard it now grades against: Fig 10 :47 says "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one", and Fig 10 :48 calls it "a constant the seat authored, carrying the seat's authority". A board that designs the metric and then monitors the metric is spending its oversight seat across uses. Nothing in a checklist records which kind the seat is. This is why the pre-registered refuter asks for independence to be measured separately, not inferred from a clean report. Mode `general` for the catalyst definition, `specified` for the board application.

### Q3 - What is the functional group?

Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
Fig 10 :29: **"→ keep the anchor reachable"**

All four oversight members (board, internal audit, external audit, incident feed as a record) share the functional group `seat → non-updating ground → residual read`. The group says what kind (a ground maintainer); the qualifier says which member (which seat, at which scale, for which slot). This matters because it licenses *substitution of members* without changing the kind, provided the independence condition holds. Fig 10 :67-69: "The seat above is not a larger copy of this one. Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between." Replacing an external auditor with a bigger internal committee is not swapping one functional-group member for another if the internal committee shares the builder's evidence pool; it is collapsing two frames into one (Fig 8 :48). Mode `specified`.

### Q4 - The order of the chain, and the link without an instrument

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
Fig 3 :29-30: **"Before asking whether a system is good, ask which link it currently stops at, and whether the answer was measured or assumed."**

The chain in this question:

1. **Perception.** Can the board tell what is actually deployed, and distinguish it from what was deployed last quarter? NACD: only 21 percent of boards had audited where AI was in use. This link has an instrument (an inventory) and obvious failures.
2. **Vocabulary.** Does it have terms for what a deployment affords, what can be moved, opened, joined or blocked, and which actors are in contact? Mostly absent. A risk register names ingredients, not bonds.
3. **Objective.** Can it say what would count as progress or safety here, in its own terms, against a reference that does not update? **This is the missing link.** It has no instrument in most board reporting, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments. It is the middle link Fig 3 :24-27 predicts.
4. **Planning.** Which differing room is booked next against the residual the last one surfaced (Fig 7 :42)? Often present as an audit calendar, but selected by the calendar rather than by the residual.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, carry it as a method, not a recording (Fig 4 :22-25).

Optimising link 1 (more model metrics) or link 4 (more audit meetings) cannot rescue a break at link 3. A reading taken below the break is not a weak reading; "It is a reading of nothing" (Fig 3 :22-23). Mode `specified`. **Falsifier:** show a board whose objective link is measured by an instrument that does not update on its own reports, and whose additional perception or planning investment still fails to change outcomes. That would move the break elsewhere and require a re-census.

### Q5 - A checklist cannot name a route

Fig 12 :111-113: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."** And: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."**

A checklist names states: control present, policy approved, assessment filed. It cannot write the ordered move from a residual to a refusal, because that requires `→`, `⋛`, `−` and `¬`. It cannot say that a term management holds is false. A governance vocabulary with `+` only "can say what is missing from an arrangement and cannot say that an arrangement is wrong, so it can only ever accumulate" (Operators table amendment :73-74). This is the structural reason "checklists alone" fails, independent of any empirical study. Mode `general`.

### Q6 - Two currencies, which do not add

Fig 12 :125-127: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."** "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds." "They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them and will not notice the shortfall."

A governance framework has a description cost (the length of the policy, the number of controls, the training material) and an action cost (an independent audit, an external incident feed, an affected-community channel, a refusal that stops a deployment). A long chain of cheap controls is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand, for example a single refusal backed by an external audit, is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle. Neither figure predicts the other, so both must be read. Summing them into one "governance maturity score" is the error this names, not the fix. Mode `general`, triangulated with Fig 1 :24-27 (frame-internal quantities) and Fig 9 :60 (filters versus witnesses).

### Q7 - Round trip on the abstraction

Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

- **Sent up:** the figure set, chiefly Fig 1 :19/:22 (per-slot residual), Fig 2 :33-34 (anchor must not update), Fig 10 :42/:82 (blind verifier, arrange for refusal), Fig 9 :60 (filters not witnesses).
- **Abstraction:** the shorthand "govern per slot with an independent ground and a refusal seat; checklists are budgets".
- **Concretisation back down:** a board dashboard with a single AI risk score, an internal audit using management's metrics, and a green checklist gate.

The gap is nonzero. Lost on the way down: the per-slot indexing (collapsed into one number); the independence debit (the auditor draws on the builder's evidence); the negation (the gate releases rather than refuses); the filter/witness distinction (the score becomes the decision). What survives cleanly: the need for a reference that does not update and a seat able to refuse. R_T is not measured numerically because no board measurement was taken; it is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints. Treating "the formula is the condensed version of the metalaws" as identity would set R_T to zero and would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

## CONCLUSION

Two governance modes over the same atom set are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117, provided the shared-atom condition survives the falsifier in Q1. The oversight seat is a catalyst when its independence regenerates and a spent part when it authors the standard it grades. The functional group is a seat ranging against a non-updating ground; members are swappable only when their closures differ. The chain breaks at the objective link, which has no instrument, so more metrics and more meetings land above the break. A checklist can name states and cannot name a route, a refusal or progress, which is the structural content of "not checklists alone". Description cost and action cost are held apart. The abstraction carries a nonzero round-trip gap, recorded as an enumerated set rather than assumed to be zero.
