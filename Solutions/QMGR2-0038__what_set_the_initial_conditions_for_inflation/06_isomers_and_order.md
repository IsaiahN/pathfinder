# Structure, order and isomers for the initial-conditions residual

**Mode: `specified` on the structural derivation, `general` on the bond reading, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

What structure and what order must an answer to the initial-conditions question have, and are the competing world proposals isomers over a shared atom set or unrelated answers to unrelated questions? A falsifier is stated before the conclusion in the FALSIFIER section below.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms already in the inflationary frame: inflaton field value, field velocity, potential shape, expansion rate, curvature perturbation, vacuum state. Bonds available: the seven in Fig 12 plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. The boundary condition is not an atom of this set.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Order by dependency, not by importance

Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

The dependency chain for this question runs:

1. **Past boundary** (no settled instrument): what replaces the singular surface.
2. **Kinematic patch** (partially instrumented through large-scale modes): why a region larger than the Hubble radius was smooth.
3. **Dynamical entry** (well instrumented within model classes): why the field value and kinetic fraction landed on a flat potential.
4. **Predictions** (grounded): n_s, r, adiabaticity, non-Gaussianity.

Most published effort sits at link 4 because that is where the data are. A result at link 4 does not travel up to link 1. Fig 3 also warns that optimising a link that already works cannot rescue a broken one, so better CMB constraints cannot substitute for a boundary condition.

### Q2. Partition or union

Fig 9's union rule states that a bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones, and that a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. Applying it to links 1 to 3:

- Link 3 can be cleared by an attractor in a plateau potential while link 1 remains open.
- Link 2 can be cleared by a kinetic-dominated Planck surface while link 1 remains open.
- Link 1 can be addressed by a no-boundary proposal while links 2 and 3 remain model-dependent.

So this is a **union with at least three conditions and nonempty intersections**. One lever clears a single-condition region and leaves the intersections standing. The residue after any one solution is smaller and categorically different (Fig 9 :54 to 58). The honest workspace is three loops plus one named intersection loop, not one loop with three labels.

**Falsifier for Q2:** a proof that link 1 follows from link 3, or that link 2 is identical to link 1, collapsing the union to a partition.

### Q3. Are the competing proposals isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 12 :124 names the functional group: a substructure whose presence fixes the outcome, where the group says what kind and the qualifier says which member.

Write the candidate boundary-condition proposals over the shared atom set {inflaton, potential, Planck density, de Sitter vacuum, measure, decoherence, time orientation}:

| proposal | atoms held | dominant bond | what it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| no-boundary wave function | de Sitter vacuum, Planck density, time orientation | sequence, a smooth closing of the past | a weight over field values |
| tunneling from nothing | de Sitter vacuum, Planck density | production, B from nothing prior | a creation event with an arrow |
| kinetic dominance at Planck epoch | inflaton, velocity, potential, Planck density | sequence, kinetic to potential | a Planck surface feeding slow roll |
| bounce or contracting phase | expansion, curvature, time orientation | comparison at a threshold, then sequence | a transition through a minimum radius |
| measure selection on a landscape | de Sitter vacuum, measure, decoherence | comparison, a threshold over vacua | a typicality weight |

Same atoms, different bonds, different behaviour. Under Fig 12 they are **isomers over the shared atom set**, not variants of one answer. The difference is invisible if you list only the ingredients (a smooth patch and a field value) and decisive in the behaviour (which observable signature each predicts). As each proposal is actually written in the literature they share only a subset, so the weaker claim is a **functional group**: each is a past-boundary supplier, with a different qualifier.

**Falsifier (written before this conclusion):** if one proposal requires an atom that none of the others holds and that cannot be composed from the shared set, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. If two proposals are provably the same term under a symmetric bond, one of them is a spelling, not a substance.

### Q4. Negation is not a bond here

Fig 12's amendment says a vocabulary without a unary operator can say what a situation contains, what follows what and how far along it is, but cannot say that any of those is false. The BGV past-incompleteness result functions as a negation of the internal-derivation route: it refuses the claim that inflation alone supplies its past. That refusal is not a new bond joining two atoms; it removes the standing of the internal-derivation term to be composed over. This is why F1 in `01` is a refusal before a mint, not a composition.

**Falsifier for Q4:** a derivation that states past-completeness as a positive composition inside the inflationary frame with no imported atom. Then the negation was unnecessary and the order of refusal in `01` is wrong.

### Q5. Settling and recursion

Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"

Inflation's predictions are settled at the CMB level, so the inflationary core can serve as an atom for a higher-level comparison with a candidate past-boundary frame. That comparison is second order: (settled inflationary core) compared with (settled or candidate boundary frame). It is legal only if the boundary frame is settled; where it is not, the comparison is a candidate, not a composition. This is the distinction between Fig 12's necessity (a term must be settled to be composed over) and its insufficiency (settling does not guarantee composability).

**Falsifier for Q5:** a demonstration that a candidate boundary frame cannot serve as an atom even after its predictions are settled, which would make the second-order comparison illegal.

## CONCLUSION

The residual's structure is a union of at least three conditions ordered by dependency, with nonempty intersections. The competing proposals are isomers over a shared atom set when written in terms of atoms and bonds, and a functional group as actually published. The BGV result acts as a negation, refusing the internal-derivation route before any mint. Recursion lets a settled inflationary core become an atom for a second-order comparison with a boundary frame, but only when that frame is settled. No new primitive is introduced by any of these moves.
