# Conditions as a union: structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks both which conditions support cooperation among strangers and how those conditions vary across societies. A list of conditions cannot carry the second half. Two obligations follow: (1) say what kind of structure the condition set is, and (2) say what order the conditions stand in, if any. Both are decided from the figures before the world candidates are admitted.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the metatheory: Space, Time, Energy (states) and Spectrum, Gradient, Network (structures), seven bonds plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. World candidates (repetition, reputation, sanction, boundary rule, group structure) are compositions or imports, not atoms. Actors, currency and anchor per domain are in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §1; this derivation works mainly in D1 and D3.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Is the condition set a flat list?

Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
Fig 12 :46-47: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands. A recipe that names only its ingredients has recorded half of itself."**

A list such as "reputation, punishment, dense networks, shared norms" records ingredients and not the bond. Reputation before helping is `→` sequence with a comparison attached; sanction after defection is `⇒` production following a subtraction; a norm that survives its own use is a catalyst; two societies with the same ingredients in different bonds are different substances. So the condition set is a molecule, not a bag.

**Falsifier:** show that the list of conditions fully predicts the cooperation outcome with no information about arrangement. If arrangement adds nothing, the bond is free and Fig 12 :5 fails here.

### Q2 — Which bonds appear in a condition claim?

From the Operators table and Fig 12:

- `→` sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened."* Reputation must be known before the donor decides (Nowak's `q > c/b`). A sanction follows a defection. Fig 12 :73: **"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."** Order is load-bearing.
- `⋛` comparison: *"a threshold relation between two quantities."* Each of Nowak's five rules is a threshold: `r > c/b`, `w > c/b`, `q > c/b`, `b/c > k`, `b/c > 1 + n/m`. The whole mechanism literature is written in the comparison bond. This is a structural observation with a world instance, not a derivation of the rules.
- `⇒` production: *"A produces B; B did not exist before."* A rule creates an enforcement capacity that did not exist before it was agreed.
- `−` subtraction: *"A with B removed or absent."* A boundary rule removes outsiders from the set of claimants; Ostrom's user associations define who may use the commons.
- `¬` negation is not a bond. It is the eighth operator and unary (Operators table :70-78). A falsifier written before the run is a negation: it refuses a claim rather than adding an ingredient. This is why the refuters in `01` §4 are not part of the condition list.

**Falsifier:** write all candidate conditions using only symmetric `+`, and show no loss of predictive information. That would collapse the bond structure and break this derivation.

### Q3 — Are two societies' condition sets isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
Fig 13 :124-125: *"Two chains built from different links may compose functionally identical terms, and the converse holds too: the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."*

Take two societies with the same atoms: repeated encounters, knowledge of reputation, a network, a group boundary, a sanctioning rule. If society A bonds them as `reputation → help → sanction` and society B as `boundary → repeated encounter → help`, the ingredient lists match and the behaviour need not. By Fig 12's definition they are isomers, and the difference is invisible in a list of conditions. This is the structural answer to "how do those conditions vary across societies": variation is a change of bond and of the conditions the seed lands in (Fig 4 :34-35), not a change in the number of ingredients.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two societies with identical condition atoms in different arrangements return the same cooperation outcome in the same residual, the isomer claim fails and the weaker functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 — Functional group versus isomer

Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

If the mechanisms do not share one atom set as written (Nowak's kin selection needs relatedness, which a stranger interaction excludes; network reciprocity needs a graph degree), then the honest weaker claim is a functional group: each mechanism says what kind of cooperation problem it can solve, and the local parameters say which member of that kind is present. Henrich et al. (2006) supply the analogue at the measurement level: all 15 populations show some willingness to punish, so the group is present everywhere; the magnitude varies substantially, so the qualifier differs by population. Group and qualifier are both in the data.

**Verdict:** `specified`, single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12), with a field-level analogue from Henrich et al. that is not counted as a second figure.

### Q5 — Cycle and catalyst in the condition set

Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input."** Direct reciprocity is a cycle: help given returns as help received, and Nowak notes that it fails when errors break the loop (*"Tit-for-tat cannot correct mistakes"*).
Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."** A norm or a monitor survives its own use and regenerates across encounters. Ostrom's finding that users *"frequently develop sophisticated mechanisms for decision-making and rule enforcement"* (Nobel press release, 2009) is a catalyst claim at the institutional level.

**Falsifier:** show a norm or monitor that is consumed by its first use and never regenerates in any society. That breaks the catalyst reading.

### Q6 — What is the order of the union?

Fig 9 :56: **"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."**
Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**

The union has a dependency order, not an importance ranking. Perception of who counts as a stranger precedes vocabulary for what conditions afford; vocabulary precedes the objective of what counts as progress in that society; the objective precedes planning an intervention; planning precedes carrying a method to the next society (Fig 3 :4-15). Nowak's thresholds impose a second, local order inside an encounter: reputation must be known before the decision; a defection must occur before sanction; a boundary must exist before exclusion. These are two different orders at two different levels, and reading one against the other is the error the figures warn against (Fig 6 :31: **"Neither answer tells you much about the other. Run them as two tracks, and never read one against the other."**).

**Falsifier:** show that the social-learning order and the within-encounter order are the same order at one level. That would collapse the levels and break the membrane rule.

## CONCLUSION

The condition set is a molecule, not a list. Its bonds are ordered where sequence, comparison, production and subtraction are involved, and only the falsifier carries negation. Two societies with the same condition atoms in different arrangements are isomers under Fig 12's definition; where they do not share one atom set, the honest weaker claim is a functional group whose qualifier is the local measurement. The dependency order of the union is Fig 3's chain, and the within-encounter order of thresholds is a different track at a different level. This framing rescues the variation half of the question without a seventh primitive, without an import that moves the wall, and without naming a condition the framework cannot name. It leaves the empirical settlement owed to the ground.
