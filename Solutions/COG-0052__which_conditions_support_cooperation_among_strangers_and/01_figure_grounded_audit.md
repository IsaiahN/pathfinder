## 0. The residual, stated in effect terms

**Question as given:** Which conditions support cooperation among strangers, and how do those conditions vary across societies?

**The residual R.** A frame predicts that a stranger with whom there is no kinship, no repeated encounter, and no enforcer will not be helped. Observation returns something that fails to resolve against that prediction: people do cooperate with genetically unrelated strangers, often in large groups, with people they will never meet again, and when reputation gains are small or absent (Fehr and Gächter, 2002, abstract). The magnitude of the apparently relevant behaviours also differs substantially between populations (Henrich et al., 2006, abstract). R is the gap between the no-mechanism prediction and that observation, plus the gap between a single list of conditions and their observed variation.

State R by arity, symmetry and scale before naming any frame, per Fig 9 :21-23 (*"R, described / arity · symmetry · scale"*):

- **Arity:** multiple and overlapping conditions, not one lever. The residual is a **union compound**, not a single hard question. Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."* Nowak (2006) independently confirms the overlap: punishment is not a fifth independent mechanism but *"can enhance the level of cooperation that is achieved in such models"* built on indirect reciprocity, group selection or network reciprocity.
- **Symmetry:** the conditions are **not interchangeable**. Some are ordered. Reputation must be known before the decision to help (Nowak's `q > c/b`); a norm must be breached before punishment can be applied; a boundary rule must be drawn before a commons can be governed. Under Fig 12, these are ordered bonds (`→` sequence, `⇒` production, `⋛` comparison), not a symmetric `+` pile of ingredients.
- **Scale:** individual encounter, local group, society, cross-society comparison. These are different strata with different actors, currencies and anchors (Fig 10 :68: *"Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between."*). A mechanism settled at one stratum becomes an atom for the next (Fig 12 :104), but it does not thereby explain the variation at the next.
- **Slot indexing:** R is indexed by the specific cooperation problem in the specific place, never by a global average. Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."* The slot here is, for example, "one-shot anonymous public good in population X", not "cooperation" in general.

**What an answer would look like, bound as an ordinary term (formula step "Direction").** Before searching, write down the sought-for shape as a claim that can accrue a residual:

> `SHAPE_COOP`: for any cooperation residual s, the conditions that support cooperation in s are the actors, conditions and relations in contact with s, enumerated outward until the cascade stops mattering; their valence is relative to s; because the conditions overlap, s is a compound to be unbundled; and because the seed's outcome depends on where it lands, the same condition need not have the same valence in another society.

This is a prediction, not a finding. Committing it before the run is what lets it be refused later (Fig 5 :65-66).

## 1. Domain census, before choosing any figure line

One line per domain: actors, currency, anchor. No nameable anchor means it is not a domain yet. Then partition or union, then dependency order.

| domain | actors | currency | anchor | question type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D1, the encounter** | donor, recipient, observer, stranger | cost c, benefit b, reputation, payoff | observed cooperation rate in a one-shot or repeated interaction | union of mechanisms, not a partition |
| **D2, the local habitat and institution** | resource users, monitors, sanctioners, norm carriers; conditions such as repeat probability w, reputation knowledge q, network degree k, group count m, boundary and rule enforcement | decisions, rule enforcement, conflict resolution, information about reputation | field outcome: whether the commons or the group persists, measured | habitat, enumerated (Fig 11 :43), never composed |
| **D3, cross-society comparison** | populations, field sites, experimental labs | measured magnitude of punishment, altruism, cooperation rate | the cross-population measurement itself (Henrich et al., 2006) | per-slot residuals; explicitly not averaged (Fig 1 :19) |
| **D4, the verification frame** | experimenters, replicators, preregistration venues, outside readers | agreement and residual, replication outcome | the ground, never agreement (Fig 2 :35, Fig 11 :90) | in this simulated run all seats draw on one evidence pool, so its independence is zero |

**Partition or union.** D1 is a **union** of overlapping mechanisms. Nowak (2006) lists five (kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, group selection) and immediately complicates the list by saying punishment *"can enhance the level of cooperation that is achieved in such models"* rather than constituting an independent mechanism. Ostrom's design principles are likewise conjoint: the Nobel press release says users *"frequently develop sophisticated mechanisms for decision-making and rule enforcement to handle conflicts of interest"* (2009), plural and interacting. So the question is not "which one lever" but "which compound in this slot". Treating it as a list of separable levers is the collapse Fig 9 :54 names.

**Dependency order (Fig 3).** Perception (tell a stranger from kin; tell cooperation from defection, including errors, which Nowak notes break tit-for-tat) → vocabulary (cost, benefit, reputation, norm, sanction, boundary) → objective (what counts as progress here, in the society's own terms) → planning (which condition to introduce) → learn and carry (which methods transfer, as head starts, across societies). Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* The middle link is the likely break: having terms for what a condition affords is not the same as being able to say what would count as cooperation progress in that society. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* A cross-society comparison with no shared objective is a reading below that break, which Fig 3 :23 calls *"a reading of nothing."*

## 3. Findings F1 to F9

Every finding gives verbatim figure text first, then the verdict, then a refuter written now. Triangulation status is recorded per finding; a figure plus a world source from a different field is **not** two figures.

### F1 — Cooperation is a property of agents in a habitat, so "the conditions" are the habitat

Fig 11 :39: **"capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."**
Fig 11 :40: **"An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."**
Fig 11 :43: **"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."**
Fig 11 :52: **"And contact cascades."**

And at a different scale and with different vocabulary, Fig 4 :34-35: **"A seed carries no small tree: it carries what will grow one, and the conditions it lands in decide what it becomes."**

The question's word "conditions" is already the framework's word "habitat". So the correct operation is enumeration, not composition: list what acts on the cooperation residual, then what contacts those, outward until the cascade stops mattering (Fig 11 :44-48). Fig 11 :46 gives the template directly: **"conditions: light, water, temperature, season, terrain"**. Translated to this residual without claiming the list is complete: repeat probability, knowledge of reputation, network degree, group structure, monitoring, sanction, boundary rule, cultural norm. These are read off the world, not invented.

**Verdict:** `general` structural, **triangulated** (Fig 11 habitat + Fig 4 seed/conditions, low overlap between contact language and membrane language).
**Refuter:** show a durable change in cooperation produced by changing an isolated individual while every contact in the habitat is held fixed. That breaks Fig 11 :39-40 at this level.

### F2 — Valence is relative to the residual: this is the "vary across societies" answer

Fig 11 :49: **"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."**
Fig 11 :50: **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."**
Fig 11 :51: **"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."**

Fig 4 :34-35, already quoted in F1, supplies the second route: a mechanism is a seed, and the conditions it lands in decide what it becomes.

A punishment institution, a reputation system, or a tight network is not a cooperation-supporting condition in itself. It is a condition relative to a specific residual. The same sanction that sustains contribution in one commons can suppress voluntary cooperation in another setting; the same density that lets cooperators cluster also traps them when the network is too closed to bring in new information. This is why "how do those conditions vary across societies" is not a footnote to "which conditions". It is the same enumeration re-run per slot, because the residual changed.

**Verdict:** `specified` prediction, **triangulated** (Fig 11 valence + Fig 4 seed/conditions).
**Refuter:** exhibit one condition that raises cooperation across every enumerated cooperation residual and every society, with no sign reversal. That breaks Fig 11 :51.

### F3 — The conditions are a union of overlapping compounds, not a list

Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
Fig 9 :56: **"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."**
Fig 9 :57: **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."**

The world agrees from a different field. Nowak (2006, PMC full text): *"Here I discuss five mechanisms for the evolution of cooperation: kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity and group selection."* And in the conclusion: *"Punishment can enhance the level of cooperation that is achieved in such models."* Ostrom's Nobel citation: *"resource users frequently develop sophisticated mechanisms for decision-making and rule enforcement to handle conflicts of interest"* (Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2009). Both describe conjoint conditions. A frame that asks "which single condition" will clear the single-condition regions and leave the intersections, which are the actual cases in stranger cooperation.

**Verdict:** `general` structural, **single-source** for the figure claim (Fig 9), with world corroboration from evolutionary theory and institutional economics that is explicitly not counted as a second figure.
**Refuter:** show the candidate mechanisms partition the residual, each cooperation failure in exactly one condition with no intersection. That breaks Fig 9 :54-57.

### F4 — Do not average across societies

Fig 1 :19: **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."**
Fig 1 :22: **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."**
Fig 1 :28: **"The gap is measured per object, not per step."**

Henrich et al. (2006, abstract) report from 15 populations that *"all populations demonstrate some willingness to administer costly punishment as unequal behavior increases"* but *"the magnitude of this punishment varies substantially across populations"* and *"costly punishment positively covaries with altruistic behavior across populations."* The universal part and the varying part are both in the same measurement. A global mean would keep the first and erase the second, which is the exact live signal the question asks about.

**Verdict:** `general` methodological, **triangulated** (Fig 1 per-slot + Fig 11 :51 no standing list).
**Refuter:** show a cross-society cooperation average that correctly identifies the intervention in every slot, so that the aggregate carries the per-slot information. That breaks Fig 1 :19 and :22.

### F5 — Strangers are frames with different closures: cooperation is convention or triangulation, not negotiation

Fig 2 :29: **"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."**
Fig 2 :35: **"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."**
Fig 2 :40: **"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"**
Fig 2 :41: **"efficient for a convention: agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it"**
Fig 8 :45: **"Two frames together reach what neither reaches alone."**
Fig 8 :48-49: **"How much two frames draw on the same evidence decides both whether their agreement means anything and whether their union has any surplus."**

This is the most delicate point in the audit and it must not be flattened. Correlation has two valences. A shared norm between strangers is a **convention**: agreement about a shared meaning is the product, so high overlap is efficient there. A shared evidence pool in a test of *which condition causes cooperation* is a **verdict**: there the same high overlap is disqualifying. Nowak (2006) makes the same structural point in a different vocabulary: *"Indirect reciprocity resembles the invention of money. The money that fuels the engines of indirect reciprocity is reputation."* Reputation is a convention whose value depends on a network of frames with partly different access. A mirror cannot generate that value.

**Verdict:** `general` structural, **triangulated** (Fig 2 valence + Fig 8 independence/surplus, low overlap).
**Refuter:** show two frames with rho approximately 1, drawing on the entire same evidence pool, giving each other genuine mutual correction on a world fact. That breaks Fig 2 :35 and :40.

### F6 — Describe the residual first, then look for the condition

Fig 9 :27-28: **"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"**
Fig 9 :30-31: **"your words are borrowed too, so describe the effect rather than the cause"**
Fig 8 :53: **"Residual first, frame second."**

The common failure in this literature as imported into a general claim is to name the institution first (reputation, punishment, norms) and then find a society where it appears. The figures require the opposite: describe the specific failure to resolve, in effect terms, then find the frame whose closure predicts its shape. Fig 9 :32: **"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."**

**Verdict:** `general` methodological, **triangulated** (Fig 9 :27-28 + Fig 8 :53).
**Refuter:** a successful general intervention designed by first selecting a favoured institution and then finding the cooperation problem it happens to fit, with the selection order provenanced. That breaks the rule.

### F7 — Where the cross-society chain breaks

Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
Fig 3 :20-21: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
Fig 3 :26-27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

Perception of cooperation and defection has obvious tests (including error handling, which Nowak notes: *"Tit-for-tat cannot correct mistakes"*). Planning an intervention has obvious tests. The link between them, having terms for what a local condition affords and being able to say what would count as progress in that society, has neither. Cross-society work that imports a single objective (a fixed payoff metric, a fixed definition of "efficient") and then compares societies is effort landing above the break. Fig 3 :23: **"It is a reading of nothing."**

**Verdict:** `specified` diagnostic, **single-source** (Fig 3). The world sources make the same practical point in the language of external validity, but that is not a second figure.
**Refuter:** an instrument for cooperation progress that is demonstrably valid in every society without local calibration. That shows the middle link has an instrument.

### F8 — Which bonds can express a condition claim

Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input."**
Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."**
Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Direct reciprocity is a cycle (help returned to donor). A norm or a monitoring institution is a catalyst (it survives its own use, regenerating across encounters). Reputation, sanction, and boundary rule form a functional group: presence fixes the kind of cooperation problem solvable, local parameters say which society's version. Two societies with the same atom set arranged differently are isomers in Fig 12's sense: same atoms, different bond, different substance, which is invisible in a list of ingredients. This is the structural reason a list of conditions cannot answer the variation part of the question. The full derivation is in `06_isomers_and_order.md`.

**Verdict:** `specified`, **single-source** (Fig 12); the analogy to chemical isomerism is the figure's own, not imported by this derivation.
**Refuter:** show that two societies with identical condition atoms and different arrangements return the same cooperation outcome in the same residual. That breaks the isomer reading.

### F9 — The world supplies candidates as imports, with a gate and a debit

Fig 6 :23: **"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."**
Fig 8 :50: **"But shared atoms are not the signature."**

The candidate mechanisms (repeat probability, reputation knowledge, network degree, group structure, punishment, user-made rules) are imports from evolutionary biology, experimental economics and institutional studies into the metatheory. They are not derived from the six-term substrate. Each arrives with a source gate (provenance) and a shadow test (does it explain a described gap), and each is debited against independence (Fig 8 :51-52 and Seat Map :163). They are therefore recorded as **world candidates with their own ground support in their own fields**, not as figure verdicts. The figures decide the shape; the experiments decide which candidate fills which slot.

**Verdict:** `specified`, **triangulated** for the import rule (Fig 6 :23 + Fig 8 :50-52).
**Refuter:** derive one of these mechanisms solely from the six-term substrate with no import and no provenance debt, or show an import with no source gate that still moves the wall legitimately.

## 4. Pre-registered refuter

Required before any outreach. Seat Map :287: *"a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."*

The `SHAPE_COOP` claim in §0 is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. One condition is shown to raise cooperation across every enumerated cooperation residual and every society, with no sign reversal. (Breaks Fig 11 :51.)
2. Cooperation is shown to be fully determined by individual disposition with the habitat held constant. (Breaks Fig 11 :39-40.)
3. A global cross-society average is shown to locate the correct intervention in every slot. (Breaks Fig 1 :19 and :22.)
4. Two frames sharing the whole evidence pool are shown to give each other genuine correction on a world fact. (Breaks Fig 2 :35 and :40.)
5. Two societies with the same condition atoms in different arrangements are shown to have the same outcome in the same residual. (Breaks the Fig 12 isomer reading in F8.)

If any fires, the shape claim is withdrawn. Until one is run, no part of this may be labelled `grounded`.

## 5. Recommendation

1. Lead with the shape, not with a list: conditions are the habitat of a specifically described cooperation residual, enumerated, with valence relative to that residual (F1, F2).
2. Unbundle the union before searching. Ask which compound is live in this slot, not which condition wins (F3).
3. Report per-slot results from every society. Never publish only the pooled effect (F4).
4. Keep the convention/verdict distinction explicit. Shared norms are the product; shared evidence in a causal test is a defect (F5).
5. Describe the cooperation failure before selecting the mechanism or the field site (F6).
6. State the mode on every sentence. The candidates from the world are `specified` and provenanced, not `grounded` by this derivation (F9).
7. Fig 4 :22: **"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."** Send the enumeration procedure and the refuter, not a conclusion packaged for agreement.
