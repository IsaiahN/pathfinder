# Isomers, order and structure in institutional change

**Mode: `general` for the structural derivation, `specified` for the institutional reading, with one live type strain named below.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two further questions are owed after `01`:

1. If both a practice and the institution it produces are composed of the same held atoms, what is the structural relation between two institutions that differ only in arrangement? (isomer question)
2. What is the order of the whole process, and do the four actor classes form a sequence, a union, or a cycle? (order question)

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the institutional-research frame: actors (ordinary people, officials, firms, movements), rules, routines, resources, habitats, records, a ground at the slot. Bonds held: the seven plus negation, including sequence (→), production (⇒), comparison (⋛) and subtraction (−). No new primitive is available without import. The institutional stratum is a composition at a higher level, not a separate kind of thing (Fig 12 :103, :104).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are two institutions isomers?

- Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*
- Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance."*
- Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*

Two institutions built from the same actors, rules, routines and resources but with different bonds (who reports to whom, which rule binds which act, what is counted as payment) are isomers under Fig 12 :117. Their ingredient lists match and their behaviour does not, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond. This is why a reform that changes only the org chart while keeping the same measured residuals may be a different substance or may merely be a different spelling of the same one; the bond decides, and it is not recoverable from the ingredient list.

**Converse:** two institutions reached by different historical routes may be the same substance (Fig 13 :121), which is why "path dependence" does not by itself tell you the current structure. Recovered from the lineage, not from the artefact (Fig 13 :129: *"Nothing about a composed term tells you, from looking at it, which route produced it or whether that route was available."*).

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two institutions with identical atoms and identical typed bonds behave identically at every slot, the isomer claim is empty for that case; if they differ while sharing atoms and bonds, some unrecorded atom has been smuggled in and the census must be redone.

### Q2 — What is the order: sequence, union or cycle?

- Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."*
- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*

All three are present at different objects, so asking for one is another bundle error:

- **Sequence** governs a single handoff: perceive the slot, name the practice, set what counts as progress, repeat the act, carry what worked. Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- **Union** governs the four actor classes at one slot (Fig 9 :55). They are not steps in a sequence; they overlap.
- **Cycle** governs the recurrence across handoffs. Fig 12 :121 says the sign decides whether the loop closes. A reinforcing cycle reproduces the institution; a transforming cycle changes what counts as an atom at the next level. A chain cannot close by construction, so a linear "protest leads to reform leads to stability" story cannot represent the recurrence.

**Falsifier:** if a single ordered chain over the four classes predicted the trajectory at every slot with no cycle and no union remainder, the union-and-cycle claim would be redundant.

### Q3 — Is there a functional group, and does it give a standing list?

- Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- Fig 11 :49: *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."*
- Fig 11 :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*

The four actor classes form a functional group in the figure's sense: "an institution is reshaped by practice" fixes the kind of relation, and "people / officials / firms / movements" are qualifiers selecting members. A functional group is not a standing list of good actors. Which member fixes the outcome depends on the residual (Fig 11 :49), and the list does not survive a change of residual (Fig 11 :51). This is the precise distinction the question needs: the classes are real slots, but their valence is not carried.

**Falsifier:** a fixed ranking of the four classes that predicts benefactor versus malfactor across a documented change of residual would refute this and reinstate a standing list.

### Q4 — Catalysts and spent parts

- Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."*
- Fig 12 :120: *"Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*

Some institutional parts are catalysts: an office, a norm, a budget line that survives its own use and can act again. Others are spent after one use. The figure notes that nothing in the ingredient record tells you which is which; repeated use is the discriminator. Applied here: a protest repertoire that regenerates itself each cycle is a catalyst, a one-off campaign is spent, and the difference is invisible in a description that lists only the actors and demands.

**Falsifier:** if a part's catalyst status could be read from its ingredient list with no observation across uses, Fig 12 :119 would be empty for this domain.

### Q5 — What do the two currencies price?

- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."*
- Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."*
- Fig 9 :60: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."*

Describing an institutional arrangement (bits) and testing whether it actually pays at the slot (actions) are different accounts. A long written code of conduct is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle; a short norm over a costly actor is cheap to state and may be impossible to settle at all. Summing them loses the shortfall. The practical consequence for this question: counting the number of policies, rules or signatories is a filter for budget, not a witness for the verdict. The verdict is whether contact changed and the ground paid.

**Falsifier:** a single measure that predicts both description cost and action cost across institutional cases with no residual in either account would refute the two-currency claim.

## THE LIVE STRAIN (named, not hidden)

Fig 12 :117 (same atoms, different arrangement, different substance) strains against the ordinary institutional-research intuition that changing a formal rule changes the atoms (a new law is a new thing). Re-census resolves most of it: a new rule is a new bond over held atoms if the actors, resources and habitat are unchanged, so it is an isomer case; it is an import (a genuine new atom) only if the frame lacked the primitive before (Fig 6 :23). The strain that remains live is the **fixed-atom condition**: in real institutional change the habitat also changes, so the atom set is rarely fixed across the before and after. That is why the isomer claim in `07` and `09` is stated conditionally: *over a fixed atom set*, two institutions are isomers. When the atom set changes, the relation is composition plus import, and the import needs provenance and a shadow test (Fig 8 :51-52). This is a type question requiring an instrument, not an argument.

## CONCLUSION

The structure of the answer is a union of conditions whose internal order is a sequence per handoff and whose recurrence is a signed cycle. Two institutions over the same atoms are isomers; the four actor classes are a functional group, not a standing list; some parts are catalysts and some spent; description cost and action cost do not add. The one live strain is the fixed-atom condition on the isomer claim. No figure line gives the local arrangement or the sign of a particular cycle, so the isomer and cycle claims are structural possibilities with falsifiers, not readings of any actual case.
