# Isomers and order: what varies when category systems differ

**Mode: `specified` on the isomer claim, `general` on the structural distinction, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05` section 6), not a ground reading. Live strain S2 (shared-atom condition) is named rather than hidden.

## RESIDUAL

Two societies' category systems differ. Is the difference a change of *atoms* (a different mental structure), a change of *bonds* over the same atoms (different learned categories), or a change of *ground* with the same atoms and bonds (same mechanism, different local payoff)? The offered either/or does not distinguish these. The supporting derivation below does.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What is the atom and what is the bond?

- Fig 12 :5 *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- Operators :12-14: `+` conjunction, *"both hold at once"*, symmetric, *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."*
- Operators :30-32: `→` sequence, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, not symmetric.
- Therefore "two societies categorize differently" is underdescribed. If the ingredients are the same and the bond differs, the difference is a binding. If an ingredient itself differs, the difference is structural and an import is owed.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** produce a category system whose ingredients cannot be placed in the held atom set at any price, with provenance for the new atom and a shadow test showing which gap it explains. If that succeeds, the atom/bond distinction here is wrong.

### Q2 — Are different learned categories isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :98-99 gives the historical case: silver fulminate and silver cyanate, *"same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation"* (Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; named isomerism by Berzelius, 1830).
- Write two category systems S1 and S2 over the shared atom set (perceptual dimensions, similarity, taxonomy, thematic relations, prototype mechanism, labels and generics). Let S1 bind `+` mostly to taxonomic similarity and S2 bind `+` to material or thematic similarity, or let them partition the same colour space at different points. Same atoms, different bond arrangement, different behaviour in a sorting task. That is isomerism.
- Saalbach and Imai (2007, opened) report that *"the global structure of everyday object concepts is strikingly similar across different cultures and languages"* while classifier effects are *"miniscule compared with the importance of taxonomic and thematic relations for both language-culture groups."* This is the empirical shape of a shared atom set with local bond weights.
- **Status:** `specified`, triangulated across Fig 12 :117, Fig 13 :121 *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*, and the Saalbach/Imai result. **Strain S2 stays live:** the shared-atom set is supported but not proven for every domain.

**Falsifier:** two societies whose category systems share no atom at all (no common perceptual dimension, mechanism or transmission device), so the systems are incommensurable closures rather than isomers. Then the learned-category answer fails and the different-structures answer gains ground.

### Q3 — What is the order, and does it matter?

- Fig 12 :92 *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."* Under a symmetric bond, *"k operands have k! spellings and one term"* (:92-93). Under an ordered bond, different orders are different terms (Operators :61-62).
- A similarity grouping is symmetric: English "red" grouping two chips does not depend on which chip is presented first. A category-to-inference relation is ordered: membership `→` inference, not the reverse. A threshold `⋛` comparison between two category systems is ordered in the sense that the operands play different roles.
- Consequence: a cross-cultural experiment that measures only symmetric similarity cannot detect a difference in ordered bonds. It will report "same structure" when the inference structure may differ. This is a per-slot measurement requirement (Fig 1 :19), not a minor caveat.
- Fig 13 :81-83: *"relations / fields / gradients / flows / networks / and on"* with *"Each step acts on one kind and produces the other, so the colours alternate by construction rather than by choice."* Order in the *compositional* sense is fixed by the two kinds; order in the *bond* sense is what varies locally. Do not confuse them.

**Falsifier:** a domain where the order of operands in what was claimed to be a symmetric category judgement changes the judgement reliably under a non-updating anchor. Then the bond was misidentified and the symmetry analysis fails.

### Q4 — Convergent derivation versus adopted import

- Fig 8 :50-51 **"But shared atoms are not the signature. Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence."**
- Fig 8 :51-52 **"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them, and the surplus lives only where the closures differ."**
- Two societies arriving at similar colour foci independently is corroboration at maximum independence, not a shared evidence pool. Two societies using the same imported test battery is one frame wearing two names (Fig 8 :48-49 *"Two frames that overlap almost completely are one frame wearing two names."*).
- The ledger therefore records overlap per source. Kay and Regier (2003) and Abbott et al. (2016) share the World Color Survey pool; Giurfa et al. (2001) and Smith (2014) do not. Only the latter pair can corroborate the shared-kernel claim at maximum independence.

**Falsifier:** a provenance check showing that supposedly independent frames drew on the same stimulus set, the same observers or the same theory. Then the corroboration is withdrawn, not the verdict flipped by agreement.

### Q5 — What does the isomer mapping not do?

- It does not name which bond is used by which society. That is a binding, licensed by the ground in a named domain, not by the figure (Fig 9 :32 *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*).
- It does not produce a feature list. Fig 13 :115 *"The set is closed. The arrangements are not."*
- It does not make all differences benign. Fig 11 :49 *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* A binding that pays in one habitat can fail in another; the isomer claim is structural, not an evaluation.
- It does not remove the need for a discriminator. *The Hard Problem is About Source* (opened): *"An instrument that can't distinguish these cases demonstrates its own inadequacy."*

## CONCLUSION

A category system is a molecule: atoms from a shared set, joined by typed bonds. Societal differences are mostly different bond arrangements over the same atoms, so they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117, not different periodic tables. A genuinely different mental *structure* would be a different atom or bond grammar, which would need an import with provenance and a shadow test and is currently a `gap` (Fig 9 :32, Fig 13 :135). Order matters within the claim: symmetric similarity can hide ordered differences in inference, so a per-domain instrument is owed. The isomer mapping is checkable and has a stated falsifier; it is not a dressed-up restatement of "some differences are learned."
