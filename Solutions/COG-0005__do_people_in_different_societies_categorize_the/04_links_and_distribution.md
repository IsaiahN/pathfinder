## 2. Wider literature that actually bears on the question

All of the following were opened and are recorded with author, venue, year and quote in `10_source_ledger.csv`.

1. **Kay and Regier, 2003, PNAS** (*Resolving the question of color naming universals*, full text at PMC). The methodological anchor case: the original Berlin and Kay sample was skewed to 17 written, industrialised languages with often one speaker each. Their objective test over 110 unwritten languages found *"strong universal tendencies in color naming exist across both sorts of language."* Use this when explaining why a WEIRD baseline is not an anchor.
2. **Kay and Regier, 2006, Trends in Cognitive Sciences** (*Language, thought and color: recent developments*, full text extracted from the Berkeley PDF). The cleanest statement of the union: *"universal tendencies in color naming"* and, at the same time, *"naming differences across languages do cause differences in color cognition"*, summarised as *"nature proposes and nurture disposes."* This is the paper to offer to someone who wants the either/or dissolved rather than split down the middle.
3. **Abbott, Griffiths and Regier, 2016, PNAS** (*Focal colors across languages are representative members of color categories*, full text at PMC). One statistical model accounts for *"both universal tendencies and variation in focal colors across languages"*; the authors conclude that colour categorisation *"may be governed by principles that apply more broadly in cognition."* This is the strongest bridge from a specific learned domain to a shared mechanism.
4. **Gelman and Roberts, 2017, PNAS** (*How language shapes the cultural inheritance of categories*, full text at PMC). *"human language is a technology that enhances and expands the categorization capacities that we share with other animals."* Also: *"We are not born with a fixed set of categories."* Use for the shared-mechanism-plus-cultural-overlay claim.
5. **Saalbach and Imai, 2007, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General** (abstract and reproduction opened). *"the global structure of everyday object concepts is strikingly similar across different cultures and languages"*; classifier effects were *"miniscule compared with the importance of taxonomic and thematic relations."* Use for the isomer claim over a shared atom set.
6. **J. David Smith, 2014, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review** (full text at PMC). *"Diverse species share operating principles, default commitments, and processing weaknesses in categorization."* This is the low-overlap witness: comparative cognition, not cultural psychology.
7. **Giurfa, Zhang, Jenett, Menzel and Srinivasan, 2001, Nature** (abstract opened). Honeybees *"can form 'sameness' and 'difference' concepts"* and transfer the learned rule to new stimuli. A second low-overlap witness that abstract relational categorisation does not require a human cultural vocabulary.
8. **Imai and Masuda, 2012, chapter in Advances in Culture and Psychology** (full text read via search extraction, direct PDF fetch unsupported). *"the ontological distinction between objects and substances is universally shared, therefore, refuted the strong version of linguistic relativity"* while cross-linguistic differences remain. Marked as a reduced-weight read in the ledger.

**Overlap warning.** Sources 1, 2 and 3 share authors and the World Color Survey evidence pool; sources 5 and 8 share the Imai evidence pool. That is one witness per pool, not five. Sources 6 and 7 are genuinely low overlap and carry the corroboration for the shared-kernel half. No world source conflicts with the figure verdict, so the conflict rule was not triggered.
