# Expert verification packet: the Tether on "objectively misleading"

**What this is:** a claim in a form a misinformation researcher, pragmatist, or communication scholar can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `general` on the structural shape, `specified` on the operationalisation prediction. Not `grounded`.** No measurement is claimed to settle a conceptual point.

**Status: simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.** It cannot settle the question it reports on.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

Misleadingness is not a property carried by a message. It is a receiver-indexed relation over a union of two conditions: factual accuracy (objective, fact-checkable) and receiver uptake (context-bound). A message-only verdict fails the anchor test because it moves when the receiver or context changes. A true statement can mislead through arrangement alone, and a declared definition of "objectively misleading" is a convention until it declares the reference audience it uses. The Tether returns that shape with pre-registered refuters; it does not name a correct audience or threshold. "Objectively misleading" is coherent only after a reference audience and context are made explicit.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1 - Anchor test; checkable by reading Figure 2
- **Statement:** a property that changes when the receiver or context changes is not an anchor for "is this misleading"; the legitimate anchor is the receiver ranged against the facts, because the question is constitutively about people.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :29 - *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* Fig 2 :30 - *"If it moves when you push on it, it is not an anchor."* Fig 2 :36 - *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."* Fig 2 :37 - *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people."*
- **What to check:** is the premise true that the verdict moves with the receiver? Look for a case where a true claim misleads one audience and not another (the Chicago Tribune vaccine-headline case in the Nature Medicine review is one). If the verdict does not move, C1 loses its premise.
- **Refuter:** a message-only metric that is invariant under receiver and context changes and still tracks actual belief divergence or harm, without a hidden reference audience.

### C2 - Relation, not kind; checkable by reading Figure 11 and by cross-field comparison
- **Statement:** a message's misleading valence is relative to the receiver, not carried by the message; consequently the same tokens can be misleading or not depending on the residual.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 11 :49 - *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* Fig 11 :50 - *"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."* Fig 11 :51 - *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*
- **What to check:** in your own data, does the same content carry the same misleading verdict across audiences that share no evidence? If yes, the relation claim is weakened. Note that agreement from a shared evidence pool is one witness, not two (Fig 2 :40).
- **Refuter:** a demonstration that the valence is carried, invariant across residuals, so Fig 11 :49-51 fails.

### C3 - Union compound and arrangement; checkable by reading Figures 9 and 12
- **Statement:** accuracy and uptake are two conditions, not one; and the difference between false content and true-but-misleading content is a difference of arrangement, not of ingredients.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 9 :4 - *"First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."* Fig 12 :95-96 - *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*
- **What to check:** can a fact-check that records only a truth value distinguish a false claim from a true claim with a misleading implicature? Wayne Davis's SEP entry on implicature gives the parallel case: a speaker can mislead while making no false statement. If a truth-value record fully predicts uptake, C3 fails.
- **Refuter:** a single instrument that settles both conditions at once without fixing a reference audience, or a demonstration that falsity alone determines the verdict so the union collapses.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not that misleadingness is subjective or arbitrary. The accuracy condition is objective.
- Not that content-based measures are useless; they settle one condition of the compound.
- Not that the framework can name the right audience, threshold or "reasonable consumer". Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."*
- Not that a committee's declaration grounds a definition. Fig 10 :49: *"Declaring a meaning does not ground it."*
- Not that the field's definitional disagreement is independent corroboration. It is one evidence pool.
- Not `grounded`. Nothing here is asserted as settled by experiment.

## 5. What would settle it

A discriminating instrument, not agreement. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: a color reading *"cannot comment on the source"*, and it was the spectrometer that resolved it. Concretely:

1. **Invariance test:** a metric invariant under receiver and context changes that still tracks real belief divergence across independent audiences.
2. **Independence test:** identical verdicts across audiences sharing no evidence (Fig 2 :31).
3. **Discriminator test:** an instrument separating false content, misleading implicature, de-contextualisation and receiver-prior effects.
4. **Collapse test:** falsity alone determining the verdict.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 2 :29-30, :36-38, Fig 11 :49-51, Fig 9 :4, Fig 12 :95-96. Five short passages.
2. Name one case from your own data where a literally true message misled a specific audience. If you cannot, C1 and C2 lose their premise.
3. Say whether a truth-value-only fact-check predicts uptake in that case. If it does, C3 fails.
4. Name one instrument that would discriminate the sources of the misleading reading, or say why none is possible.
5. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
