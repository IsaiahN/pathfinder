# The Tether's answer: how snowpack change reaches river flow and water supply

**Mode: `specified` on the substantive shape, `general` on the structural constraints, not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a result. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §7), not a ground reading. The directional and numerical content comes from world sources and is labelled as such.

## 1. The residual it answers

Given a change in the cold-season snow store, what changes in the timing of river discharge, in the volume and reliability of water available in each season, and in which slot carries the live signal, and what measurement would settle each part? The figures do not contain snow or discharge data. What they constrain is the form of any acceptable answer.

## 2. The shape

1. **Report per basin and per season, never as an annual total.** Fig 1 :19 *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 11 :46 names *"conditions: light, water, temperature, season, terrain"*. An annual supply figure averages away the shift that matters.

2. **Treat size, timing and phase as three different changes.** Fig 13 :76 *"Energy and a gradient compose a flow. A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."* The snowpack is Fig 13 :46 *"a difference that can be spent"*. Resizing the store, moving the date it is spent, and replacing snow with rain are not the same operation.

3. **Do not bond the snow store directly to discharge.** Fig 13 :75 *"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."* A melt gradient and a routing network sit between store and gauge. Either can change independently, which is why snow and river regimes can decouple.

4. **Unbundle before answering.** The question is a union compound, not one hard problem. Fig 9 :54 *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Run one loop for the cryosphere store, one for routing, one for supply and demand, and keep the verdicts separate.

5. **The seasonal loop has a sign.** Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Melt recharges soil and groundwater and conditions the next season. Shifting precipitation from snow to rain can change the regime rather than just the numbers.

6. **Follow the cascade outward.** Fig 11 :44 *"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."* Second-order effects reach groundwater, vegetation, aquatic ecology, irrigation, hydropower and urban supply.

7. **Bind the anchor and watch the channel.** Fig 2 :29 *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* SWE, discharge and reservoir storage are anchors; models are frames. The instruments can fail while the ground does not.

## 3. What the world sources add (secondary, not derived here)

- Earlier snowmelt and earlier peak runoff, with lower summer and autumn flow where demand is highest (Barnett et al. 2005; IPCC AR6 WGII; Clow 2010; Jenicek et al. 2021). These agree from one observation pool and are recorded as one witness.
- The magnitude of snowpack change does not predict annual runoff change (López-Moreno et al. 2020), and prediction is hardest where multiple mechanisms interact (Gordon et al. 2022). These are low-overlap witnesses and are the strongest empirical checks on claims 2 and 3.
- The "less snow equals earlier streamflow" paradigm is actively contested (Berghuijs and Hale 2025), which is a reason to split by snow-fraction regime rather than average.

No world source contradicts the figure-derived shape. A conflict would send the derivation back to the domain census; it would not flip the verdict.

## 4. What it explicitly does not say

- Not a number. No days of advance, no percent of summer flow, no basin-specific forecast.
- Not a mechanism named by authority. Which structure dominates in a basin is a measurement.
- Not that annual runoff must fall when snowpack falls. The figures distinguish magnitude from timing, and the literature reports decoupling.
- Not that agreement among hydrologists settles anything. Fig 2 :40 *"agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"*.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement in this folder settles any claim.

## 5. What would settle it, and the honest stop

The ground, not a further argument. Per *The Hard Problem is About Source*, a gauge reading cannot say whether the water came from snow, rain or groundwater, so a source-discriminating instrument is owed. The specific tests are:

1. **Per-slot test:** does between-basin and between-season variance in timing response exceed within-slot noise? If not, claim 1 fails.
2. **Gradient test:** does store magnitude map proportionally to annual runoff? If so, claim 2 fails. Present evidence says no.
3. **Mediation test:** can SWE predict both volume and timing without a melt-gradient or network term? If so, claim 3 fails.
4. **Cycle test:** does a rain-dominated basin retain the former seasonal hydrograph and storage cycle? If so, claim 5 fails.
5. **Cascade test:** is supply reliability unchanged after a large timing shift with no compensating storage? If so, claim 6 fails.

**Honest stop: unreached, not unreachable.** Fig 8 :39 *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* Fig 13 :134 *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"* but absence of a settled number here is a statement about this derivation's budget and instruments, not about hydrology. The lineage is recorded so the stop is checkable.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not predict how many days earlier the spring peak will arrive, and it does not replace a gauge. It says that the snowpack is a stored difference that can be spent, and that the size of the store, the date it is spent and the phase of the precipitation are three different changes with different consequences. It says that snow and river discharge cannot be treated as directly connected: a melt gradient and a channel network sit between them, so snow and river regimes can decouple. It says the question is a bundle of overlapping conditions that must be split before it is searched, that the seasonal loop has a sign so a shift from snow to rain can change the regime rather than just the numbers, and that the effects reach soil, groundwater, ecology and water supply several steps out. It refuses to name which mechanism dominates in any basin, because it returns a shape, not a name. What it offers is that shape, five refuters written in advance, and the instruments that would close it. The numbers stay with the gauge record until the ground settles them.
