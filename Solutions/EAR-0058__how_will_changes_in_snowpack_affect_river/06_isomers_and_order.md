# Regimes as isomers, and the order of the seasonal chain

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7). Its falsifiers are written before its conclusion.

## RESIDUAL

Two supporting questions are owed after the main audit:

1. Are "snowmelt-dominated" and "rain-dominated" river regimes different substances under Fig 12's definition, or merely different labels on one substance?
2. What bond orders the snow-to-flow chain: sequence, production, or cycle, and what would make that reading fail?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: water as state, energy as state, time and space as states, with gradient, network and spectrum as structures (Fig 13). Bonds held: the seven, plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. Seasons are slots, not atoms.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Are the two regimes isomers?

Fig 12 :117 *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
Fig 12 :124 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
Fig 13 :76 *"A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."*

Both regimes are made of the same atoms: water, energy, a network to route through, and a gradient to spend. Under the weak reading (the hydrograph alone) they look like one substance described twice, which is Fig 12's *"difference is invisible in the formula"* case. Under the strong reading, the difference is a bond difference: in a snowmelt regime the gradient is bonded to a **state that is stored in space** (the winter snowpack at elevation), while in a rain regime the gradient is bonded to a **state that arrives in time** (the precipitation event). Fig 13 :76 says those are different terms because which state the gradient bonds to is part of what the term is.

Therefore:

- Same atoms, different bond, different behaviour -> **isomers** in the figure's sense.
- They also share a **functional group**: water plus a spendable gradient plus a routing network. That group fixes the kind (a river regime); the qualifier (which state the gradient bonds to) fixes the member (snowmelt or rain).

This is not a rhetorical analogy. The empirical case for a real difference is strong: López-Moreno et al. report *"a generalized decoupling of mountain river hydrology from headwater snowpack regimes,"* with regimes shifting *"from reflecting the seasonal snowmelt freshet to responding rapidly to winter and spring precipitation."* Different behaviour from a shared atom set is what an isomer claim predicts.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if both regimes can be written with the same bond to the same state (for example if snowmelt routing is shown to be a pure time-shift of rainfall routing with identical storage and source terms), the isomer claim fails and only the weaker functional-group claim survives.

### Q2 : Sequence or production?

Fig 12 :72 *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (sequence, `->`).
Fig 12 :77 *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (production, `=>`).

The winter snowpack and the summer discharge are not two events in a sequence. The liquid water that appears at the gauge in May did not exist as liquid in the snowpack in January. That is production, not sequence: `snow store => meltwater => discharge`. A pure sequence reading (`snow -> river`) would treat the water as already present and waiting, which is the flat-formula error Fig 12 names: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not."*

But the cycle leg is a sequence in one respect: melt must follow accumulation, and the next season's soil moisture depends on this season's melt. Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* So the full object is a production chain embedded in a cycle, which is why a plain arrow chain cannot represent it.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the same water can be shown to persist as liquid through the cold season with no phase transition, so the discharge water existed before melt, the production reading fails and the sequence reading stands.

### Q3 : What does settlement buy, and what does it cost?

Fig 12 :104 *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."*

A seasonal regime once settled against gauges (decades of hydrograph records) becomes an atom for the next level: water-supply planning, reservoir operations and irrigation allocation compose over it. Its irreducibility is a fact about that level, not about the world (Fig 12 :94 *"The arrangement is the substance."*). This is why a water manager can treat "the snowmelt freshet" as a primitive without claiming it is physically primitive.

Settlement is necessary but not sufficient for composition, per the Fig 5 amendment: a regime can be well measured and still not compose into a supply model if the routing or demand term is missing. This is the dependency break of Fig 3 :19 *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a settled regime cannot serve as an operand for supply planning, or if a supply model composed over it fails only because of forecasting skill rather than a missing term, the level claim needs re-census.

### Q4 : Order and symmetry check

Fig 12 :92 *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."* Production (`=>`) and sequence (`->`) are ordered bonds: swapping operands changes the term. Snow `=>` melt is not melt `=>` snow. The seasonal cycle closes only because the output re-enters as input at the next turn, and the residual of each turn is what the next turn composes against (Fig 13 :88 *"The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no two passes begin from the same place."*). Therefore no two seasons begin from the same snow state, and a model that reuses one seasonal template unchanged is treating a cycle as a replay.

## CONCLUSION

The two regimes are best read as **isomers over a shared functional group**: same water, energy, network and gradient atoms, different bond (gradient to a stored spatial state versus gradient to an arriving temporal state), different hydrograph behaviour. The production chain `snow store => meltwater => discharge` is embedded in a seasonal cycle, not a plain sequence. Each settled regime becomes an atom for supply planning, but settlement alone does not guarantee composition. All three claims are `specified`, with falsifiers written above. The regime distinction is corroborated, not derived, by López-Moreno et al. 2020 and by the IPCC assessment that seasonal river flows are affected by shorter snow seasons and earlier, less rapid melt.
