# Union, order and analogues: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The word "prioritize" presupposes an ordering over four items. Two questions are owed: (1) is the relation among the four objectives a partition (a list of separate levers) or a union (overlapping conditions with a compound residue), and (2) if it is an order, is it an order of importance or an order of dependency? A third question follows: are the four objectives isomers of one another under Fig 12's definition, or members of a functional group?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Census from the residual line alone: actors are the model Γ, the reader, the target system and the decision maker; currency is residual mass for D1, description bits for D2, invariant composition for D3, and actions for D4; anchors are the measurement, the person, the discriminating instrument and the outcome. Atoms held: the four objectives and their instruments. Bonds held: the sequence (→) from perception to planning, the comparison (⋛) that sets a threshold for "good enough," the subtraction (−) of progress, and the negation (¬) that refuses a priority claim that fails. No new primitive is needed.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :54: *"when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*
- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- The four objectives overlap on known cases: accuracy and intervention overlap when a prediction changes its own environment; interpretability and mechanism overlap when an interpretable structure is also causal; interpretability and intervention overlap when an explanation must be contestable.
- ⇒ The correct structure is a **union**. A single lever clears the singly-bound cases and leaves the intersections (Fig 9 :56). Each intersection is a compound with its own loop, not a harder case of either condition (Fig 9 :57).
- **Falsifier:** exhibit four evaluation procedures whose binding sets are disjoint in every deployment tested, so a single lever clears the whole space. That would make it a partition and remove the need to unbundle.

### Q2 - Is the order importance or dependency?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 3 :22-23: a step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed; a reading below the break is a reading of nothing.
- The dependency chain for modeling is: perception (what can be represented) → vocabulary (terms for what the situation affords) → objective (what counts as progress) → planning (what action to take) → learn and carry. D2 (representation and readability) feeds the objective; D1 (measurement) ranges the objective; D4 (intervention) consumes it; D3 (generator) is what the whole chain ranges against.
- ⇒ "Priority" is a **dependency order with per-slot exceptions**, not an importance ranking. Work on the lowest unreached link first; effort above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it.
- **Falsifier:** an importance-ranked scheme that rescues a broken upstream link by downstream effort alone, measured across several deployments.

### Q3 - Are the four objectives isomers or a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- Written as bare words, the four objectives share no atoms in the figure sense: they are not isomers, because each has different atoms (a measurement, a reader, a generator, an outcome). They are four members of a **functional group**: each is a "range R against an anchor" substructure with a different anchor in the slot. The group says what kind (an anchor relation); the qualifier says which member (measurement, person, instrument, outcome).
- The stronger isomer claim: if a single deployment is written over the full shared atom set {model, action, observation, reader, generator, habitat}, with the four objectives differing only in which anchor is bonded to which slot, then they **are** isomers in Fig 12's sense. That is the version worth testing, because it predicts that two models with the same ingredients but different anchor bonds will behave differently under deployment.
- **Falsifier:** if one objective can be written without an atom the others require (for example a "usefulness" claim with no habitat, or a "mechanism" claim with no intervention), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 - What about the two currencies?

- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."*
- Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."*
- Fig 12 :129: *"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."*
- Interpretability and (partly) mechanistic realism are priced in bits. Usefulness for intervention is priced in actions. Predictive accuracy is a third reading: residual mass against a ground. Three readings, not one score.
- The analogue from statistics: Box's *"all models are wrong, but some are useful"* separates correctness from utility, and JMLR's author guide requires that claims be supported by experiments or theory and that experiments be reproducible. Those are two different obligations, not a weighted sum.
- **Falsifier:** a demonstrated common unit in which description cost and test cost are commensurable without loss, on a case where each currently predicts the other's failure.

### Q5 - Analogues that clarify the order

- **Tolman's cognitive map** (opened via the Wikipedia entry): rats in a cross maze learn a representation that lets them take the correct route from a new starting point, not a fixed turn. That is the difference between an interpretable list (a fixed turn) and a model useful for intervention (a route). Bennett's critique that simpler alternatives cannot be ruled out is the mechanism-discrimination analogue: a behavior reading is not a source claim until the design distinguishes them.
- **Box's map/territory maxim**: usefulness is a relation between a model and a purpose, not a property of the model. This is Fig 11 :39 in statistics vocabulary.
- **The spectrometer** from *The Hard Problem is About Source*: the instrument, not the argument, distinguished sources. This is the F8 gap in concrete form.
- **The DARPA accuracy/interpretability curve** that Rudin (2019) calls fictional: a smooth tradeoff chart with unquantified axes is the flat-formula error. It records two words and no bond.

### Q6 - Where does the union compound come from?

- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- The compound case is a deployment where the model predicts accurately, is readable, has a claimed mechanism, and will be acted on, and where the four disagree. That case is not "accuracy plus interpretability plus mechanism plus usefulness." It is a new object: a model in a habitat with a contestable decision. It needs its own loop, its own anchor and its own refuter. This is the center that `07_the_tether_answer.md` leaves open.

## CONCLUSION

The four objectives form a union, ordered by dependency and not by importance. As bare words they are a functional group (an anchor relation with a different anchor in the slot); written over a full shared atom set they are a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier. The two currencies must be read apart. The compound case where all four bind and disagree is categorically different and needs its own loop. The shape that survives is the four-row assignment, not a ranking.
