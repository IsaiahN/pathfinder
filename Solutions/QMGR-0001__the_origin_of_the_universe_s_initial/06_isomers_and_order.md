# Are the two proposals isomers, and what is the order of the composition?

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6). The physics-facing claims are unmeasured and are labelled as such.

## RESIDUAL

The record treats Hartle-Hawking and Vilenkin as rival answers to one question. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. Are they **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or merely two members of a functional group, or two genuinely different frames?
2. What is the **order** of the composition: does a boundary condition sit inside the dynamics, over it, or beside it?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (before choosing a figure line)

- **R.** Two named arrangements over one formalism. Arity: two. Symmetry: no boundary versus outgoing-wave boundary are not related by a symmetry of the formalism; they differ in which histories are admitted. Scale: first order inside the formalism, second order at the comparison of the two.
- **D1, the atoms held.** A gravitational action, a wave function of the universe, minisuperspace variables (scale factor and matter fields), a path integral or canonical constraint, a semiclassical approximation. Currency: solutions and their weights. Anchor: mathematical consistency, not the ground.
- **D2, the arrangements.** HH sums over compact Euclidean geometries with no past boundary and regular matter configurations; V imposes an outgoing-wave condition at nucleation. Currency: probability weights over histories. Anchor: none nameable. Both are inside D1's closure.
- **D3, the comparison.** The analyst's act of setting one against the other. Currency: structural similarity and divergent predictions. Anchor: an instrument that discriminates, which is absent. In this simulated run D3 draws on the same evidence pool as D1 and D2, so its independence is zero.

Order by dependency: settle the atoms before the arrangement (Fig 3 :19); describe the residual before naming a frame (Fig 9 :27-28); a settled molecule becomes an atom before the next composition (Fig 12 :104).

## DERIVATION

### Q2. Are they isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- HH and V share the atom set `{action, wave function, minisuperspace variables, path integral or constraint, semiclassical approximation}`. They differ in the boundary bond: no past boundary versus outgoing wave at nucleation. The difference is not in the ingredient list; it is in which histories are admitted. That is the bond carrying information the operands do not.
- Under the strong reading (both proposals over the full shared atom set), they are **isomers**: same atoms, different boundary bond, different predicted weights.
- Under the weak reading (compare only the headline rules "no boundary" and "outgoing wave"), they share almost nothing explicitly and look like a **functional group** in Fig 12 :123 terms: **"A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** The kind is "boundary condition"; the members are HH and V.
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."** So the strong reading is the one worth testing: if the arrangements differ, the theories differ even though the ingredient list is identical.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one proposal requires an atom the other does not (for example, one depends on a landscape of vacua and the other does not, in a way that changes its closure), the isomer claim fails and the two become genuinely different frames. The functional-group reading then stands instead. A second falsifier: if both proposals yield exactly the same probability weights over the same history space with no structural remainder, then the difference is not a bond that carries information and the isomer claim fails the price test of Fig 12 :99-101.

### Q3. What is the order of the composition?

- Fig 12 :103: **"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."**
- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom."**
- The formalism (dynamics) is settled against the ground in its own domain. It can therefore serve as an atom for the next composition. The boundary condition is composed **over** the settled dynamics, not inside it and not beside it. It is second order to the dynamics.
- Fig 13 :121: **"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."** HH and V are two routes from the same settled dynamics to different substances, which is why they are isomers rather than two names for one thing.
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** Refining the path-integral contour or the semiclassical approximation (a link that already works) cannot rescue the missing link, which is the discriminator. Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- The dependency order is therefore: formalism first, boundary condition second, comparison third, discriminator fourth. Asking which proposal is right before stating what the boundary condition owes is the Fig 3 inversion.

### Q4. What about a "first cause" at the end of the chain?

- Fig 7 :6: **"the limit: no last room."** Fig 7 :31: **"no finite number of them ever arrives."** Fig 13 :90: **"One chain is one route through a space that has no last route."**
- A boundary condition is a handoff in a schedule. It closes one residual and surfaces the next. Claiming it ends the regress is a terminal-room claim and is refused.
- This does not refuse the search. Fig 7 :33-34 distinguishes *"one long room: motion, no progress"* from *"the truth is where the schedule is heading."* The schedule of boundary-condition refinements still travels.
- **Falsifier:** a demonstrated frame with no outside left to import from, or a derivation of one of the six substrate terms from the other five. Either would permit a last room and break this conclusion.

## CONCLUSION

The two cores are **isomers over a shared atom set**, in the strong sense of Fig 12 :117, provided the full formalism is counted as the atom set. As headline rules alone they form a functional group (Fig 12 :123), which is the weaker claim. The boundary condition is **second order**: it composes over the dynamics that has already settled, under Fig 12 :104. The comparison of the two proposals is third order, and the missing discriminator is a link with no instrument, not a missing argument.

This framing keeps what is useful (a structural relation, a dependency order, a stated falsifier) without importing a seventh primitive or declaring a terminal room. Which boundary condition nature uses remains a physics question for a ground reading. The `metric tensor equals Gradient` style of binding, and its analogues here, stay unproven.
