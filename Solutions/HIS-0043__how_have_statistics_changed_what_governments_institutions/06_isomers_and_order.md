# Order and isomerism in statistical categories

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the bond rules, `gap` on the counterfactual list. Not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The historical literature names many categories (poverty, unemployment, normality, crime rate, suicide rate, the average man). Two structural questions are owed: (1) what order binds enumeration to policy, and (2) are these categories **isomers** over a fixed atom set, or merely members of a **functional group**? If they are isomers, type rather than degree is the variable; if they are a functional group, the differentiating qualifier decides which member is present.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: states (space, time, energy) and structures (spectrum, gradient, network) from Fig 13, plus the bond set from Fig 12 and the Operators table, notably `⋛` comparison and `→` sequence. Bonds held: seven, plus negation. No new primitive is available without import.

The categories named by the world literature are **bindings** from the history-of-statistics frame onto these atoms, not new atoms. A rate is an ordering over a state (spectrum bonded to time or population); a poverty line is a comparison (threshold) over that ordering; a registry is a network; the "average man" is a settled composition reused as an atom (Fig 12 :104).

## DERIVATION

### Q1. What order does the chain have?

- Fig 12 :30 defines `→`: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened."* The enumeration to policy chain is ordered, not symmetric.
- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
- The chain reads: **registry (perception) → rate and code (vocabulary) → what counts as progress (objective) → policy (planning) → revised code (learn and carry).**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** The objective link is the one that most often lacks an instrument: a falling rate is not the same as a falling condition.
- Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* A vocabulary of counts alone can name a population and cannot name a route from a reading to a policy. The comparison bond is what makes a goal stateable: Operators table, *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one."*
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a policy route can be derived from a count without any comparison (no threshold, no target, no direction), the ordered chain is wrong and the `→` is a free bond, not a priced one.

**Verdict:** the chain is ordered per Fig 3 and bonded per Fig 12. Mode `general` for the ordering rule, `specified` for this chain. Triangulated (Fig 3 and Fig 12, low overlap).

### Q2. Are the statistical categories isomers over a fixed atom set?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Consider two readings over the same atoms `{population, time, ordering, threshold}`:
  - `rate → ⋛ threshold`: unemployment as a percentage above a defined cutoff.
  - `rate → ⋛ normal curve`: abnormality as deviation from a distributional centre.
- Both use the same atoms; the bond target differs (a fixed cutoff versus a distributional centre). Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, and the difference is decisive in behaviour: one attaches benefits by a rule, the other attaches a norm by a curve. Grue and Heiberg (2006, opened 2026-11-23) record the historical consequence of just this difference: *"normality, norm, etc. entered the European languages more or less coincidentally with Adolphe Quetelet's introduction of the concept of the average man."*
- The weaker claim: if the two readings do not share the full atom set (for example, one requires a registry of individuals and the other only an aggregate curve), they are members of the same **functional group** (a threshold over an ordering), distinguished by the qualifier. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either reading can be written without an atom the other requires, and the behavioural difference disappears when the atoms are matched, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group claim stands.

**Verdict:** the strong isomer reading is checkable but unmeasured here; the functional-group reading is the safer `specified` claim. Mode `specified`. Triangulated for the figure definition, single-source for the historical binding.

### Q3. What does the average man do to the atom set?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- The "average man" was a settled composition (many measurements, one centre) that then served as an atom for further composition (normality, deviation, eugenic classification, BMI). That is the recursion Fig 12 names, not a new primitive.
- Fig 13 :43-44 removes the continuum from the generating set: *"A continuum was the seventh and was removed for failing exactly this: it is a spectrum without gaps, or smoothness over space and time. A generating set must not contain what its own members produce."* Normality and the average are likewise compositions, not members of the six. My earlier reading that they might be primitives is retracted in `01` section 6 and kept visible here.
- **Falsifier:** derive the ordering structure from the other five members of the six-term set, or show that normality is indivisible into ordering plus population. Either breaks this reading.

**Verdict:** `general` for the settled-molecule rule, `specified` for the historical case. Triangulated (Fig 12 and Fig 13).

### Q4. What is the union order of D1, D2 and D3?

- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- The union has a dependency order, not a flat conjunction: D1 (instrument) must register something before D2 (category) can bind it, and D2 must bind it before D3 (attention) can allocate a budget. But each also feeds back: D3 funds D1. So it is a cycle with an ordered spine, not a chain and not a partition. Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*
- The sign of the loop is the question F4 and F6 ask: a negative loop to an independent outcome anchor is a maintained channel; a positive loop through public regard is the mirror chain of Fig 7.
- **Falsifier:** show that the three conditions form a partition with no shared case, or that the feedback from D3 to D1 is absent in a documented regime.

**Verdict:** `specified`, single-source for the cycle reading (Fig 12 plus Fig 9), with the sign left as an instrument question.

## CONCLUSION

The enumeration to policy chain is ordered by dependency and priced by bonds; it is not a flat list of causes. The statistical categories named in the history are isomers over a shared atom set in the strong reading, and members of a threshold-over-ordering functional group in the weaker reading; the strong reading is checkable but unmeasured here. The average man is a settled composition promoted to an atom, not a seventh primitive. D1 to D3 form a union with a cycle, and the sign of that cycle is exactly what distinguishes a maintained channel from a mirror chain. All four claims carry their falsifiers above.
