# Are the four conditions isomers? Structure, order, and the cycle analogue

**Mode: `specified` on the structure claims, `general` on the composition rule.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

"Jointly" leaves three questions unanswered: (1) what is the **structure** of the four-condition compound, (2) what is the **dependency order** among them, and (3) are the four conditions **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or merely members of a functional group? These are structural questions, decidable from the figures before any coefficient is measured.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in a social-contagion census: persons, places, physical contacts, information, rules. Bonds available: the seven (conjunction, disjunction, identity, sequence, production, subtraction, comparison) plus negation. No new primitive is imported. The four named conditions are **arrangements** of these atoms, not new atoms: mobility rearranges persons across places; household structure fixes persistent contact cliques; institutions add or remove edges by rule; online connections carry information between persons.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What structure does the compound have?

- Fig 12 :116: "Four structures a compositional system exhibits, and each names something the flat form cannot write."
- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- A flat list of four coefficients records ingredients and not arrangement. By Fig 12's opening line, "a list of ingredients is not a molecule": the bond is the rest of the information and "the half nothing was recording."
- The compound is therefore not four independent terms added together. It is a bonded arrangement over a shared atom set. At minimum the bond between the information layer and the physical contact layer is a **production/sequence** bond: information changes behavior, behavior changes contact, contact changes infection.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- Ordered links for this question: perceive the residual per slot -> enumerate the habitat -> pin the ground -> identify the couplings -> fit coefficients.
- The middle link (having terms for what a situation affords and saying what counts as progress) is where chains usually break (Fig 3 :24-27). Here the middle link is the information-to-behavior bridge: it has no clean instrument in many datasets, so it attracts the least attention. A model that fits transmission coefficients (above the break) while omitting that bridge produces "a reading of nothing" (Fig 3 :22-23).
- Import: dependency order explains why the four conditions cannot be ranked by "importance." Ranking is a budget filter, not a witness (Fig 9 :60).

### Q3 - Are they isomers?

- Written over the full shared atom set `{persons, places, physical contacts, information, rules}`, the four conditions differ only in which structure mediates which pair. They are then **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance (Fig 12 :117). The difference is invisible in a coefficient table and decisive in behaviour.
- Written as disjoint blocks (mobility has places, household has contacts, online has information, institutions has rules, with no shared atoms), they are not isomers. They are members of one **functional group**, namely "conditions that alter contact" (Fig 12 :124): the group says what kind, the qualifier says which member.
- The stronger isomer claim is the one worth testing, because it predicts that rearranging the same atoms changes the outcome class without adding a new condition.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one condition can be written without an atom that another requires and still produces the same outcome class (for example online information producing the same contact change with no information atom, or institutions acting with no rules atom), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q4 - Cycle versus chain: the decisive structural analogue

- Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."
- Chain form: online information -> behavior -> contact -> infection. It cannot close, so rising cases cannot generate new information. That is a wreath of the real system.
- Cycle form: cases -> information -> behavior -> contact -> cases. It closes. The sign decides whether it damps (awareness reduces contact) or amplifies (fear increases mobility and contact). World sources make the same structural point: Wang et al. (2015) call it a "coupled, nonlinear system"; Granell et al. (2013) describe a "cyclic information awareness process" on a virtual layer.
- Fig 12 :119 also names the **catalyst**: "A part that survives its own use." Institutions and information are catalyst-like: they persist across uses and can be spent and regenerated, so treating them as one-shot interventions misprices them. Nothing in a flat coefficient list records which part is catalytic.

## CONCLUSION

The four conditions are arrangements of one shared atom set, not four new atoms. Over that set they are a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier; split into disjoint blocks they collapse to a functional group. Their dependency order runs from slot-wise perception through habitat enumeration and ground pinning to couplings and coefficients, with the information-to-behavior bridge as the fragile middle link. The decisive structural analogue is the **cycle**, not the chain: cases feed information, which feeds behavior and contact, which feed cases. This rescues the structural content of "jointly" without importing a seventh atom or treating a coefficient table as an explanation.
