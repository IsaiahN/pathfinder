# The Tether's answer on randomness, computation and prediction

**Mode: `specified` on the four domain verdicts, `general` on the shape and on the ontic stop. Not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §7), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

"Randomness and nondeterminism" is a union of two conditions with different anchors (Fig 9 :54-57). One asks what a machine can compute, anchored on proof. The other asks what a frame can predict, anchored on the ground. A verification sub-question asks what randomness buys against an adversary, and a fourth question asks whether the randomness is ontic. Verdicts are kept separate and never averaged.

## 2. The shape

1. **Randomness and branching do not add a kind, so they do not move the computability wall (D1).** Fig 13 :115: "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists." Fig 6 :21: "MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom." A random choice selects among arrangements; a branch enumerates them. Neither produces a seventh member. Fig 13 :118 gives the limit: "infinite effort inside a finite reach."
2. **They change coverage, cost and independence, not the reach (D1 and D3).** Fig 6 :49: "more search at the same reach: is it in here and unfound? The closure does not change. Same atoms, same wall, more of the space actually visited." Fig 12 :125-126: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." Randomness spends actions to save bits. It only buys more search when the searchers are independent; "searchers drawing on the same evidence are one searcher wearing many names" (Fig 6 :53-54). In verification it buys unpredictability for the verifier: "Zero-knowledge proof systems, where the verifier's non-access to the witness is what makes the verification sound rather than a limitation it tolerates" (Fig 10 :84).
3. **In prediction, randomness is the residual, not a primitive (D2).** Fig 13 :28: "They alternate, and the alternation is where both the recursion and the randomness come from." Fig 13 :31-32: "a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos, and the detail a crossing discards reappears above it as noise." Chaos and noise are isomers over the same atoms under different bonds (Fig 12 :117). So long-horizon unpredictability can arise in a fully deterministic system: the present determines the future, but "the approximate present does not approximately determine the future" (Lorenz, via the chaos source read 2026-11-23).
4. **The round-trip gap is the honest report of what prediction loses (D2).** Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked." The gap is "not an error to eliminate" (Fig 4 :31). Depth and genuine absence look the same from inside (Fig 6 :27), so a failure to predict is never by itself a proof of a new kind.
5. **There is a hard limit on certifying randomness from inside a frame (D1).** Fig 8 :37: "A frame cannot certify its own limit. Chaitin: beyond a fixed constant, no system proves that any object exceeds its own complexity." And: "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence" (Fig 8 :39). The 2026 CCC paper on nondeterministic Kolmogorov complexity read this session sits exactly at this boundary.
6. **Whether the randomness is ontic is a gap, and the framework says so (D4).** Fig 13 :143-144: "genuine indeterminacy is the open case, unresolved." Fig 13 :134: "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence." The response is `gap`, not agnostic ornament and not a guess.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not a determinism thesis. The framework does not decide whether the world is indeterministic; it records the question as open.
- Not "randomness never helps." It helps as a resource, as search independence and as verifier unpredictability. It helps in cost and coverage, not in kind.
- Not P equals BPP, and not P does not equal BPP. That is a bounded-resource question in a different domain; the world literature calls it open.
- Not a claim that Chaitin's theorem proves randomness does not exist. It limits proof of complexity inside a fixed system.
- Not a derivation of one domain from another. D1, D2, D3 and D4 have different anchors and stay separate.
- Not `grounded`. No computation, measurement or proof in this folder settles any of the four verdicts.

## 4. What would settle it - the ground

1. A machine whose randomized or nondeterministic version computes a function outside the deterministic compositional reach, or a derivation of a seventh kind. That would break verdict 1.
2. A randomness in computation that is neither an amplified remainder nor discarded detail at a crossing, or a demonstration that correlated searchers add reach as fast as independent ones. That would break verdicts 2 and 3.
3. An instrument that discriminates ontic from epistemic randomness at the source, the analogue of the spectrometer in *The Hard Problem is About Source*. That would close verdict 6 and move D4 from `gap` to a real domain.
4. A proof inside a fixed system that an arbitrary string exceeds its complexity beyond the constant. That would break verdict 5.

## 5. The honest stop

Reached in D1 to D3, unreached in D4. The six-term set is "a human's careful guess at reality's compression axes"; declaring it makes the choice visible, not neutral. The answer stops at the limit of what the available instruments can distinguish and records the lineage so the stop is checkable. "Indiscernibility is not identity." A source that has not been opened is not a witness, and a room of agreeing readers is not an anchor.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The short answer is that randomness and nondeterminism change different things in the two halves of the question. For computation they do not add a new kind to what a system can compose, so the wall of the composable reach does not move; they change how much of that reach is searched, what the search costs, and whether independent searchers buy anything. For prediction, randomness is not a primitive at all: it is the remainder a turn leaves behind. A remainder amplified through a chain is chaos, and the detail a crossing discards reappears as noise, so fully deterministic systems can still be unpredictable beyond a finite horizon. There is a formal limit too: a frame cannot prove, beyond a fixed constant, that an object exceeds its own complexity. The framework stops at the question of whether the randomness is ontic and reports it as open, because the instrument that would discriminate ontic from epistemic randomness is not in hand. What is offered is that shape, four refuters written in advance, and the names of the instruments that would close it.
