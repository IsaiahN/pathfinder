# Isomers and order: the structure of the two-effect answer

## RESIDUAL

The audit split the question into a computability half and a prediction half, with a verification sub-domain and an ontic gap. Four questions remain: is the split a union or a partition, what bond carries randomness, are chaos and noise isomers, and in what order do the loops run. Each is answered below with its own falsifier.

Bonds held: symmetric `+`, `∥`, `≡`; ordered `→`, `⇒`, `−`, `⋛`; plus `¬`. No new primitive is available without an import, and an import adds no atom to the world (Fig 6 :25).

### Q1 - Union or partition?

Fig 9 :54-57: "when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again." And: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

Computability and prediction are **two conditions**, not two severable problems: a randomized algorithm can be perfectly computable and perfectly unpredictable to an observer; a chaotic trajectory can be fully deterministic and uncomputable in closed form only at the level of the question asked. The intersection is real, so this is a **union compound** with its own loop, not a partition. Inside the prediction half, chaos and noise are themselves a union of two mechanisms (Fig 13 :31-32).

**Falsifier:** show that every instance of "computable but unpredictable" is also an instance of "uncomputable" or of "predictable", that is, that the intersection is empty. If that holds, the union collapses to a partition and this derivation is withdrawn.

### Q2 - What bond carries randomness?

The Operators table defines negation as unary: "Negation joins nothing, so its symmetry entry is empty rather than no." A refutation "is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."

So randomness is not best written as a new component joined to a system by a binary bond. In the prediction domain it is the **refusal of a prediction**: `¬(predictable at horizon h)`. That is a unary operation on a claim the frame holds, not a seventh atom and not a relation between two operands. This is why F1 and F3 are consistent: no new kind is added, and the reach falls (or the prediction fails) because something was added to the record, namely the refusal.

In the computation domain the relevant operator is different: a random branch is a **distributed search** over arrangements, governed by the independence rule (Fig 6 :50, "Distribute the search, and keep the searchers independent"). Confusing the two operators is the source of the usual bad argument that randomness "adds a dimension"; it adds a refusal in one domain and a search strategy in another.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a binary bond whose operands are a system and a random source and that produces a new kind not in the six, with a test that separates it from `¬` and from distributed search. If none can be stated, the unary-plus-search reading stands.

### Q3 - Are chaos and noise isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Both mechanisms are composed from the same atoms: a remainder left by a turn, a crossing between kinds, and the six-term substrate. They differ in the bond applied:

- **Chaos:** the remainder re-enters an amplifying chain. The operative bond is ordered sequence, `→`, with a positive feedback sign (Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes").
- **Noise:** the detail a crossing discards reappears above it. The operative operation is subtraction or a lossy transform, `−` and `T_A`, where "The transform between scales loses information, and that is the point" (Fig 4 :27).

Same atoms, different bond, different predictive consequence. So at the level of a turn, chaos and noise are a real isomer claim. Their difference is invisible in a bare formula that records only "randomness" and decisive in what a forecaster can do: a chaos horizon can be pushed by better state measurement, while discarded detail cannot be recovered from the artefact without the lineage.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if chaos can be derived without a crossing, or noise without a remainder, or if both reduce to the same bond under the six tests of Fig 12, the isomer claim fails and the weaker reading (two names for one residual effect) stands.

### Q4 - What order do the loops run in?

Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."

1. Settle the closed reach (D1). Without it, "randomness adds power" cannot be separated from "randomness visits more of the same space."
2. Read the residual across a crossing (D2). Without step 1, chaos gets mistaken for a new atom.
3. Price the search and buy independence (D3). Without step 2, more branches look like more evidence.
4. Face the ontic gap (D4). It needs an instrument no earlier step supplies.

A reading taken below the break is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23). Attempting step 4 first is how the question usually gets a bad answer: people pick a metaphysical position on randomness before saying which quantity it moves.

**Falsifier:** show that the ontic question can be settled by a proof about the compositional reach, that is, that step 4 depends on no instrument beyond step 1. Fig 8 :39 would then be wrong for this case.

### Q5 - Why the two currencies cannot be added

Fig 12 :125-126: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add. Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."

A Monte Carlo method pays actions to buy an approximate answer where a closed-form derivation would pay bits; a derandomization pays bits to avoid actions. These are two readings held apart. Summing them into one "efficiency of randomness" figure is the named error, not the fix. The world source *Randomized algorithm* agrees at its own level: "Randomness can be viewed as a resource, like space and time." Space and time are two different states in the six-term set, not two readings of one quantity (Fig 13 :35-36), so treating them as a single budget is a type error one level down.

**Falsifier:** produce a single unit in which description bits and settlement actions add without a declared conversion, and show that the conversion settles a prediction the two separate readings cannot. Until then, the two readings stay apart.

## CONCLUSION

The computed answer is a union of two loops with a verification sub-loop and one honest gap. Randomness is carried by unary refusal in prediction and by distributed, independence-gated search in computation. Chaos and noise are isomers over the same remainder-and-crossing atoms under different bonds. The loops run in dependency order, and bits and actions are kept as two currencies. Each of the four parts has a falsifier above.
