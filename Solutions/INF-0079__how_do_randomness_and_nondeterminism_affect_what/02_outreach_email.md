**Subject:** One checkable claim about randomness, computation and prediction

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The residual, in effect terms (`specified`, not `grounded`):** "Randomness and nondeterminism" bundles two different questions. One is about what a machine can compute, which has proof as its anchor. The other is about what a frame can predict, which has the ground as its anchor. They do not share a ground, so one answer cannot settle both.
>
> **The output I would like checked:** randomness and branching do not add a new kind to what a system can compose. They reorganise a fixed reach: they change how much of it is searched, what the search costs, and how independent the searchers must be. In prediction, randomness is not a primitive. It is what a turn leaves behind: an amplified remainder is chaos, and the detail a crossing discards reappears above it as noise. Whether that loss is ontic or epistemic is explicitly open in the framework, and I report it as open rather than guessing.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that randomized algorithms never beat deterministic ones under bounded resources, that P equals BPP, that the world is deterministic, or that a proof of incompressibility proves anything about existence. Those are different domains and I have kept them separate.
>
> **What would refute the output:**
> 1. a randomized or nondeterministic machine computes a function outside the deterministic compositional reach, or a seventh kind is derived by rearranging the six;
> 2. correlated searchers buy as much reach as independent ones, or sampling moves the wall;
> 3. a randomness in computation is found that is neither an amplified remainder nor discarded detail at a crossing;
> 4. a ground reading settles ontic versus epistemic randomness, which would reverse the open slot.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, the corresponding part is withdrawn. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
