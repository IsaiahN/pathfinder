# Redundancy, isomers and order of enumeration in ECO-0045

## RESIDUAL

The question asks whether services can be maintained while species are lost. Three supporting
questions are owed: (1) when are two species interchangeable for a service, (2) in what order must a
service be assessed, and (3) what is the structure of a bundle of services. The census that answers
them is already in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §1 and is not repeated here.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Interchangeable species: functional group or isomer?

- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says
  what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible
  in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance."

**Reading.** If two species occupy the same substructure under the same bond, they are members of one
functional group: swapping one for another changes the qualifier, not the kind, and the service is
preserved. If the atoms are the same but the bond differs (pollinator versus seed predator,
decomposer versus pathogen), they are isomers: the formula listing the species looks unchanged, but
the substance, that is the service, is different. Fig 13 allows different routes to arrive at the same
substance, so redundancy is real in that case. **Mode `specified`. Triangulated** across Fig 12 and
Fig 13, which have low overlap. **Falsifier:** two arrangements with identical functional groups and
identical bonds but different measured service flows; or a service preserved after the removal of an
entire functional group with its bond intact.

### Q2 — What is the order of assessment?

- Fig 11 :43: "The habitat is enumerated, never composed."
- Fig 11 :44: "List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and
  outward until the cascade stops mattering."
- Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"
- Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the
  one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

**Reading.** The order is: describe the per-service residual, enumerate actors, conditions and
relations, extend to the cascade, then measure. Counting species first is effort above the break;
it arrives at a step that cannot use it. A link without an instrument is not a link that works (Fig 3
:27). **Mode `general` for the ordering, `specified` for its application here. Triangulated** across
Fig 11, Fig 9 and Fig 3. **Falsifier:** a service whose outcome is fully predicted by a species count
measured before any enumeration of contacts, across several habitats and cascades.

### Q3 — Cycles and catalysts in service provision

- Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes,
  and a chain cannot close by construction."
- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave
  differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- Fig 11 :65: "Introducing an actor for its cascade is ecosystem engineering, after Jones, Lawton and
  Shachak, 1994. Cane toads and kudzu are the same move with the cascade unenumerated."

**Reading.** Nutrient cycling is a cycle: the output re-enters as input, and the sign of the loop
decides whether it closes. An ecosystem engineer is a catalyst-like part that survives its own use,
and nothing in a species count records whether a part is spent or regenerating. An introduced actor
is an introduction made for a cascade, and the historical examples in the figure are the same move
with the cascade unenumerated. **Mode `specified`. Triangulated** across Fig 12 and Fig 11.
**Falsifier:** a measured service in which the sign of the loop is irrelevant and spent and
regenerating parts are indistinguishable in outcome across uses.

### Q4 — What is the order of a bundle of services?

- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate
  ones."
- Fig 9 :56: "A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the
  intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two
  conditions is not a harder case of either."
- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a
  fact about a level and never about the world"

**Reading.** The five services form a union, not a partition. A lever that preserves pollination can
leave the intersection with water regulation failing, because the second condition still binds. The
residue after one lever is categorically different, not merely smaller. Each service, once settled
against the ground, becomes an atom for a cross-service trade-off at the next level; that is the
recursion Fig 12 names, not a new primitive. **Mode `general`. Triangulated** across Fig 9 and Fig
12. **Falsifier:** one lever that restores all services with no categorically different residue, or
a trade-off that must be composed before any constituent service has been settled.

## CONCLUSION

The redundancy question is a bond question, not a headcount question. Same functional group under the
same bond gives real interchangeability; same atoms under a different bond give an isomer and a
different service. Assessment order runs from per-service residual, through habitat enumeration and
cascade, to measurement; never from a count to a verdict. Cycles and catalysts are structures a count
cannot see. The bundle is a union, so verdicts stay per service and are composed only after each is
settled. This framing does not depend on a diversity threshold, a species name, or a habitat
borrowed from ecology.
