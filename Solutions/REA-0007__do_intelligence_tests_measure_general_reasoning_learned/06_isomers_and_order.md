# Are the three candidates isomers? Structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `general` on the structural relations, `specified` on the conditional isomer claim, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §7), not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The question offers three sources for one reading. Two things are owed: (1) are they three arrangements of one held atom set, or three different things, and (2) in what dependency order must they be assessed? The score alone cannot decide either, so both are derived from the composition rules.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the psychometric and cognitive frames: item content, rule structure, exposure history, testing context, feedback, a norming sample, and a score. Bonds available: the seven plus negation (Fig 12 :49, *"The seven bonds, and whether the order of the operands matters. Negation is the eighth operator and is not a bond"*). No new primitive is needed for any of the three candidates, so none of the composition below is an import that moves a wall (Fig 6 :21).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Same score, different route: the isomer claim

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**

A score records ingredients (number correct, time, version). The bond between the test-taker and those ingredients is not recorded. Write the three candidates over a shared atom set `{item content, rule structure, exposure history, testing context, feedback}`:

- **General reasoning** binds rule structure to item content through a cross-kind structure, with exposure history treated as background. It is the `state → structure → state` first-order form of Fig 13 :74-75.
- **Learned conventions** binds item content to exposure history through a shared-meaning relation, so agreement is about a convention (Fig 2 :41).
- **Test-environment familiarity** binds testing context and feedback to the score through a harness-substitution relation (Fig 11 :70).

Over this shared set the three are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the score and decisive in what transfers. This is the precise reason a score cannot source-discriminate: the discrimination lives in the bond, which the score does not record.

**Condition on the claim (strain S3, kept visible):** the atom sets are not identical in every framing. A convention account does not require a rule-structure atom; a familiarity account does not require item content to carry shared meaning. The isomer claim therefore holds **only when all three are written over the full shared set**. In the narrower framings the three fall out as a functional group instead (Q2). The unconditional isomer claim is downgraded, not hidden.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one of the three can be written only with an atom the others do not require, and that atom is load-bearing for the score, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2 - The functional-group reading

Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

All three candidates share the substructure **"a test-taker in contact with a harness returns a reading"**. That group fixes the outcome we observe: a score. The qualifier (which bond mediates the contact) says which member of the group we have. Under the narrower framings of Q1, this is the honest claim: the three are members of one functional group, distinguished by their binding, not by their atoms.

Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** Swapping the bond changes the thing while leaving the ingredient list unchanged, so the difference between members is ordered, not symmetric. Under a merely symmetric reading the three would collapse into one term and the question would have no three options to compare. It does have three, so the bond is load-bearing.

### Q3 - The two readings come apart in two currencies

Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

- A **description** of the source is cheap: a study can say "the gain came from rule learning". That is a short arrangement over held atoms.
- An **action** that settles which source fired is expensive: a transfer test, a retest design with a counterbalanced order, a framing manipulation, a differential item functioning analysis. These take a whole budget.

Fig 12 :128-129: *"A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."* The three candidates are cheap to state and expensive to settle. Neither price predicts the other, and summing them into one "strength of evidence" figure would be the error Fig 12 names.

### Q4 - Dependency order: which link must be settled first

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**

1. **Reading link.** What the score is: an artifact with the bond removed (Fig 13 :128-129). If this is wrong, source talk is undefined.
2. **Habitat link.** Whether a substituted habitat was in play and what contact it changed (Fig 11 :66, :70).
3. **Source link.** Which bond produced this reading, settled only by an instrument that discriminates bonds (Fig 6 :34-35).

Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** The source link is exactly the uninstrumented middle link in the psychometric frame. There are instruments for the reading link (reliability, standard error) and for parts of the habitat link (retest, coaching, framing studies), but no general instrument that reads the bond off a score. That is why the question persists.

### Q5 - An analogue from comparative cognition, and its limit

Colombo and Scarf 2020 (opened, Frontiers in Psychology): in pigeon matching-to-sample work, apparent species differences in "matching concept" ability disappeared when contextual variables were adjusted. *"Once these contextual variables are accounted for, however, the performance of pigeons becomes indistinguishable from that of monkeys."* The same score, under a different harness, changes what it appears to measure. This is a low-overlap corroboration of the habitat-substitution claim (Fig 11 :70).

**Limit of the analogue (honest):** pigeons are not people, and the comparative literature's own caveats about "general intelligence" are sharp. The 2020 Royal Society meta-analysis (search-index text, reduced weight) reports a low average correlation among animal cognitive abilities (*r = 0.185*) and *"relatively weak support for general intelligence in animals."* The analogue is used for the mechanism shape, not for the human construct.

- **Falsifier for the analogue:** if adjusting contextual variables leaves a stable cross-species difference that the same instrument measures in both habitats, the habitat-substitution analogy weakens for that domain.

### Q6 - What would close the source link

Fig 6 :34-35: **"The question is not whether a sensor could exist. It is whether anything, at any resolution, is already returning something that fails to resolve. Where nothing is, there is no edge to extend from, and the search for a proto-instrument is a search for one that is already there."**

There are proto-instruments already returning something that fails to resolve: retest gains (Wikipedia synthesis: five or six points on the same test), order-of-administration effects (Trahan et al. 2014), framing effects (Steele and Aronson 1995 via Schmader et al. 2008), coaching gains (Kulik et al. 1984), and norm-drift measured by differential item functioning (Wicherts et al. 2004 via Wikipedia reference list). Each returns a source-sensitive difference while the score stays the same. The instrument is built from these, not specified from an account of intelligence.

## CONCLUSION

Over the full shared atom set the three candidates are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117): same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference invisible in the score. Under the narrower framings they are members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :123): same "test-taker in contact with a harness" substructure, different binding. The conditional form is the one carried forward. The bond, not the atom list, carries the source information (Fig 12 :5, :46), which is why a score cannot settle the question's either/or. Dependency order requires the reading link and the habitat link before the source link (Fig 3 :20, :27), and the source link is owed an instrument built from already-returning proto-instruments (Fig 6 :34-35).
