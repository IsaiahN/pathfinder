# Outreach draft: check the separator before you decide about the theory

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public macroeconomics venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the figure quotes in the packet (`08_expert_verification_packet.md`) have been checked by the sender against `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`. The first stage must survive a check or the two-stage offer collapses on contact.

**Subject:** One checkable claim about "the main cause of recessions"

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The question as posed asks for the wrong object (`general`, not `grounded`).** "Demand, supply, or interacting shocks?" treats three overlapping conditions as if they were mutually exclusive candidate causes. The figure I would ask you to check says that a disagreement which does not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, is not one hard question but several well-formed ones, and should be split rather than searched.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the effect to look for is indexed per sector and per episode, not per aggregate. A demand rule fits some episodes (a fall in spending pulling output down through credit); a supply rule fits others (a loss of productive capacity pushing prices up while output falls); the "interacting" option names the intersection plus a contact cascade that reaches employment several steps out. Over a fixed set of held atoms, the demand-recession and the supply-recession are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance, which is why the headline GDP reading cannot tell them apart while policy responds very differently. The universal "main cause" is a `gap`: a shape is returned, not a name.
>
> **What would refute it (written before this check):**
> 1. One mechanism accounts for every episode with a non-updating anchor that discriminates it from the alternatives.
> 2. The three conditions form a partition rather than a union: no episode ever satisfies two at once.
> 3. A demand-recession requires an atom a supply-recession never uses (which would kill the isomer claim).
> 4. A source-discriminating instrument already exists and returns one named cause across episodes and sectors.
>
> If any holds, I will retract the corresponding claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the figure text and the wider literature are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Blanchard and Quah, NBER Working Paper 2737 (1988): https://www.nber.org/papers/w2737
> - Baqaee and Farhi, NBER Working Paper 27152 (2020): https://www.nber.org/papers/w27152
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the eight-step formula is in the figures folder. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> Signed, [sender]

## Two-stage offer

1. **Stage 1:** send the separator, the four refuters, and the question "what would you count as a failure here?". Record the verdict before showing any framework material.
2. **Stage 2, opt-in only:** if curious, send the figure text and the two linked working papers. Later agreement is never pooled as corroboration (Fig 2 :40: agreement from one evidence pool is the pool talking).
3. **Stage 3:** offer the formula as a method each recipient can reuse against their own ground, with their own refuter written first.

No stage asks for trust, an endorsement, or a show of hands. A failure is a result.
