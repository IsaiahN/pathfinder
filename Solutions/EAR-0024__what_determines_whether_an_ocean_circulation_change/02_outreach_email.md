**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Subject:** One claim about gradual vs abrupt vs reversible ocean change, with a failure condition

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read any theory first, and I am not asking you to agree. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the three adjectives in "gradual, abrupt, or reversible" are not points on one dial. They are a union of at least three conditions with different determinants, and a single circulation change can be in two conditions at once. My claim is about which determinant governs which condition, not about any particular number.
>
> 1. Gradual versus abrupt is set by the sign of the dominant feedback cycle against a threshold. A stabilising loop lets the circulation track the forcing. A self-amplifying loop compounds a small remainder and produces a jump when a comparison is crossed. In the ocean literature this is the salt-advection feedback, named here as a binding from ocean science, not derived by the framework.
> 2. Reversible versus hysteretic is set by basin structure. With one basin the system returns when forcing is removed. With two basins it stays and needs a reverse forcing past a lower threshold. Structurally, the system cannot get back by internal search; something has to move the boundary.
> 3. The rate of forcing is a third determinant, not reducible to the threshold value. Fast forcing can tip without crossing a critical level, and reversing fast to avoid a threshold can tip on the way back. So "the tipping point" is not a single well-defined number once rate is admitted.
>
> The verdict is per circulation cell, per depth and per region. A basin-wide average can look gradual while individual convection sites jump and fail to return.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** a numerical freshwater threshold, a critical rate, a date for an AMOC collapse, or that today's circulation is or is not reversible. The framework returns a shape, not a name. Observations, not model agreement, settle a real case, and most models share one evidence pool.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. all observed ocean circulation changes lie on one continuous axis with a single governing parameter, so the union collapses to a partition;
> 2. an abrupt change with a demonstrably stabilising dominant feedback, no threshold crossing and no rate effect;
> 3. a return to the original basin on forcing removal with two basins present and no reverse threshold crossed;
> 4. a critical forcing level invariant across very slow and very fast forcing, with no rate-induced or return tipping;
> 5. per-cell residuals well approximated by the basin-wide average for abrupt events.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any holds, the framing is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
