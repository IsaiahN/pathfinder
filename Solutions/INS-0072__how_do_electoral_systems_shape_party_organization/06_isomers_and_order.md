# Isomers, bonds and order: the electoral rule as a typed relation

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the skeleton, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 5).

## RESIDUAL

The question says one system "shapes" four outcomes. Two questions are owed before any answer:

1. What kind of object is an electoral system, structurally? Is it an atom, a bond, or a method crossing a boundary?
2. If two systems are built from the same atoms, what is their relation, and what is the order in which their effects must be assessed?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: voters, candidates, parties, seats, and the preference distribution (states and structures of a political system at this scale, per Fig 4 :19 to 20, *"Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."*). Bonds held: the seven typed relations of Fig 12, plus negation. No new primitive is available without an import. Currency: ballots to seats conversion. Anchor: a distribution that does not update on the institution's own report.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 What kind of object is the rule?

Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*

Fig 12 :46: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."*

World check, opened 2026-11-22: *"An electoral system is a set of rules and mechanisms used to determine the results of an election"* and the rules govern *"how votes translate into the election outcome (seats)"* (Wikipedia, *Electoral system*, 2026).

Therefore the rule is a **bond**, specifically a threshold comparison over quantities (Fig 12 :87, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*; Operators :51, *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*). It is not an additional voter or party. Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."*

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a reform is shown to introduce a new primitive rather than re-arrange held atoms, this reading fails.

### Q2 Are two systems over the same atoms isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*

World corroboration, genuinely low overlap, opened 2026-11-22: the effective number of parties is *"equivalent to the Herfindahl–Hirschman index, used in economics; the Simpson diversity index in ecology; the inverse participation ratio (IPR) in physics; and the Rényi entropy of order α=2 in information theory"* (Wikipedia, *Effective number of parties*, 2026). One weighted-count atom keeps its form across four unrelated fields. That is Fig 13 :121 in the field's own data.

- Plurality and list PR over `{voters, candidates, parties, seats}` are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance.
- A reform that adds a new regional party changes the atom set. The relation is then composition plus import, and Fig 8 :12 to 13 requires a **source gate: provenance** and a **shadow test: explains a gap**.
- **Live strain, S1:** real systems gain and lose atoms, so the isomer claim holds only over a fixed atom set. The condition is stated here and carried into `01`, `09` and `11`. Not smoothed over.

**Falsifier:** if the cross-field measure changes form in one of the four fields where it is said to be equivalent, the isomer corroboration fails.

### Q3 What is the order of assessment?

Fig 3 :20 to 21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*

Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*

The dependency order for this question is:

1. **Perception**: can the system tell a vote from a seat and one party from another? (Fig 3 :4 to 5)
2. **Vocabulary**: does it have terms for what the rule affords (entry, threshold, list, district)? (Fig 3 :6 to 8)
3. **Objective**: can it say what would count as progress, in its own terms? (Fig 3 :9 to 11) This is the likely break: "representation" is a comparison (Operators :51), but most institutions have no instrument that discriminates among sources of a reading.
4. **Planning**: a route from rule to outcome under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry**: name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next election (Fig 3 :14 to 17).

A study that measures outcomes before it has a vocabulary for the bond is reading below the break. Fig 3 :22 to 23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can. Which is why a reading taken below the break is not a weak reading. It is a reading of nothing."*

Fig 9 :27 to 28: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."* Description precedes frame selection; frame selection precedes measurement.

**Falsifier:** if an outcome can be reliably attributed to the rule while the vocabulary link has no instrument, the dependency order is wrong.

### Q4 What is the relation between the four outcomes?

Not a chain, not a list: a **union** with overlapping conditions (Fig 9 :54 to 58). Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

Over a fixed atom set:

- The **unit of competition** bond touches party organization and representation (D2 and D3).
- The **threshold** bond touches representation and polarization (D3 and D4).
- The **refusal** operator (negation, Operators :58) touches accountability and, indirectly, the others (D5).

Because the conditions overlap, one lever clears the region where only one condition binds and leaves the intersections standing. Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."* Each intersection needs its own loop.

Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The four outcomes share a functional group (a threshold comparison over a distribution) with different qualifiers. That is why a single "effect of electoral systems" average is a category error, not merely an imprecise estimate.

### Q5 Is there a scale crossing, and what crosses?

Fig 4 :22 to 24: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down. Upward: a method that can be reapplied. Never the recording of a particular success."*

A party that learns how to coordinate under a rule can carry the method to the next contest. A party that carries only a recording of the last win produces a repeat and cannot produce a new outcome (Fig 4 :25). Fig 4 :30: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."*

**Falsifier:** if a recording of one occasion can be shown to generate a new outcome without a re-applicable method, the membrane rule fails here.

## CONCLUSION

An electoral system is a **typed threshold bond** over held political atoms, not a new atom and not the outcome itself. Two systems over the same atoms are **isomers**, with the fixed-atom condition kept visible as a live strain. The four outcomes are a **union of overlapping conditions** sharing a functional group, not a chain and not a four-way partition. The order of assessment is perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry, with the vocabulary-to-objective link the likely break. What crosses a scale boundary is a method or a head start, never a recording of one election. No figure line returns a best system or a numerical effect size; that remains a `gap`.
