# Supporting derivation: task atoms, bonds, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the operator distinctions, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a labor-market reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks how the answer varies by occupation. Two readings of an occupation are available: a list of tasks (ingredients), and a bonded arrangement of tasks (a molecule). The residual is the unstated bond. A list of tasks before and after automation can look identical while the work is decisively different, because *"the bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording"* (Fig 12 :5). Three questions are owed: (1) does "shape, not a name" forbid naming task bindings; (2) which bond is expressed by "creates jobs" versus "changes jobs"; (3) what order and analogues govern an occupation over time.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: the task atoms available to an occupational slot at time t, plus the seven bonds and negation. No seventh task kind is available without an import (Fig 13 :115). Bonds held: `+`, `∥`, `≡`, `→`, `⇒`, `−`, `⋛`, and `¬`. Scale: the slot at one time, the slot across a career (ordered), and the population of slots (Fig 4). Anchor: the market's payment for the tasks at the slot, not the occupation label.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Does "shape, not a name" forbid naming a task binding? CORRECTION TO MY OWN §3 of `05`

- The README: *"what a missing thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)."* The subject is a missing primitive, one not held in Γ.
- Task atoms are held in D3 and are named in public taxonomies such as O*NET. They are not missing.
- Naming a task binding is therefore **not** forbidden. It is a binding from the labor frame onto atoms already held. What is forbidden is inventing a seventh task kind, or claiming the binding moves the wall (Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom"*).
- **I overstated this earlier** in `05` §3 and retract that form here. Kept visible under the correction note in `05` §5. Triangulated (README + Fig 6).

### Q2: Which bond is "creates" and which is "changes"?

The Operators table separates two ordered bonds that a flat formula would write the same way:

- `→` sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :72). Test: *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*
- `⇒` production: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Fig 12 :78). Test: *"is there a B before A fires? No."*

The question's verb "create" commits to **production**. Under Fig 6 :21, composition inside a closure never adds an atom, so a `⇒` bond whose second operand did not exist before cannot be satisfied by rearranging held tasks alone. It requires an **import** from a frame with a differing closure (Fig 6 :23), with a source gate and shadow test (Fig 8 :10-13). That is why "new jobs" is the C2 condition and why it needs provenance, not just an assertion.

The verb "changes" commits to a rearrangement under held atoms: same atoms, different bond, different substance (Fig 12 :117). Autor, Levy and Murnane report *"Changes in task content within nominally identical occupations explain more than half of the overall demand shift induced by computerization"*: the occupation label stayed, so the atoms were held; the demand shift moved, so the bond changed. That is an isomer reading measured at the ground.

**Falsifier, written before the conclusion:** if a `⇒` bond in an occupation can be satisfied with no new atom and no import from a differing closure, Fig 6 :21 is false and the C1/C2 split collapses. If two occupations share every task atom and every bond yet diverge systematically in employment, Fig 12 :117 fails as a description of occupational variation.

### Q3: What order governs an occupation over time?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* The five links are perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry. For an occupation, the middle link is the task-level discriminator: does the slot have a term for what the changed situation affords and can it say what would count as progress? Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- Fig 7 :37: *"Progress per handoff has two factors. Zero either and the chain runs in place:"*, namely *"the bracket's fidelity across the handoff"* (:38) and *"the ground's pressure on the room"* (:39). A decade of stable employment is a chain of handoffs; an occupation that keeps handing itself the same room is a mirror chain (Fig 7 :36).
- Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."* An occupation settled against the market becomes an atom for the next level (a region, an industry, a national accounts category). That is the recursion, not a new primitive.
- Ordering is not a claim that "automation happens first, then jobs change". Fig 13 :75 says a structure sits between states and adds both. The `→` in any narrative is imposed by the narration, and the derivation explicitly declines to name which task composed first (compare the exemplar's *"we cannot say which state composed first, and we don't know if the order mattered"* in *How Intelligence Actually Works*, carried mapping).

### Q4: What analogues are licensed, and what is refused?

Licensed:

- **Isomer** (Fig 12 :117): two occupations over a shared task set, arranged differently, are different substances with the same ingredients. This is the exact form of "same workers, different work".
- **Catalyst and spent part** (Fig 12 :119): a skill that survives its own use behaves differently across uses from one that does not, and nothing in the skill's description records which. This is the mechanism behind a strength becoming a rigidity.
- **Functional group** (Fig 12 :123): where two slots share only a substructure, such as `task → task` mediation, they are members of a functional group, not isomers. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member. Under the strict Fig 12 definition, two occupations sharing only one task atom are functional-group relatives, not isomers.
- **Low-overlap corroboration:** Moravec's paradox, *"the hard problems are easy and the easy problems are hard"*, says a task's position in a difficulty ladder is not a standing property; it is a property of which atoms are codifiable and which sit in a long sensorimotor lineage. This corroborates Fig 11 :49-51 at the cognition stratum without sharing the labor-economics evidence pool.

Refused:

- The six-term physical substrate (Fig 13) is **not** an analogue for task kinds. It only fit by renaming a routine as energy, which is a strain. Re-census assigns Fig 13 to the honest-stop role (strain S1 in `01` section 6).
- A "technology adoption curve" is not a sequence bond at the level of task atoms. It is a population-level reading across the membrane (Fig 4), and the transform is lossy (Fig 4 :30).
- A "percent of jobs automatable" is not a bond or a substance. It is a candidate Γ with a residual, a filter for the budget (Fig 9 :60), not a member of the task set.

## CONCLUSION

Over a held task set, an occupation before and after automation is an isomer: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The verb "create" commits to the production bond `⇒`, which needs a new atom and therefore an import from a differing closure; the verb "change" commits to a rearrangement under held atoms and needs no import. Order is by dependency (Fig 3) and by two-factor handoffs (Fig 7), not by a narrative claim about which task fired first. The licensed analogues are isomer, catalyst and functional group; the physical six-term set, an adoption curve and an automatable percentage are refused as analogues. This rescues what was defensible in the plain-language question (tasks are rearranged and some new work crosses in from elsewhere) without the part that failed (a universal net sign, or a standing list of safe occupations). The falsifiers are stated above and were written before the run.
