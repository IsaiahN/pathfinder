# The Tether's answer: automation and net employment by occupation

**Mode: `specified` on the per-domain verdicts, `general` on the structural shape, not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a separator, not a labor-market result. Simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 6), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

R = |Γ(b,a) − o′| per occupational slot s, ordered by time. Two rival frames share each slot: one predicts that automating task t removes the labor that performed t; the other predicts that lower costs raise output and demand for labor in the remaining tasks. o′ is what the market actually returns per occupation. The question asks for one aggregate net sign and then for per-slot variation, which are different objects.

> **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."** (Fig 1 :19)
> **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."** (Fig 1 :22)

The contact failure is a **union compound**, not one hard question with two opposite answers (Fig 9 :54, :55, :57). It has its own loop and its own ground. The sought-for shape, bound before the run, was a separator plus a discriminator plus the refusal of a universal sign.

## 2. The shape

1. **There is no universal net sign at the object level asked.** "Usually creates more jobs than it eliminates" is a threshold over an aggregate this derivation did not measure. A net count is a budget filter, not a witness: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."* (Fig 9 :60).
2. **Displacement at a slot is a channel failure, not the decay of the ground.** Fig 10 :24: *"the ground does not decay, the channel does"*; :26: *"saturated metrics, pooled readings"*. An occupation loses work when its reading of what pays goes stale while the market keeps paying someone else. A decade of employment is a chain of handoffs with two factors, either of which can go to zero (Fig 7 :37-39).
3. **Creation at a slot is an import, not an internal composition.** Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom"*; :23: *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."* New tasks enter from entrants, adjacent technologies, other industries, customers and science, and the import needs a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 8 :10-13). The verb "create" is the production bond `⇒`: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Fig 12 :78).
4. **How the answer varies by occupation: valence is residual-relative and no standing list survives.** Fig 11 :49: *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."* Over a fixed task set, before and after are isomers: *"same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* (Fig 12 :117). The occupation label can stay while the bond changes, which is what the within-occupation task-shift evidence reports.
5. **A skill can turn from a catalyst into a spent part when the residual changes.** Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*
6. **The labor market is the anchor, not consensus or forecast.** Fig 2 :29 names it; :30 disqualifies anything that moves when pushed; :35 says *"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."* A 48/52 opinion split and an "on balance" projection are rooms, not anchors.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not a "yes, automation usually creates more jobs" and not a "no, it destroys them". Both are `general`-mode verdicts with no per-slot measurement in this run.
- Not a percentage of jobs at risk, not a net jobs forecast, not a list of safe occupations. Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."*
- Not that displacement and creation cancel. They are two conditions with different anchors and different instruments; summing them is the flat-formula error (Fig 12 :5).
- Not that reskilling, robot taxes or any named lever is the answer. Those are candidate bindings with provenance records, not figure outputs.
- Not that the six-term physical substrate names task kinds. That was a strain and was re-censused to the honest-stop role (Fig 13 :115 governs only the stop).
- Not `grounded`. No per-occupation labor-market measurement was taken by this run; no term here is settled by the ground.

## 4. What would settle it, the ground

A task-level discriminator, not a further aggregate argument. *The Hard Problem is About Source*, opened this session, states the rule: an instrument that reads green *"cannot comment on the source"*, and several paths can produce the same reading, so *"behavior is a projection with lossy information"*. An employment count is such a projection: task removal, task transformation and task import all produce it. The instruments that exist are O*NET (task taxonomy), BLS OEWS (annual employment and wages for about 830 occupations) and BLS Employment Projections (a forward instrument, not an anchor). This run did not use them.

Standing tests, written before the run:

- Does the slot still have a ground that pays for its tasks, and is the bracket faithful across handoffs (Fig 7 :37-39)?
- Does a new task cross in from a closure that differs, with provenance and a shadow test (Fig 6 :23, Fig 8 :50-52)?
- Can a per-occupation measurement separate removal from transformation from import before the count is taken?
- **Fundamental restatement so the falsifier can fire:** a prospective, per-occupation statistic that predicts which named slots gain and lose next, survives a habitat substitution that changes who pays, and holds across a genuinely independent second frame. As usually written, "net jobs" is already satisfied by any aggregate and is therefore dead.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable in principle.** The gap is the universal net sign and the winning-occupation list. The instruments that could close it exist in the world, but this simulated derivation did not run them, so the gap is unreachable-at-this-budget, not underivable. Fig 8 :40-43 issues a different instruction for each, and importing an atom against a budget limit adds one that was not needed (Fig 6 :51-52). The answer stops where the instruments stop distinguishing, and the lineage is recorded so the stop is checkable. *"Indiscernibility is not identity."*

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not answer "yes" or "no" to whether automation usually creates more jobs than it eliminates, because that net sign aggregates across the very slots in which the signal lives. It returns a separator instead. At an occupational slot, losing work is a failure of the channel to a market that still pays someone else, while gaining new work is an import of task atoms from a closure that differs from the slot's own. An occupation that survives is an isomer over a held set of tasks, same atoms arranged with a different bond, and its value flips when the residual changes, so there is no standing list of safe occupations. The labor market, not a poll or a projection, is what settles each slot. What is offered is the shape, four refuters written in advance, and the task-level instrument owed to close it. The net sign stays open until the ground pays for it.
