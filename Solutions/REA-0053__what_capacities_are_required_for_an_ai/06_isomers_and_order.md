## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**
- Fig 9 :56: **"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."**

The conditions in the audit overlap. A system can have a live channel and a dead reach; a system can have an unbroken chain and a stale channel. These are not four versions of one difficulty. They are a union with intersections, and each intersection is a specific compound (Fig 9 :58). So the answer to "which capacities" is not a partition that can be searched one bucket at a time.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one intervention restores all four conditions at once and leaves no categorically different residue, the union reading fails and the partition (or single-capacity) reading stands.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Fig 3 :22-23: a step whose input never arrives **"cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can."**
- Formula step 2: refuse before mint. Fig 8 :53: **"Residual first, frame second."**

The order is: describe the residual -> locate the broken link -> refuse the false term -> extend perception or vocabulary -> set the objective -> plan -> learn and carry -> settle against the ground -> promote methods across the membrane. Length of autonomous operation is not a property of the last link (planning); it is a property of whichever link currently stops the chain. Fig 3 :29-30 notes that most improvement effort lands above the break because that is where the measurements already exist.

**Falsifier:** if sustained small R persists with a measured break below the planning link, the dependency-order claim fails.

### Q3 - Are alternative capacity sets isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**

Two systems built from the same atoms (chain, ground, habitat, reach) but with different bonds, for example one with reach renewed by peer import and one with reach renewed by inward instrument extension, are **isomers** under Fig 12: same atoms, different arrangement, different behavioural substance. Their ingredient lists are identical, so nothing in a flat capacity inventory distinguishes them.

If two systems share only one atom (say, both have a ground channel but nothing else in common), they are not isomers. They are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124): *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* This matters because most comparisons of "autonomous agents" in the literature are functional-group comparisons, not isomer comparisons.

**Falsifier:** if two systems with identical atom sets and different bonds behave identically under a discriminating instrument, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 - What does the arrangement cost?

- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- Fig 12 :127: **"They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"**
- Fig 12 :128-129: **"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."**
- Fig 12 :100: **"The price of an arrangement is the choice it records, so it is logarithmic in the number of bonds available."**

A capacity set is a molecule, not a bag. Adding a bond type changes the term even when the atoms are unchanged. The cost has two currencies: bits to state the arrangement, and actions to find out whether it actually holds in a given habitat. A long chain of cheap parts can be cheap to state and expensive to test; a short arrangement over a costly operand can be the reverse. Summing them into one figure is the error Fig 12 :130 names. For this question: a checklist of capacities is cheap in bits and often ruinously expensive in actions, because the actions are what a long horizon consists of.

**Falsifier:** a demonstrated fixed conversion between the two currencies for autonomy capacity sets would refute the "different units" claim.

### Q5 - Is the union ordered or symmetric?

- Operators table :48: comparison ⋛ is a threshold relation, not a symmetric join.
- Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**
- Fig 7 :37: **"Progress per handoff has two factors. Zero either and the chain runs in place:"**

The conditions are not symmetric, and the chain is not a set. Swapping the order of perception and learning gives a different term (sequence -> is ordered; Fig 12 :49). The two factors in Fig 7 (bracket fidelity and ground pressure) are a product, not a sum: zero either and the chain runs in place. So the union in Q1 is an **ordered union with a multiplicative gate**, not a commutative conjunction. Progress over a long horizon is Fig 7 :26: *"R = h² × S"* as the figure writes it, with fidelity and pressure as the two factors.

**Falsifier:** a demonstrated case where ground pressure is zero and the chain still makes progress would break the product reading.

## CONCLUSION

The capacities form an ordered, overlapping union with a multiplicative handoff gate, not a flat inventory (Q1, Q2, Q5). Alternative capacity sets over the same atom set are isomers, while most literature comparisons are functional-group comparisons (Q3). The arrangement is priced in two incommensurable currencies, so a cheap checklist is not an affordable design (Q4). All four conclusions are `specified`, ungrounded. The falsifiers are written in advance and none has been fired here.
