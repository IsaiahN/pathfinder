## Q1 - Why the pattern-mechanism step is a bond change, not an identity

> **"two names, one referent"** (Operators :24) and **"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."** (Operators :27)
> **"A produces B; B did not exist before"** (Fig 12 :76) and **"is there a B before A fires? No."** (Fig 12 :78)
> **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** (Fig 12 :92)
> **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."** (Fig 12 :46)

"Same pattern" is an identity claim at the level of the reading. It uses the symmetric bond ≡: substitute one domain's exponent for the other's, and nothing is lost at that level. "Same mechanism" is a production claim: the generator fires and the pattern appears; before the generator fires, that pattern was not waiting in the second domain. The test for production is an intervention that removes the generator and re-reads the pattern.

The ordinary-language question treats these as one relation, as if seeing the second name entitled you to the first bond. It does not. Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."* The equation written on the page is the ingredient list. The bond is what the intervention reveals.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the pattern itself carries a readable route from generator to reading, so that the production claim can be substituted for the identity claim without any intervention and nothing is lost, the bond-change finding fails.

**Status:** `general`, figure-derived for the bond distinction; `specified` for the cross-domain application. Single-source on the figure side (Operators table and Fig 12 are one framework), with the Gentner structure-mapping literature as an independent low-overlap witness that structural alignment is not object matching.

## Q2 - Isomer, convergence or functional group?

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :123)
> **"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."** (Fig 13 :121)
> **"Two chains built from different links may compose functionally identical terms, and the converse holds too: the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."** (Fig 13 :122)
> **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."** (Fig 12 :104)

Three distinct readings of "same pattern" are admitted by the figures:

1. **Isomer.** Two candidate mechanisms hold the same atoms but different typed bonds. The shared pattern hides the difference; the intervention reveals it. This is the strict "same form, different mechanism" case (Fig 12 :117).
2. **Convergent arrival.** Two chains built from different links compose functionally identical terms. Here the same pattern reflects genuinely the same coarse substance reached separately, which is the strict "same form, same mechanism at the coarse level" case (Fig 13 :121). Universality classes are the world instance; the renormalization-group flow is the instrument that identifies the shared fixed point.
3. **Functional group.** Both candidates share a `state → structure → state` shape while differing in which atoms occupy the slots. The pattern fixes the kind, not the mechanism. This is the weak, and most common, cross-domain case (Fig 12 :123).

The level matters. Fig 12 :104 lets a settled molecule become an atom for whatever composes over it, so a mechanism that is irreducible at one level can be an operand at the next. Calling two mechanisms "the same" can be true at the coarse level and false at the fine one. That is not a contradiction; it is the recursion the figure names. Re-census S3 in `01` §6 resolves the two senses of "shared mechanism" this way.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two mechanisms with identical atoms and identical bonds diverge in which interventions break them, with no habitat difference and no unmeasured bond, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D4). Conversely, if two mechanisms with different bonds never diverge under any intervention at any resolution, the isomer claim is empty. Both directions are recorded.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated for legality (Fig 12 and Fig 13, different figures); single-source for any given domain application until an intervention is run. The fixed-atom qualifier is live and named, not hidden: without a measured atom set the isomer claim degrades to the functional-group claim.

## Q3 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)
> **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."** (Fig 3 :25)
> **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** (Fig 3 :27)

Order for a researcher facing a shared pattern:

1. **Perception.** Can the pattern be read reliably in each domain? Failure here has obvious tests.
2. **Vocabulary.** Do we have a term for the bond and for the source of the reading, or only for the ingredient list? This is the middle link. It has the fewest instruments.
3. **Objective.** Can we say what would count as a failure of "same mechanism"? Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A vocabulary with only pattern names cannot state this.
4. **Planning.** Design the discriminator or intervention under a budget.
5. **Learn and carry.** Record the route, the refusal and the mode.

C1 and C2 mostly fail at links 1 and 2; C3 and C4 mostly fail at link 5, because the field names what worked but never records the bond or the habitat. That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear both (Fig 9 :55). Budget will naturally pile up at link 1 and link 4, where fitting tools and experiment budgets already exist, while the break sits at 2 and 5.

**Falsifier:** a research program whose perception, vocabulary and objective links all measure as working and whose failure is located at planning alone. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim, though not the dependency ordering.

## Q4 - Competing explanations, and what each can and cannot reach

| candidate | what it names | figure-line bearing | what it cannot reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Universality class** (statistical physics) | a shared coarse generator behind different microscopic models | Fig 12 :104 level-relative irreducibility; Fig 13 :121 convergence | Which microscopic model applies to a new domain without a discriminator; it is a coarse-level verdict only |
| **Convergent evolution** (biology) | analogous form from independent origins | Fig 12 :95-97 bond invisible in ingredients | Whether a mathematical pattern has an analogous source; it is a domain analogue, not this ground |
| **Isomorphism / structure mapping** (mathematics, cognition) | structure-preserving correspondence | Fig 12 :92 symmetry decides newness; Operators :24 identity | Source. An isomorphism preserves structure and says nothing about which generator fired |
| **Buckingham pi / dimensional similarity** (engineering) | equivalent dimensionless descriptions | Fig 2 :29-30 anchor versus instrument | Mechanism: the theorem explicitly does not identify the most physically meaningful group |
| **Spurious correlation / common cause** (statistics) | one hidden factor producing two readings | Fig 2 :6-7 collapse 2 | Positive attribution: it rules out a mechanism, it does not name the right one |
| **Equifinality** (systems theory) | many means to one end state | Fig 9 :54-57 union; Fig 11 :49-51 valence relative to residual | Which mean operated in a given case without process evidence |
| **Kleiber-type allometry** (biology) | a stable exponent with contested generators | F10 `gap` row; Fig 9 :32 | The source: the pattern is settled, the theoretical explanation is not |
| **"Same equation, same mechanism"** (the claim under test) | identity promoted across levels | Fig 12 :46; Fig 2 :39 | Every case where the bond differs; it is the candidate that must pay, not a rival verdict |

No candidate is discarded by prestige or familiarity. Each is ranged against the same residual in the order Fig 8 :53 requires. The first six are mostly low-overlap fields; the seventh and eighth are the phenomenon and the claim, not independent witnesses.

## Q5 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the universality-class emphasis on a shared coarse generator, as Fig 12 :104 plus a working discriminator;
- the convergent-evolution emphasis on independent routes, as Fig 12 :117 plus Fig 13 :121;
- the structure-mapping emphasis on relations over objects, as Fig 12 :5 and :92;
- the spurious-correlation warning, as Fig 2 :6-7 and :40;
- the equifinality warning, as Fig 9 :54-57 and Fig 11 :49-51.

It cannot generate the *name* of the mechanism behind a specific shared pattern. That is the `gap` recorded in F10 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 (*"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*) and Fig 8 :39 apply. The source essay states why: an instrument that reads a result *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists"*.

## CONCLUSION

The observation "same pattern" is a symmetric identity claim; "same mechanism" is an ordered production claim, and the change of bond must be paid for by an intervention. Two candidates with the same atoms under different bonds are isomers (Fig 12 :117); two chains from different links may reach the same substance (Fig 13 :121); two candidates sharing only the `state → structure → state` pattern are members of one functional group (Fig 12 :123). The conditions bind in dependency order with vocabulary and carry the usual breaks, which is why C1/C2 and C3/C4 need different instruments. The standard cross-domain explanations are generated by the shape up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim has a written falsifier in both directions; the order claim has one; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
