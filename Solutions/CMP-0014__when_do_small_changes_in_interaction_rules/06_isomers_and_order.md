# Isomers, order and the two currencies in a small rule change

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

## RESIDUAL

The shape claim in `01` says that where a small rule change lands matters more than how big it is. Three supporting questions are owed before it can be checked:

1. Under Fig 12's definition, is a small rule change an **isomer** change or something else?
2. Does the **order** of operands matter for whether the jump occurs?
3. A rule change is cheap to describe and expensive to verify. Can the two costs be compared?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (the supporting layer)

Atoms held: the six of Fig 13 (space, time, energy states; spectrum, gradient, network structures). Bonds held: the seven of Fig 12 plus negation. Operands in the empirical domains: agents, sites, neurons, controllers. Currency: bit-cost of describing the changed rule; action-cost of measuring whether the jump happened. Anchor: the measured discontinuity or early-warning statistic in each domain. This is a partition at the anchor level, a union at the mechanism level (Fig 9 :54-57), and the dependency order is perception of the slot residual, then vocabulary of bond and cycle, then an objective definition of "abrupt".

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Is the small change an isomer change?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :95: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing."**

- If δ changes only the rule (the bond) and leaves actors and quantities (the atoms) unchanged, the two states are **isomers** by the figure's own definition. Same atoms, different bond, different collective substance.
- If δ changes an atom (adds an agent type, a new quantity, a new field), it is **not** an isomer change. It is a new atom, and under Fig 6 an atom can only arrive by import: *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."*
- The distinction matters because the question asks about *interaction rules*, not about new components. A rule change is therefore usually an isomer change, and the abruptness is the behavioural difference the bond carries.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a rule change leaves all seven bond tests returning the same answers and the atoms unchanged, yet the collective behaviour differs qualitatively, the isomer reading fails and a hidden atom must be found. Record that as a re-census, not as a strain to be smoothed.

**Status:** triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 and Fig 4 :20, *"Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change"*), with the physical scope strain recorded in `01` F1.

### Q2: Does order matter?

Operators table: sequence is *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :70, :72). Comparison ⋛ is *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*. Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**

- Under a symmetric bond (`+`, `∥`, `≡`), k operands have k! spellings and one term. Reordering the same agents under a symmetric interaction does not make a new substance; a small reordering should not, by itself, produce a jump.
- Under an ordered bond (`→`, `⇒`, `−`, `⋛`), different orders are different terms. Swapping two operands is a different substance at the same description length. A small ordering change can therefore be abrupt for exactly the same reason a bond swap can.
- The threshold bond ⋛ is ordered. "A crosses B" and "B crosses A" are different states at the same atoms, which is the figure-level warrant for treating a threshold crossing as a state change rather than a continuous variation. The Operators table adds: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*. A system that can only state conjunctions can describe that a condition holds; it cannot state that a threshold was crossed.

**Falsifier:** if, in a domain under test, swapping the order of two operands under an ordered bond leaves every measurable collective quantity unchanged, the order clause does not govern there and must be removed for that domain.

**Status:** single-source for the bond definitions (Fig 12 and Operators table are the same figure family). World corroboration: the sandpile result that spatial arrangement matters at fixed grain count (Orzechowski et al. 2026) is a different witness to "arrangement carries information", but it does not test the six bond tests.

### Q3: Cycle, functional group, catalyst: three structures that make a small change large

Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."**

- **Cycle** supplies the amplification. A chain cannot close, so a chain bounds the response; a re-entrant loop can compound a remainder (Fig 13 :31, *"a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos"*).
- **Functional group** supplies the discreteness. Its presence or absence is a binary event, which is why a continuous parameter change can yield a discontinuous state change: the threshold is on the group, not on the magnitude.
- **Catalyst** supplies the persistence. A part that survives its own use keeps the loop primed after the perturbation, which is what makes the new state stick rather than relax. In the classical sandpile this is the slow driving that reloads the system; in cortex it is the sustained background activity; in an engineered controller it is the integral term.

Each is a different structure and each is necessary for a different part of the claim. None is a seventh member of the substrate (Fig 13 :124: *"Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member"*).

**Falsifier:** remove the persisting part while keeping loop and group. If the state still jumps and holds, the catalyst clause is idle; record that rather than asserting it.

### Q4: The two currencies do not add

Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**

Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

- δ is cheap in bits: one bond changed, one parameter nudged. That is the "small change" in the question.
- The same δ can be arbitrarily expensive in actions: measuring whether the loop crossed its threshold may need a whole experimental budget, long time series, or a dense spatial snapshot.
- Because the currencies are in different units, a system holding only one accounting will not notice the shortfall. It will propose rule changes it cannot afford to test. Fig 12 :127: *"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."*
- Applied here: the claim "small changes cause big effects" silently mixes the two currencies. Small in **bits**, large in **collective effect**, and expensive in **actions to verify**. These are three readings and must be held apart. Summing them is the error the figure names, not the fix.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a case where the bit-cost of the rule change predicts the action-cost of verifying its consequence with the same units and no remainder. That would collapse the two-currency claim.

### Q5: Round trip gap between the micro rule and the macro state

Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

Fig 10 :68: **"Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between."**

1. **Sent up (x):** a micro rule description, for example "the inhibition parameter is changed by δ".
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** the shorthand "a small rule change occurs in an amplifying loop".
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** a macro prediction, for example "the network jumps to the absorbing state at δ_c".

**R_T is nonzero.** The shorthand drops: which slot carries the residual first; the sign of the specific loop; which functional group is present; and the bits/actions distinction. It also drops the anchor, because "jump" is only defined relative to a measuring instrument. Conversely, the kernel survives: the same loop, the same rule for naming what worked. R_T is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints, not measured numerically, because no ground reading was taken here. Treating the shorthand as identical to the micro description would set R_T to zero and commit the dead-reckoning error.

## CONCLUSION

A small rule change is an isomer change when atoms are held fixed, and the abruptness is the behavioural difference the bond carries. Order matters under ordered bonds and not under symmetric ones. Three structures make the effect large: cycle for amplification, functional group for discreteness, catalyst for persistence. The change is small in bits, potentially large in collective effect, and expensive in actions to verify; those three readings do not add. Any macro claim carries a round trip gap R_T that must be enumerated. The falsifiers above are written before the conclusion and are copied into the expert packet.
