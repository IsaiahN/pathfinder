# Isomers, functional groups, and order in an origin-of-life pathway

**Mode: `specified` for the structure claim, `general` for the bond definition. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).**

## RESIDUAL

Does "the same basic origin-of-life pathway" mean the same atoms, the same bonds, or the same operation order? Three questions are owed: (1) can a pathway be treated as a molecule at some level, (2) are two pathways on different habitats isomers or members of a functional group, and (3) does the order of operations carry information the ingredients do not?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: feedstock molecules (for example HCN and derivatives), energy sources (UV, geothermal, impact), solvents, mineral catalysts, and the operations produce, activate, polymerise, encapsulate, select, copy. Bonds held: the seven, including ordered production (⇒) and sequence (→), and comparison (⋛). No new primitive is available without import. The habitat is enumerated from the planet, never composed (Fig 11 :43).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Can a pathway be treated as a molecule?

- Fig 12 :46: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."*
- Operators table: ⇒ is *"production"*, defined as *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, and is ordered. → is *"sequence"*, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*.
- A pathway is a chain of productions and sequences over feedstocks and catalysts. That is a molecule in the figure's sense, not a loose metaphor.
- Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."* A pathway settled in H1 can serve as an atom when we compare it with a pathway in H2.
- **Caveat.** Fig 12 :48 defines a chain as *"the degenerate case: one bond type, no branching"*. Real prebiotic networks branch. Treating them as a single chain is the flat-formula collapse in Fig 12 :6-17: the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not. Mode `specified`, unmeasured.
- **Falsifier:** if a prebiotic network cannot be decomposed into typed binary relations without losing a measurable property, the molecule reading fails and the functional-group reading stands alone.

### Q2 - Isomer or functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."* Fig 13 :124: *"the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."*
- Consider two habitats H1 and H2. If both use the same feedstock set (for example HCN derivatives, sulfite, ferrocyanide, metal ions, UV) but arrange them with different bond order and different catalysts, the two pathways are **isomers** by Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The behaviour difference is exactly what a checker should look for.
- If H2 uses a different solvent or a different energy source so that the feedstock set itself changes, the two pathways share only the **functional group**: the operation shape produce → activate → polymerise → encapsulate → select → copy. That is Fig 12 :124.
- The reading that fails is "same atoms, same bond order, same outcome in both habitats". That is not a new instance of a pathway; it is a replay of one, and Fig 11 :66-70 explains why it fails: a new habitat is a substitution, and you inherit actors you did not choose.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two pathways sharing only the functional group nevertheless produce indistinguishable living systems under every measurement, the isomer-versus-functional-group distinction has no purchase in this domain and the "generic method" verdict moves to `grounded` for that case.

### Q3 - Does order carry information?

- Fig 12 :42: *"one bond type costs nothing; seven cost 2.8 bits each"*. The bond is charged for what it could have been.
- Fig 12 :79: production test, *"is there a B before A fires? No."* Order is intrinsic to production. A feedstock that appears only after activation fires is a different substance from one present at the start.
- Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* A list of ingredients for life cannot say what gets where first. This is the exact failure of a pathway described only as "HCN, UV, iron, water".
- Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* In this question the goal is R reaching zero (⋛), the plan is the ordered operation sequence, and progress is the shrinking residual (−).
- **Falsifier:** if permuting the order of the operations leaves every measured outcome unchanged, then order is a free bond here and the sequence claim is refuted.

### Q4 - What about the threshold comparison between two habitats?

- Operators table :51: comparison ⋛ is *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, tested by *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present?"*
- The question "does R reach zero in H2?" is a ⋛ claim. The operands are residuals, which are quantities at their level. This use is in type, unlike the QMGR-0037 case where ⋛ was applied to two structures.
- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."* Bits price the description of the pathway; actions price finding out whether it closes R in H2. A short arrangement over a costly habitat is cheap to state and may take a whole mission budget to settle. Summing them into one score would hide the shortfall (Fig 12 :129-130).
- **Falsifier:** a demonstration that the bit cost and the action cost are the same quantity in this domain, so that a compact pathway description predicts the mission cost exactly.

### Q5 - Where does the order come from, and does it transfer?

- Fig 13 :88: *"The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no two passes begin from the same place."* A pathway is not replayed; each turn begins from the residual the previous turn left.
- Fig 4 :34-35: *"A seed carries no small tree: it carries what will grow one, and the conditions it lands in decide what it becomes."* What crosses to H2 is the seed (the operation sequence), not the tree (the H1 outcome). The conditions in H2 decide what it becomes.
- Combine with Fig 11 :49: *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* Even the same operation can flip sign when the residual changes.
- **Falsifier:** a seed that produces the same tree regardless of where it lands, with all habitat conditions measured and different. That would refute the transfer-by-shape claim and establish literal pathway portability.

## CONCLUSION

A pathway is a molecule in the figure's sense: atoms plus typed, partly ordered bonds, with the arrangement carrying information the ingredients do not. Two pathways across different habitats are **isomers** if they share atoms and differ in bond order, and members of one **functional group** if they share only the operation shape. The first is the strong claim, the second the weaker and more likely one. Order is load bearing: production and sequence are ordered bonds, and a list of ingredients cannot name a route. What crosses between habitats is the seed (the method), never the tree (the achieved arrangement), and the conditions at the landing site decide what it becomes.

This rescues what is defensible in "the same basic pathway works" without the part that fails: portability is of the generator and of the functional shape, not of the identical arrangement. Fig 11's habitat law and Fig 4's seed line are the two supports. The remaining strains, namely the molecule-to-pathway level move and the branching-network simplification, are named above rather than hidden.
