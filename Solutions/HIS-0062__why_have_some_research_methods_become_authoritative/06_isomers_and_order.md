# Union, order, and isomers in the authority question

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The explanation "some methods won because they were better" treats the asymmetry as one hard question. Two questions are owed: (1) is the residual a partition or a union of conditions, and (2) if it is a union, what is ordered and what is merely arranged differently? A third question follows: can two methods with the same ingredients be different in the figure's sense of *different substance*?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Partition or union?

Fig 9 :54: *"when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*

Authority here is a union. A field where D1 (ground contact) holds but D2 (mirror chain) fails produces a different failure from a field where D2 holds but D3 (habitat substituted) fails. One lever, say more rigorous training, clears the region where only D2 binds and leaves the D3 intersections standing. By Fig 9 :55-56, *"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single intervention moves methods from marginal to authoritative across all five domains at once, with no residue that is categorically different, the union reading fails and the partition reading stands.

### Q2 - What is ordered?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."* The order for a method is: perception (does it register the world?) → vocabulary (does it name what the situation affords?) → objective (can the field say what counts as progress?) → planning (is there a route the habitat permits?) → learn and carry (does the method cross up as a method?).

Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* This matters historically: the middle link, having terms for what a situation affords and saying what counts as progress, attracts the fewest instruments (Fig 3 :24-26). So a method can be authoritative at the planning link and unexamined at the objective link, which is why "the best method won" cannot be read off success alone.

**Falsifier:** if a chain that stops at the objective link (no agreed measure of progress) nevertheless produces ground-paid authority at the same rate as a chain that passes it, dependency order does not govern this case.

### Q3 - Are authoritative and marginal methods isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Take two methods over the same ingredient set: {intervention, control, outcome, population, time}. One assigns units to arms by random allocation (the randomized controlled trial). The other observes already-formed groups (a cohort study). Same atoms, different bond between population and arm, different substance. On a fresh question where allocation is the binding condition, they behave differently; in a formula that lists only ingredients they are indistinguishable. That is Fig 12's isomer, and it is exactly the historical case in the world sources: the RCT page reports that observational studies and RCTs *"overall produced similar results"* in two 2000 NEJM studies, while a 2001 JAMA study found *"discrepancies beyond chance do occur"*, including the hormone-replacement-therapy reversal. The disagreement is about the bond, not the ingredient list.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two methods with the same ingredient set but different bonds yield the same decision at the ground on every case where they are both admissible, the isomer claim fails and they are a single method under two names (Fig 8 :14-15: *"two names for one frame"*).

### Q4 - What is the functional group?

Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Across the methods that genuinely settle facts, the invariant substructure is not "experiment" or "quantitative." It is an anchor relation: a comparison that the field cannot move, with a per-slot residual. That is the functional group; randomization, blinding, blinding-plus-registration, or a physical counter are qualifiers naming members. This is why the shape answer refuses to name a single winning method while still predicting what any winner must contain.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a method that reliably settles the question at the ground without any unmoved reference point, where agreement does not do the work of a reference point. Under Fig 2 :29-30 that would be an anchorless verdict.

### Q5 - A catalyst method

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

A method that becomes authoritative often survives its own use: it trains its own practitioners, funds its own instruments, and reproduces its own habitat. A method that is spent by its use (a one-off technique tied to a single instrument or a single champion) behaves differently across occasions and is easily mistaken for the catalytic kind when only the results are recorded. Fig 12 :119 gives the distinction; nothing in the citation record automatically records which a method is.

**Falsifier:** show that a method whose habitat is not regenerated (no training pipeline, no instrument maintenance, no venue) sustains ground-paid authority across two independent generations.

### Q6 - Two currencies, do not add

Fig 12 :125-127: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add. Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

A method can be cheap to describe and expensive to settle (a short, elegant design needing a decade-long trial), or expensive to describe and cheap to refute (a long chain of qualitative judgments that one counter-observation breaks). Summing "influence" and "evidence" into one authority score is the error this names: the two readings come apart in both directions (Fig 12 :128-129).

## CONCLUSION

The authority residual is a union compound, ordered by dependency, in which the conditions compose and do not average. Authoritative and marginal methods can be isomers: same ingredients, different bond, different behaviour at the ground. The invariant substructure is an anchor relation, not a named technique. A method that survives its own use is a catalyst in Fig 12's sense, and a one-off technique is not, even when their publication records look alike. The two currencies, description cost and settlement cost, must be read apart.

This rescues what is defensible in "the best method won" without the part that fails: some methods do carry an unmoved reference point and a regenerative habitat. It does not license naming one winner, and it does not turn agreement into evidence.
