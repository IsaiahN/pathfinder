# Are the two reductions isomers? Order, analogues and a falsifier

**Mode: `specified` on the falsifiable structural claims, `general` on the shape. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading** (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The audit in `01` found that statistical predictability and behavioral effect are two currencies (Fig 12 :125-130) and that the question is a union compound (Fig 9 :57). Two supporting questions are owed: (1) under Fig 12's structural definitions, what exactly are these two measures to each other, and (2) does an order exist among the levels a measure can address, and what breaks if the order is reversed?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are the two measures isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- A statistical measure and a behavioral measure do **not** share an atom set. The first is built from `{signal, distribution, code}`; the second from `{signal, agent, response, task}`. By Fig 12 :117 they are **not isomers**. They are two members of a **functional group**, "a measure of a signal", with different atoms in the slots. Fig 12 :124 gives the exact reading: the group says what kind (a measure); the qualifier says which member (statistical or behavioral).
- The stronger form would require both measures over a full shared atom set `{signal, source, bond, interpreter, reading, response}` differing only in which bond is priced. That is a real isomer claim, and it is the one worth testing: same signal and source, different typed relation measured (predictability versus aboutness), different substance.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either measure can be written without an atom the other requires (for example a behavioral measure with no signal distribution, or a statistical measure with no interpreter), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2: Is there an order among the levels, and what breaks if it is reversed?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."* Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- The engineering source (Yang et al., opened this session) records Weaver's order: Level A technical (how accurately symbols are transmitted), Level B semantic (how precisely symbols convey the desired meaning), Level C effectiveness (how effectively received meaning affects conduct). This is a world source and corroborates only; the figure rule about dependency order is primary.
- Mapping by dependency: the semantic level consumes a signal that was transmitted; the effectiveness level consumes a meaning that was conveyed. Sharpening the effectiveness metric while the semantic link is unassessed is effort above the break; it arrives at a step that cannot use it (Fig 3 :20).
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* The semantic link in this question is the one with no source-discriminating instrument. That is where the chain stops.
- **Order verdict:** A before B before C by dependency, not by importance. A great technical measure does not rescue an unmeasured semantic link; a great behavioral score does not either. The two reductions in the question are attempts to skip from A straight to C, and Fig 3 names the failure.

### Q3: The animal-signalling analogue (what a behavioral assay can carry)

- The vervet alarm-call literature (opened this session) gives the cleanest available case. Price et al. 2015: the calls *"constitute the classic textbook example of semantic communication in nonhuman animals"* because acoustically distinct calls elicit appropriate responses. Macedonia and Evans coined **"functional reference"** for exactly this behavioral criterion: stimulus specificity at production and a differential response at reception.
- Fischer 2017 quotes the original caution: *"There are limits to how far a semantic analysis can be carried out when it is based solely on the responses that those signals evoke."* The later reanalysis found that structurally similar calls also occurred in aggression contexts, weakening the one-to-one call-to-context mapping.
- Mapping to the figures: the playback response is a legitimate anchor for "what did the signal make the monkey do" (Fig 2 :37, anchor legitimate when the question is constitutively about it). It is an instrument, not an anchor, for "what is the call about". Fig 2 :38: *"instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure."*
- The analogue is structural, not a claim about monkey minds. Actors: caller, call, listener, predator. Currency: adaptive response. Anchor: the ground (predator present or absent, in playback). This maps onto the question's behavioral frame and corroborates F4, without deciding what any animal means.

### Q4: What kind of thing would close the residual?

- Fig 6 :34: *"the search for a proto-instrument is a search for one that is already there."* The spectrometer in *The Hard Problem is About Source* is the instance: an existing instrument that discriminates sources behind one reading.
- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."** The instrument is a catalyst in the figure's sense: it survives its own use and lets the same reading be re-examined for source across many cases. A mere refinement of the reading (finer entropy estimate, larger behavioral sample) is a spent part that does not change contact (Fig 11 :40).
- Fig 13 :135: *"It cannot say which primitive is missing, because naming it would mean already holding it."* So this derivation gives the **shape** of the missing thing (source-discriminating, survives reuse, operates per slot) and stops. It does not name it.

## CONCLUSION

The two reductions are not isomers over their published atom sets. They are members of one **functional group**, "a measure of a signal", and the stronger isomer claim holds only over a full shared atom set with a different typed bond. The three levels stand in a dependency order: technical, semantic, effectiveness; reversing it is effort above a break. The animal-signalling analogue corroborates that a behavioral assay is a sound anchor for conduct and an instrument, with known blind axes, for aboutness. What would close the residual is a catalyst-like source-discriminating instrument, shaped here and not named, matching Fig 6 :34 and Fig 13 :135.
