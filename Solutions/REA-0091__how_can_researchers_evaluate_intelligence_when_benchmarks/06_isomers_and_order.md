# Union, order and score isomers: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The question bundles three ways a benchmark fails. Two sub-questions are owed before any remedy is chosen:

1. **Structure.** Is "memorized, gamed, obsolete" one problem with three intensities, a partition of three independent problems, or a union of overlapping conditions?
2. **Order.** Once the structure is fixed, in what order must the evaluation chain be repaired, and which link is actually broken?

A third, smaller question: can two benchmarks that report the same number still be different evaluations, in the sense Fig 12 gives to isomers?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: a score, a set of items, a ground, a verifier, a habitat, a residual per slot, a maintainer seat. Bonds held: sequence (→), production (⇒), subtraction (−), comparison (⋛), conjunction (+), and negation (¬) for refusals. No new instrument is available without an import from another closure.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :56: "A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."
- The three conditions share atoms but not causes. Memorization is a training-set contact failure; gaming is an optimization failure against a proxy; obsolescence is a resolution failure of the instrument. A private held-out set addresses the first, partly the second, not the third. Refreshing items addresses the third, not a leak of the generation code. The intersection (a private set whose generation rule leaks) is a compound with its own loop.
- ⇒ **Union**, not partition and not one problem. Three loops, then a compound loop for each nonempty intersection. Triangulated with Fig 8 :47-49, where independence is read twice, and with the *Library of Babel* line: "Perhaps your problem was a bundle or conflation of several problems masquerading as one?"

### Q2 — What is the order of repair?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- The evaluation chain: perception (can the instrument tell a capable answer from a memorized one?) → vocabulary (terms for what the task affords: per-slot capability, contamination, proxy) → objective (what would count as progress here, in our own terms?) → planning (which instrument to build or import?) → learning and carrying (record, verify, refresh).
- Fig 3 :24-26: the middle link is where chains break, because "having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress" has "neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments." Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
- Fig 3 :30: "Most improvement effort in practice lands above the break, because that is where the measurements already exist." In benchmark practice that is score precision, leaderboard size, more items and larger judge models. All of these sit above the break.
- ⇒ Repair order: (1) state the objective in effect terms per slot and bind it (Formula "Direction"); (2) install the verifier non-access condition and a private ground (Fig 10 :42); (3) choose per-slot instruments; (4) only then tune scores and add items. Effort at step 4 before step 1 is effort above the break. Triangulated for the ordering (Fig 3 plus Formula step 7); the identification of step 1 as the actual break in current practice is `specified`, not measured.

### Q3 — Are two equal scores the same evaluation?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance." Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart."
- Two evaluations can report 90 percent from the same item set and the same model, but with different bonds: one measured on a canaried private set under verifier non-access, the other on a public set the model may have trained on. Same atoms, different arrangement, different warrant. By Fig 12 they are **isomers**, not two spellings of one number.
- The stronger form: if the private set is withheld from the model but the public set is not, even the same items arranged under different access conditions are different molecules. Access is a bond, not an ingredient.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two evaluations with the same atoms but different access and refresh conditions are shown to have identical warrants in every case (no difference in what the score licenses), the isomer claim fails and the two are one term with two spellings. Under Fig 12 :94-96 this should not happen; the claim is stated so it can.

### Q4 — What is the direction of the loop, and what can it not see?

- Formula "Direction": "the shape a frame is searching for is not a term it holds." The evaluation system can enumerate its bound terms and rank them by residual. It cannot check whether it is aiming at capability or at score.
- Fig 5 :62-63: "A frame searching for an answer of a particular shape holds that shape as a PREFERENCE rather than as a term. It is not bound, it predicts nothing, and it accrues no residual, so nothing here can see it."
- ⇒ The remedy is a discipline, not a new step: write down what an acceptable evaluation would look like, bind it as an ordinary term, and let it accrue a residual like anything else. That is the pre-registered refuter in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §4. Committing the refuter before the run is this discipline already working.
- The seat, not the loop, decides the direction: Fig 10 :43 "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it." Fig 9 :51 "evidence bearing only on its maker is a stake-inducing channel, and the verifier is denied it."

### Q5 — Negation and the refusal

- Fig 12 :112: "A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond." Operators table :57-58: negation "this does not hold", and "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."
- An evaluation vocabulary that can only add benchmarks cannot carry the correction "this benchmark is dead." It can add a newer one beside it, leaving both scores live. So a protocol must include a refusal operator: retire the saturated instrument, with the reason and date recorded. Fig 10 :62: "A ruling carries provenance like an atom."
- Nothing is deleted from the record (Fig 6 :42-43); what falls is the reach of the retired score. That is why the provenance entry matters more than the deletion.

## CONCLUSION

The bundle is a **union** of three overlapping conditions, not a partition and not one hard problem (Q1). The repair is **ordered by dependency**: objective before verifier before instrument before score tuning, because the middle link has no instrument and most effort lands above the break (Q2). Two equal scores can be **isomers** under Fig 12 when their access and refresh bonds differ (Q3). The loop cannot check its own direction, so the sought-for shape must be written down and bound before the run, and a seat outside the frame must hold it (Q4). Refusals require negation, which is not a bond, so a saturated benchmark must be retired with provenance rather than merely supplemented (Q5).

This rescues what is defensible in "evaluate intelligence better": a checkable protocol shape with a stated order and a stated falsifier. It does not name a benchmark, and it is not grounded.
