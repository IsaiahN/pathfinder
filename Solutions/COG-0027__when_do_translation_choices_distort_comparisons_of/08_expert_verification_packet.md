# Expert verification packet - the Tether on translation and cross-cultural comparison

**What this is:** a claim in a form a translation studies scholar or cross-cultural methodologist can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `specified` on the conditions, `general` on the structural shape. Not `grounded`.** No cross-cultural measurement is claimed.

**Status:** a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading. It cannot settle the claim it reports on.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

A translation choice is a frame transform with a measurable round trip, not a neutral copy. A comparison of concepts across cultures is distorted when one of five conditions holds: the relation between terms changes while the ingredient lists look the same; the round trip is unmeasured; a declared equivalence is allowed to decide the verdict; the two frames draw on one evidence pool; or the signal is averaged across items until one broken term disappears. The framework does not name the correct equivalent for a given pair. It returns the shape a trustworthy comparison must have, with refuters written in advance.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1 - a term-for-term match can change the substance invisibly

- **Statement:** equivalence (≡, "two names, one referent") holds only if substituting the translated term loses nothing. A translation that preserves the words but changes the relation compares two different molecules as one.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Also Fig 12 :95-96 "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing." The Operators table gives the test: "substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."
- **What to check:** take one concept pair you know. Does a dictionary match preserve scope, sentiment and causal implication, or does it change the relation while keeping the words?
- **Refuter:** a controlled substitution that changes the relation, with a ground-anchored detector of adequate power, that leaves every downstream inference unchanged. Then the bond change is not load-bearing.

### C2 - the round trip is the measurement, and it is per item

- **Statement:** translation is the frame transform F, distinct from abstraction and concretisation, and it cannot be run without a declared pose. The reverse transform gives a round-trip gap R_T per term and per item. A comparison that does not report it is dead reckoning.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 4 :30 "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked." The Symbols table defines F and says "Cannot be run without a pose." Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."
- **What to check:** for one translated instrument, is there a semantic (not surface) round-trip measure, is it reported per item, and is the pose (speaker, audience, task) stated? Note the caveat from the 2026 round-trip benchmark: a clean final back-translation does not prove a clean intermediate step.
- **Refuter:** a term pair with a measured zero semantic round-trip gap that still diverges on a ground-anchored comparison, or item-level residuals shown to be independent and no better than the aggregate at predicting the ground.

### C3 - a convention that decides the outcome has stopped being a convention

- **Statement:** if changing the translation changes the reported cross-cultural difference, the translation is a frame contributing to the verdict, not an instrument. High overlap between the two frames makes them one frame with two names, so their agreement or difference is the evidence pool talking.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 10 :47 "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one." Fig 2 :29 "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks." Fig 8 :15 "two names for one frame." The anchor test is question-relative: Fig 2 :36 "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."
- **What to check:** vary the translation while holding everything else fixed. Does the "cultural difference" move? Also: do the two samples share source wording, adjudicator and theory? If they do, what independent evidence is left?
- **Refuter:** a translation varied with the variation independently confirmed, leaving the ground-anchored comparison unchanged; or two frames demonstrably sharing one pool that still produce a replicable ground-confirmed difference.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not a verdict about any culture, language pair or concept.
- Not that translation is impossible, and not that full equivalence must exist for useful translation.
- Not that agreement is always corrupt. Fig 2 :41 "efficient for a convention: agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it." The disqualification applies to verdicts about the world.
- Not that the six-term substrate is a cultural-concept taxonomy. That analogue was refused in `06_isomers_and_order.md`.
- Not grounded. Nothing here was settled by experiment, and no R_T was measured numerically in this run.
- Not a claim that any named equivalent is the framework's output. The framework returns a shape, not a name for this question.

## 5. What would settle it

A ground-anchored demonstration, not further agreement:

1. A substitution test on a concept pair where the relation was changed and a detector had power to see any consequential loss.
2. A semantic round-trip measure per item, with the pose declared and the intermediate step checked.
3. An anchor perturbation: vary the translation, hold the rest fixed, and see whether the cultural difference moves.
4. An independence audit of the two frames, with disagreement counted only where overlap was actually low.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 12 :5, Fig 4 :30, Fig 10 :47 and Fig 8 :15, four short passages.
2. Apply the Operators table identity test to one concept pair you know: substitute the translation and list what is lost.
3. Ask whether that pair has a measured per-item semantic round trip and a declared pose. Name an instrument that would supply one, or say why none is possible.
4. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
