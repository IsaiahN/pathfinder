## Q1 - Are make and buy atoms or bonds?

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :124)
> **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."** (Fig 12 :5)

The component list is the atom set. "Make" and "buy" are not extra atoms; they are different **bonds** connecting the same component to the firm's architecture. Making composes the component inside Γ; buying imports it from a differing closure (Fig 6 :21, :23). Two firms with an identical component list but different make-buy patterns are therefore **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with the difference invisible in the bill of materials and decisive in the supply chain.

If the two firms share only a pattern, for example "critical item retained, commodity item sourced", while differing in which atoms occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: they are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), the qualifier saying which member. The presence of the pattern fixes the kind, not the outcome.

The distinction is testable. A bill-of-materials comparison that finds no difference between two firms cannot distinguish isomer from functional-group; the bond has to be recorded. This is the figure-grounded version of Fine and Whitney's empirical observation that *"similar companies, facing similar choices, select make/buy patterns in very different ways, resulting in very different patterns of interdependencies along companies' supply chains"*: the atoms are the same, the bond is what differs.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two firms with identical atoms and identical make-buy bonds nevertheless diverge in boundary outcomes with no difference in ground contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D2/D3), which moves the explanation out of composition. **Status:** `specified`, triangulated for the legality of the reading (Fig 12 and Fig 6, different figures), single-source for the firm application. Fine and Whitney is a business/engineering-pool binding, not independent corroboration.

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20-21)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)
> **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** (Fig 3 :27)

Order for a firm facing a make-or-buy choice:

1. **Perception.** Can it read total landed cost, quality and delivery at all? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests: supplier quotes, internal cost accounting, delivery records.
2. **Vocabulary.** Does Γ have terms for what the component affords, and can it say what would count as progress there, in total cost of ownership rather than unit price? This is the middle link. Fig 3 :26: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."*
3. **Objective.** Can it state a comparison ⋛ that would count as getting somewhere? Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A vocabulary with only ingredient costs cannot state a goal.
4. **Planning.** A sourcing route under incomplete contracts, including hybrids and plural sourcing.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, carry the knowledge to the next component. Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."* A bought component is a head start; the method of making it is what can cross up.

The three conditions map onto this chain: C1 (composable and settling) is mostly a failure of links 2 and 3; C2 (gated import surplus) is mostly a failure of link 4 and of the source gate; C3 (capability retained) is a failure of link 5. That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear all three (Fig 9 :55).

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Link 1 has accounting and supplier-quote data; link 5 has almost none. So budget piles up at links 1 and 4 while the break sits at 2 and 5. The empirical literature's proxies, asset specificity, uncertainty and frequency, sit at links 1 and 4; the "hidden costs" of outsourcing (Reitzig and Wagner 2010, via Klein and Mazzoni 2025) sit at link 5.

**Falsifier:** a firm whose chain demonstrably works at 2, 3 and 5 and fails only at 4. That would falsify the "usually middle and carry" claim in `01`.

## Q3 - Decomposability: figure line or engineering binding?

Fine and Whitney's framework, recorded in Weinstein 2004, states that *"Once a clean decomposition has been found, the item can be outsourced if desired or necessary"* and that the risk of outsourcing depends on *"the degree to which the item for which one is dependent is decomposable from other items or activities"*. It also records that product development and manufacturing infrastructure are *"difficult to decompose because their tools and equipment are necessarily tightly linked to elements of the product, to key processes and learning activities in the firm, and to each other"*.

Is this a figure line? Partly. Fig 6 :21 says composition inside the closure cannot add an atom, and Fig 6 :23 says the wall moves only on contact with a differing closure. A clean decomposition is the condition under which an import does not drag the rest of the architecture with it; it is the engineering-frame name for a low-independence-debit import (Fig 6 :24). But the figures do not define modularity or give a decomposability measure. So:

- **Figure-derived:** the debit exists, and a wrong atom costs more than starting from primitives (Fig 6 :24).
- **Binding from the engineering frame:** "decomposability" and "modularity" as the instrument that estimates the size of that debit. It needs provenance and a ground test before it can count as more than a name.
- The same caution applies to *"no outsourcing or architecture modularity policy that seems suitable at a given time is likely to remain suitable"*: it corroborates Fig 11 :50 and Fig 12 :119 at the engineering stratum, but it is still one pool.

**Falsifier:** a documented case in which an item that is demonstrably non-decomposable is outsourced with zero independence debit and no degradation of the firm's make-capability. That would break the mapping from Fig 6 :24 to the engineering concept.

No candidate is discarded by prestige or familiarity. Each is ranged against the same residual, in the order Fig 8 :53 requires. The point is not to pick one; it is to notice that the first, second, third and fifth are all in overlapping pools, and that the one genuinely independent field corroborates the mechanism, not the firm-level fact.

## Q5 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the transaction-cost emphasis on governing specific assets, as C2 under a market anchor that pushes back (Fig 2 :29, :35);
- the core-competence emphasis on retaining certain activities, as Γ under a closure that has not met a differing closure (Fig 6 :21);
- Fine and Whitney's dependency distinction, as the Fig 6 :24 independence debit: dependency for capacity versus dependency for knowledge;
- the comparative-advantage emphasis on trade where closures differ, as Fig 8 :45;
- the hidden-cost emphasis on channel decay, as Fig 10 :24.

It cannot generate the *name* of the specific component or the specific supplier that will win next. That is the `gap` recorded in F9 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 (*"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*) and Fig 8 :39 apply. The essay's source rule states why: an instrument that reads a cost saving *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists"*.

## CONCLUSION

Make and buy are not atoms but different bonds joining the same component atoms to a firm's architecture, so two firms with identical component lists but different make-buy patterns are isomers under Fig 12 :117. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the vocabulary link and the carry link the usual breaks, which is why C1, C2 and C3 need different instruments. Decomposability is an engineering-frame binding for the size of the Fig 6 :24 debit, not a figure line; the debit is, the measure is not. The standard business explanations are mostly bindings of overlapping pools and are generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim, the order claim and the decomposability mapping have written falsifiers; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
