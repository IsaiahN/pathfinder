# WATSON answer: unequal treatment, inconsistent enforcement, and institutional legitimacy over time

**Mode: `specified`, not `grounded`.** Figures are primary and decide. World sources are secondary, corroborate but never overrule, and a conflict returns the derivation to the domain census. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading. No measurement was taken.

## QUESTION

How do unequal treatment and inconsistent enforcement affect institutional legitimacy over time? Restated in effect terms: why does R = |Γ(b,a) - o′| stay small in some subject-slots across repeated encounters and grow in others, when subjects expect like cases to be treated alike and the same rule to be applied consistently? R is indexed per subject and per group, never aggregated (Fig 1 :19). Option A: treat unfairness as a single dose and legitimacy as a stock the institution holds. Option B: treat the two as a union of overlapping conditions, legitimacy as a property of subject-and-habitat, and the time course as a signed loop with two separately named anchors. Each option leads to a different measurement design and a different remedy.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

R by arity, symmetry, scale: a three-place relation (subject expectation, institutional action, observed treatment), indexed by slot and ordered by time; it carries an ordered bond for succession and a comparison bond for "unequal", so it is not symmetric. Scale: the single encounter, the population of subjects sharing an institutional reputation, and the time channel across years. The cross-scale transform is lossy (Fig 4).

Reading: equal treatment and consistent enforcement are isomers, comparison across subjects versus sequence across occasions; one lever cannot clear their intersection; the institution's own report is disqualified. Mode `general` for the union, `specified` for institutions. Refuter: one intervention restoring both conditions with no residue, or one scalar representing both with no predictive loss.

**D3 Social habitat, status triangulated (Fig 11 valence and Fig 6 closure, low overlap).**

> *"An actor’s valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* (Fig 11 :49)
> *"The habitat is not the ground. The habitat is where actors come from and it updates; the ground is what settles, and it does not."* (Fig 11 :73)
> *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."* (Fig 6 :23)

Reading: the same enforcement act changes sign with the group's residual; renewal requires a disconfirming encounter from a differing closure. Mode `specified`. Refuter: invariant valence across a documented change of residual, or a group renewing an unfairness expectation with no disconfirming contact.

**D4 Temporal channel, status triangulated (Fig 10 channel and Fig 7 room chain, low overlap).**

> *"the ground does not decay, the channel does"* (Fig 10 :24)
> *"saturated metrics, pooled readings"* (Fig 10 :26)

Reading: quick erosion is channel failure; persistence is a chain of differing rooms; a loop is required to explain a residual that persists. Mode `specified`. Refuter: legitimacy falls while fidelity and ground pressure both measure high and non-declining, or cooperation demonstrably does not re-enter.

## WORLD (secondary, each source read)

World sources corroborate or conflict; they never decide. Overlap with other sources is declared, not hidden.

- **Social psychology of law.** Tom R. Tyler, *Psychological Perspectives on Legitimacy and Legitimation*, Annual Review of Psychology 57, 2006. Quote: *"Legitimacy is a psychological property of an authority, institution, or social arrangement that leads those connected to it to believe that it is appropriate, proper, and just."* Prediction: legitimacy is relational, not a possession. **Corroborates** D1. Overlap: high with the rest of the procedural-justice pool.
- **Cross-national justice institutions.** Jonathan Jackson, *Norms, Normativity, and the Legitimacy of Justice Institutions*, Annual Review of Law and Social Science 14, 2018. Quote: *"procedural justice is the strongest predictor of police legitimacy in most of the countries under investigation"*; the abstract adds that fair-process judgments may be displaced by effectiveness, corruption, crime scale, and an oppressive history. Prediction: procedure dominates but its sign depends on habitat. **Corroborates** D3 with a context caveat. Overlap: medium, same field, different data.
- **Meta-analysis.** Glenn D. Walters and P. Colin Bolger, *Procedural justice perceptions, legitimacy beliefs, and compliance with the law*, Journal of Experimental Criminology 15, 2019. Quote: *"the effect was relatively weak in this meta-analysis and could not be reliably established in longitudinal datasets"*; the mediated paths survive in the longitudinal subset. Prediction: the direct path is weak; the mediated loop remains. **Conflicts** with a naive direct-cause story; returned to census R2 and narrowed to a mediated claim (Fig 9 :60 treats the pooled effect as a filter). Overlap: high with Tyler and Mazerolle.
- **Policing interventions.** Lorraine Mazerolle, Sarah Bennett, Jacqueline Davis, Elise Sargeant and Matthew Manning, *Procedural justice and police legitimacy*, Journal of Experimental Criminology 9, 2013. Quote: *"for every single one of our outcome measures, the effect of legitimacy policing was in a positive direction, and, for all but the legitimacy outcome, statistically significant."* Prediction: dialogue with voice, neutrality, dignity and trustworthy motives moves legitimacy-related outcomes. **Corroborates** C2 renewal, with the legitimacy-outcome caveat visible. Overlap: high, same pool.
- **Political science, longitudinal panel.** Jan Menzner and David Schweizer, *The Underlying Structure of Political Support*, Politics and Governance 14, 2026. Quote: *"changes in any indicator tend to predict subsequent changes in others, indicating self-reinforcing dynamics"*; *"first-difference models offer limited support for a buffering 'reservoir of goodwill'."* Prediction: self-reinforcement, not a one-way reservoir. **Corroborates** the cycle in D4 and corrects a naive reservoir story. Overlap: low, different field and method.
- **Comparative judicial politics.** David De Micheli and Whitney K. Taylor, *Public Trust in Latin America's Courts*, Government and Opposition 59(1), 2024. Quote: *"personal experiences shape the trustworthiness of the court and that education conditions the impact of institutional quality"*; also *"confidence in institutions should not be interpreted as synonymous with institutional legitimacy"*. Prediction: per-group and per-experience effects; trust and legitimacy are distinct. **Corroborates** D1 and D2. Overlap: low, political science.
- **Animal behaviour, low-overlap field.** Sarah F. Brosnan and Frans B. M. de Waal, *Monkeys reject unequal pay*, Nature 425, 2003. Quote: *"Monkeys refused to participate if they witnessed a conspecific obtain a more attractive reward for equal effort, an effect amplified if the partner received such a reward without any effort at all."* Prediction: comparison registers before institutional vocabulary. **Corroborates** the comparison bond at a low stratum, not the institutional claim. Overlap: very low.
- **Animal behaviour, critique.** Clive D. L. Wynne, *Fair refusal by capuchin monkeys*, Nature 428, 2004. Quote: the result *"does not show that monkeys are averse to inequity, only that they reject a lesser reward when better rewards are available."* Prediction: the analogue may be a reward contrast, not fairness. **Conflicts** with a strong reading of the analogue; re-census narrows the analogue to a bond-availability claim.
- **Legal psychology working paper.** Tom R. Tyler and Justin Jackson, *Popular Legitimacy and the Exercise of Legal Authority*, Yale SSRN PDF, 2014 (PDF posted 2016). Quote: *"people’s view that the legal system is legitimate increases following a negative outcome, as long as people experience the procedures used by authorities as being fair"* (citing Tyler and Fagan 2008). Prediction: a disconfirming fair procedure renews legitimacy after a bad outcome. **Corroborates** D3 renewal. Direct PDF fetch was unsupported; text read through the search extractor.
- **Public interview, practitioner level.** University of Virginia School of Law podcast, *Why Fair Procedures Matter in Policing*, 2022, Tom R. Tyler. Quote: *"when the officer leaves, we want them to continue to follow the rules and that really comes from legitimacy."* Prediction: legitimacy buys acceptance beyond compliance. **Corroborates** the loop at practitioner level; not peer reviewed, direct fetch returned 403, text read through search extraction.

## VERDICT (from the figures)

The figure-derived verdict: unequal treatment and inconsistent enforcement are a union of overlapping conditions and stand as isomers, comparison across subjects versus sequence across occasions. Legitimacy is a property of subject-and-habitat. Quick erosion is channel failure rather than decay of fair treatment; persistence requires contact with a differing closure and a signed cycle in which cooperation re-enters as input. The same act changes sign with the group's residual. The institution's own legitimacy report is disqualified as a ground. Verdicts are kept separate per domain and never averaged. Which reform restores legitimacy in a given institution is a declared `gap`. Mode `specified`, ungrounded, candidate. One genuine world conflict (Walters and Bolger 2019) was returned to the census and produced a narrower mediated claim; the verdict did not flip.

- **cost if wrong:** misdirected diagnosis and a wasted instrument. A group failing C2 would be sold outcome-equality remedies, or a group failing C1 would be told to improve its channel, and a single lever would be applied to a union that needs two. In outreach terms, an expert's twenty minutes would be spent refuting a shape that was never the right shape. Fig 2 :42 prices the remedy: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."* The cost is real but bounded and largely reversible if the refuter is sent first.
- **undoable:** yes. No term is deleted if the claim fails. A refutation is added and the reach falls because *"something was added rather than because anything was deleted"* (Fig 6 :42-43). The shape and its eight falsifiers are recorded with provenance so a later reader can locate the error (Fig 10 :62-64).
- **reversed by:** any of the eight pre-registered falsifiers, in particular (1) legitimacy unchanged after a habitat substitution that removes the payment for fair treatment, (2) an aggregate index predicting per-group falls with no group-level data, (3) one intervention restoring both conditions with no residue, (4) legitimacy falling while fidelity and ground pressure both measure high, (5) invariant valence across a change of residual, (6) two decades of same closure with new ground-verified readings and no import, (7) cooperation demonstrably not re-entering, or (8) a break in the figures themselves. A source with a genuinely different evidence pool showing that per-group legitimacy is fully predicted by aggregate trust would also reverse it.
