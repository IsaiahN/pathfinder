## 0. The residual in effect terms

The loop defines R = |Γ(b,a) - o′| per slot s (Formula step 1; Symbols table, `R`). Here:

- **b** is the subject's belief about how the institution will treat this kind of case, formed from personal history and inherited prior. Symbols table :24: *"How far the belief leans on private history versus inherited prior."* Procedural justice research calls part of this legal socialisation and part of it direct experience.
- **a** is the institution's action: apply a rule, make a decision, allocate a benefit or burden, enforce or decline to enforce.
- **Γ(b,a)** is what the subject's library predicts will happen: the same rule applied to like cases, a neutral decision maker, voice before the decision, dignity in the interaction, trustworthy motives.
- **o′** is what actually happens to this subject and to comparable others: a different outcome for the same case, or a rule enforced this time and not last time.
- **s** is the person or the group, indexed per subject and per group and per encounter. It is never aggregated into one national "trust in institutions" number.

> **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."** (Fig 1 :19)
> **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."** (Fig 1 :22)

**Legitimacy in effect terms.** Legitimacy is the state of a slot whose residual stays small across successive handoffs: the subject continues to judge the institution appropriate and entitled to be obeyed even when a particular outcome is unwelcome. Erosion is a residual that persists and does not trend down across encounters. This is an effect description. It does not yet name a mechanism, a "social contract", a "reservoir of goodwill", or a "procedural justice effect". Those are candidate names and are withheld until the census.

**Bind the sought-for shape as an ordinary term** (Formula, "Direction"), before the run: the answer would be a union of two conditions with different instruments, plus an account of the channel by which a subject keeps contact with actual treatment over repeated encounters, and an instrument that discriminates a failure of one condition from a failure of the other. Until bound, that shape predicts nothing and no loop check can see it. It is now bound, so the refuter in section 4 can fire on it.

**Anchor test, with a genuine two-anchor split.** Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* Fig 2 :30: *"you push on it, it is not an anchor."* Two distinct questions are in play:

1. *Is this institution legitimate?* This question is constitutively about people's judgment, so people's judgment is the anchor, not merely an instrument: *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."* (Fig 2 :36) and *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people."* (Fig 2 :37).
2. *Does unequal treatment or inconsistent enforcement change that judgment over time?* This causal question needs a second anchor: an independent record of what was actually done, which does not move when the institution praises itself or when a survey respondent moves. Case files, observed treatment, and audited decisions are candidates. The institution's own legitimacy score is not, because *"A ground with a reputation to protect is a ground someone benefits from, and its keeper is thereby disqualified."* (Fig 10 :93).

Both anchors are kept separate in the verdicts. Neither is allowed to stand in for the other.

## 1. Domain census (before choosing any figure line)

### 1.1 Arity, symmetry, scale of R

- **Arity.** R is a three-place relation: subject expectation b, institutional action a, observed treatment o′, indexed by slot s and ordered by time t. It is a sequence, so it carries an ordered bond (`→`, sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, Fig 12 :72) and a comparison bond for "unequal" (`⋛`, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, Fig 12 :87). It is not symmetric: subject and institution are not interchangeable, and the effect of unequal treatment is not undone by reversing the treatment.
- **Symmetry.** "Unequal" is a threshold relation between like cases, not an absolute quantity. "Inconsistent" is variation across cases or time for one rule, which is a different axis from "unequal" across subjects. Persistence in time is asymmetric: lost legitimacy is not recovered simply by replaying the encounter that lost it.
- **Scale.** Three scales are in view: the single encounter (one subject, one decision), the population of subjects with a shared reputation for the institution (*"many agents, a shared library, and something that ranks what they produce"*, Fig 4 :5), and the time channel across years. The membrane between scales is *"Not a wall: a filter with a direction"* (Fig 4 :21), and *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."* (Fig 4 :22). The transform is lossy, so the round-trip gap R_T must be measured, not assumed (Fig 4 :30).

### 1.2 One line per domain: actors, currency, anchor

A domain is admitted only if an anchor can be named. Where none can be named, it is not a domain yet.

| domain | actors | currency | anchor, nameable? | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **D2 The rule and enforcement frame** | rule makers, enforcers, discretion, sanctions | uniformity of application, predictability, neutrality | yes: independent record of what was actually done, which does not move with the institution's own account (Fig 2 :29-30, Fig 10 :93) | admitted |

### 1.3 Partition or union

D1 and D3 overlap: the encounter is experienced inside a group habitat, and the same act has different valence for different groups. D2 and D4 overlap: enforcement records travel through the temporal channel. The whole question is a **union**, not a partition.

> **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."** (Fig 9 :54)
> **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."** (Fig 9 :55)
> **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."** (Fig 9 :57)

The unbundled conditions are:

- **C1 (ground condition):** treatment is actually equal across like cases and enforcement is actually consistent across cases and time. Measured in D2 against the independent record.
- **C2 (channel condition):** the subject's contact with that treatment stays reachable over time, and the subject's reach is renewed by contact with a closure that differs from the one that produced the unfairness expectation. Measured in D1, D3, and D4.

A group suffering C1 alone and a group suffering C2 alone are not different degrees of one failure. They are different compounds and need different instruments. Unequal treatment and inconsistent enforcement are themselves overlapping conditions: unequal treatment is a comparison across subjects, inconsistent enforcement is a comparison across occasions for the same rule. A case in both conditions is a specific compound (Fig 9 :57), not a bigger dose of unfairness.

### 1.4 Dependency order (Fig 3)

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** (Fig 3 :20)
> **"Why the middle link is where chains usually break."** (Fig 3 :24)
> **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** (Fig 3 :27)

Order for this question: 1 perception (can the subject tell that treatment differed?) to 2 vocabulary (does the subject have terms for voice, neutrality, dignity, motive?) to 3 objective (can the subject say what would count as fair treatment here?) to 4 planning (comply, contest, exit) to 5 learn and carry (legal socialisation, institutional memory). The middle link is the one with no clean instrument: procedural justice research supplies a vocabulary, but the question of what counts as a failure in this particular institution is still measured by self-report. Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* Effort lands above the break because those measurements already exist (Fig 3 :29).

## 3. Findings

Mode tag on each: `general` if structural, `specified` if a falsifiable prediction about institutions, `grounded` only if settled by a measurement. None is `grounded`.

### F1 - Legitimacy is a property of subject-and-institution, not of the institution alone

**Mode: `specified`. Domains D1, D2. Status: triangulated (Fig 11 and Fig 4, low overlap: habitat relations versus scale membrane).**

> **"So the answerable question is not what intelligence is, but what makes a system capable. And capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."** (Fig 11 :39)
> **"A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."** (Fig 11 :40)
> **"Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."** (Fig 4 :20)

An institution cannot hold legitimacy as a stock independent of the people judging it and the habitat in which they judge. A reform that changes the institution's internal rules without changing what subjects actually experience may be an agent-only improvement that changes no contact. The world supplies a careful definition at the same shape: Tyler 2006 defines legitimacy as *"a psychological property of an authority, institution, or social arrangement that leads those connected to it to believe that it is appropriate, proper, and just"* (abstract, Annual Review of Psychology 57). That is a relation between authority and those connected to it, not a possession of the authority. De Micheli and Taylor 2024 find that *"personal experiences shape the trustworthiness of the court and that education conditions the impact of institutional quality"* (Government and Opposition 59(1)), which is the per-subject contact claim at the courts stratum.

**Refuter:** a documented institution whose legitimacy, measured per subject and per group, is unchanged across a habitat substitution that demonstrably removes the ground's payment for fair treatment. If it survives, F1 is false.

### F2 - The anchor is the per-subject judgment, not the aggregate trust score or the institution's own report

**Mode: `general` with a `specified` consequence for measurement. Domains D1, D2. Status: triangulated (Fig 2 anchor rule, Fig 1 per-slot indexing, Fig 10 reputation warning; three distinct roles).**

> **"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."** (Fig 2 :36)
> **"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people."** (Fig 2 :37)
> **"The gap is measured per object, not per step. A system that averages across objects has thrown away the only signal that says where to look."** (Fig 1 :28)
> **"saturated metrics, pooled readings"** (Fig 10 :26)
> **"A ground with a reputation to protect is a ground someone benefits from, and its keeper is thereby disqualified."** (Fig 10 :93)

Aggregate "trust in institutions" is a pooled reading. It answers a different question from the one asked here, and it erases the group-level signal that says where legitimacy is failing. An institution's own legitimacy report has a reputation to protect and is disqualified as a ground (Fig 10 :59-60 requires the interest to be declared, not hidden). The procedural justice literature itself warns against the conflation: De Micheli and Taylor write that *"confidence in institutions should not be interpreted as synonymous with institutional legitimacy"* (2024, citing Driscoll and Nelson 2018). That is a world-source corroboration of the figure split, not a figure line.

**Refuter:** a single aggregate trust index that predicts prospectively, per named group, which group's legitimacy will fall next and why, with no group-level measurement used in the prediction. If one exists, the per-slot requirement is weakened.

### F3 - Unequal treatment and inconsistent enforcement form a union of overlapping conditions, not one dose of unfairness

**Mode: `specified`. Domains D2, D3. Status: triangulated (Fig 9 union rule and Mazerolle et al. 2013 four-component decomposition, low overlap: framework versus policing interventions).**

> **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."** (Fig 9 :55)
> **"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."** (Fig 9 :55-56)
> **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."** (Fig 9 :57)

The four components of procedurally just dialogue in the systematic review literature are citizen participation or voice, perceived neutrality, dignity and respect, and trustworthy motives (Mazerolle et al. 2013, abstract). Neighbourliness of treatment across groups and consistency of application across occasions are not the same instrument. A reform that fixes unequal outcomes while leaving discretionary enforcement arbitrary clears C1 only and leaves the intersection standing. This is the figure-grounded reason to keep the two verdicts separate.

**Refuter:** one intervention that restores both conditions in both failure types, measured per group and per occasion, with no categorically different residue left over. That would collapse the union into a partition.

### F4 - Quick erosion is a failure of the channel, not a decay of the ground

**Mode: `specified`. Domain D4. Status: triangulated (Fig 10 ground maintainer and Fig 7 room chain, low overlap: channel decay versus handoff factors).**

> **"the ground does not decay, the channel does"** (Fig 10 :24)
> **"stale percepts, dropped transients,"** (Fig 10 :25)
> **"saturated metrics, pooled readings"** (Fig 10 :26)
> **"you have lost touch"** (Fig 10 :29)
> **"→ keep the anchor reachable"** (Fig 10 :29)
> **"Progress per handoff has two factors. Zero either and the chain runs in place:"** (Fig 7 :37)
> **"the bracket’s fidelity across the handoff"** (Fig 7 :38)
> **"the ground’s pressure on the room"** (Fig 7 :39)

An institution can keep treating people equally and consistently while the channel that tells subjects about it decays: cases stop being visible, a few memorable incidents stand for the whole, aggregate statistics saturate, and group-level readings are pooled. Legitimacy falls quickly even though the ground did not decay. Tyler's two-stage model is the world-level shape of the same channel: judgments about the justice of experiences influence views about legitimacy, and those views shape compliance and cooperation (Tyler 1990, chapter 1; UVA Law podcast transcript, 2022).

**Refuter:** a documented case in which a group's legitimacy falls while bracket fidelity and ground pressure both measure high and non-declining on independent instruments. If both stay high, F4's mechanism is not the cause.

### F5 - The same enforcement act changes valence with the residual

**Mode: `specified`. Domains D2, D3. Status: triangulated (Fig 11 habitat relations and Fig 12 catalyst structure, low overlap: social valence versus composition role).**

> **"An actor’s valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."** (Fig 11 :49)
> **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."** (Fig 11 :50)
> **"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."** (Fig 11 :51)
> **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** (Fig 12 :119)

A stop, a fine, or a neutral adjudication is not intrinsically legitimacy-building or legitimacy-destroying. Its sign depends on whether the group experiencing it expects equal and consistent treatment from that institution. Jackson 2018 gives the low-overlap world corroboration: *"procedural justice is the strongest predictor of police legitimacy in most of the countries under investigation"* (Annual Review of Law and Social Science 14:145-165). The same abstract adds, without using the figure's vocabulary, that fair-process judgments can be displaced in some contexts by effectiveness concerns, corruption, crime scale, and an oppressive history. That is the same act, different valence by habitat, observed cross-nationally. De Micheli and Taylor 2024 corroborate at a different stratum: institutional quality affects only the best-educated respondents, so the same institution reads differently to different groups.

**Refuter:** an enforcement act whose measured valence is invariant across a documented change in the affected group's residual. If no such change of valence can be found, F5 weakens to `general`.

### F6 - Legitimacy persists as a chain of differing rooms; internal agreement compounds, evidence does not

**Mode: `specified`. Domain D4. Status: triangulated (Fig 7 selection-between-rooms and Fig 2 mirror warning, low overlap).**

> **"same closure, handed along;"** (Fig 7 :12)
> **"agreement compounds, evidence does not"** (Fig 7 :13)
> **"A room’s agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes."** (Fig 7 :35)
> **"A chain of mirrors is one long room. The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced."** (Fig 7 :36)
> **"The chain approaches the truth only as the world runs out of ways to refuse it. Which means the next room you book is the direction you travel, and booking a room like the last one is how a chain stops travelling at all."** (Fig 7 :42)

An institution that keeps repeating the same kind of review, the same kind of public statement, and the same kind of encounter is handing itself the same room. Agreement inside it compounds while evidence does not. The world's "reservoir of goodwill" idea (Easton 1965, quoted by De Micheli and Taylor 2024: *"a reservoir of favorable attitudes or good will that helps members to accept or tolerate outputs to which they are opposed"*) is often read as legitimacy survives repeated disappointments. That is not a conflict with Fig 7 once the objects are separated: the reservoir describes the persistence of an attitude (Fig 10 channel), whereas Fig 7 governs whether a checking chain gains evidence. Different objects, so no strain after re-census (strain S1, section 6).

**Refuter:** an institution that keeps the same closure for two decades and still produces new, ground-verified readings of how it is perceived, without any import. Under Fig 7 :36 that should be impossible; if observed, F6 is wrong.

### F7 - Renewal of expectation requires contact with a differing closure

**Mode: `specified`. Domain D3. Status: triangulated (Fig 6 import rule, Fig 8 idempotence, Fig 13 closure, three distinct roles).**

> **"the only operator that moves the wall"** (Fig 6 :14)
> **"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."** (Fig 6 :21)
> **"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."** (Fig 6 :23)
> **"Closure is idempotent. Composing inside a closed set never leaves it, so no search, however long, adds an atom. This one is definitional: it is what closure means."** (Fig 8 :32)
> **"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."** (Fig 13 :115)

A group whose only experience is unequal treatment holds a closure that predictably returns "this institution is unfair". No amount of internal reframing inside that closure adds the disconfirming atom. What can move the expectation is an actual encounter with a different closure: the same institution applying the rule neutrally, with voice and dignity, especially when the outcome is unwelcome. Tyler and Fagan (2008) report exactly that shape in the world: *"people’s view that the legal system is legitimate increases following a negative outcome, as long as people experience the procedures used by authorities as being fair"* (SSRN working paper, quoted from the PDF text). Mazerolle et al. 2013 report that *"for every single one of our outcome measures, the effect of legitimacy policing was in a positive direction, and, for all but the legitimacy outcome, statistically significant"* (Journal of Experimental Criminology 9:245-274). The caveat on the legitimacy outcome itself is kept visible: it is the weakest of the four in that review.

**Refuter:** a group whose expectation of unfairness is renewed purely by internal composition, with no disconfirming contact across a documented period and no import, and whose subsequent judgment is nonetheless ground-verified as fair. That breaks idempotence and F7 with it.

### F8 - Legitimacy is a closed loop whose sign decides whether it holds or collapses

**Mode: `specified`. Domains D1, D4. Status: triangulated (Fig 12 cycle and Menzner and Schweizer 2026 longitudinal panel, low overlap: framework versus political-science panel).**

> **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."** (Fig 12 :121)
> **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."** (Fig 12 :112)

Compliance and cooperation are outputs that re-enter as inputs: a subject who complies with a neutral decision has a further encounter with the institution, which is another reading against the ground. A chain cannot close by construction, so a legitimacy process that is only a chain of encounters cannot explain persistence; a loop is required. Menzner and Schweizer 2026, using 29 waves of the German Longitudinal Election Study, find that *"changes in any indicator tend to predict subsequent changes in others, indicating self-reinforcing dynamics"* and that *"first-difference models offer limited support for a buffering 'reservoir of goodwill'"* (Politics and Governance 14). That corroborates the cycle and corrects a naive one-way reservoir story. It is treated as corroboration of the loop shape, not as a figure line and not as a ground settlement of this question.

**Refuter:** a documented legitimacy trajectory in which compliance and cooperation do not re-enter as inputs to later judgments, with the loop demonstrably open. If observed, F8 fails as a loop claim.

### F9 - A genuine conflict, and the re-census it forced

**Mode: `general`. Status: figure-governed; world source conflicts, verdict narrows.**

Walters and Bolger 2019, a meta-analysis of 64 studies and 95 samples, conclude that *"while procedural justice perceptions also appear to predict compliance, the effect was relatively weak in this meta-analysis and could not be reliably established in longitudinal datasets"* (Journal of Experimental Criminology 15:341-372). Read naively, that seems to conflict with the Tyler-style chain "unfair treatment erodes legitimacy which erodes compliance over time".

The conflict is not used to flip the verdict. It sends the derivation back to the census (re-census R2, section 6). What the paper actually reports is that the path from procedural justice to legitimacy and the path from legitimacy to compliance survive in the longitudinal subset, while the direct procedural-justice-to-compliance path drops out. That is precisely the mediated loop the figures predict (Fig 12 :121), and it is not a claim the figures ever made about a direct path. The conflict is resolved by narrowing the claim to a mediated loop and treating the pooled effect size as what it is: an average across slots.

> **"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."** (Fig 9 :60)

The meta-analysis is a filter for where to look and a budget instrument, not the verdict. No verdict changed, but the claim is now more precise than it was before this source was read.

## 4. Pre-registered refuter (written before the conclusion)

Per the design-instrument rule: *"a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."* The shape claim is refuted if any of the following is observed:

1. Legitimacy, measured per subject and per group, is unchanged across a habitat substitution that removes the ground's payment for fair treatment (breaks F1, Fig 11 :39-40).
2. An aggregate trust index predicts prospectively, per named group, which group's legitimacy will fall next, using no group-level measurement (breaks F2, Fig 1 :22).
3. One intervention restores both conditions in both failure types with no categorically different residue (breaks F3, Fig 9 :57).
4. A group's legitimacy falls while bracket fidelity and ground pressure both measure high and non-declining (breaks F4, Fig 7 :37-39).
5. An enforcement act's measured valence is invariant across a documented change of the affected group's residual (breaks F5, Fig 11 :49-51).
6. An institution keeps the same closure for two decades and still produces new, ground-verified readings without any import (breaks F6 and F7, Fig 7 :36, Fig 8 :32).
7. Compliance and cooperation demonstrably do not re-enter as inputs to later legitimacy judgments (breaks F8, Fig 12 :121).
8. Fig 13's six terms are derived from five of them, or Fig 8's idempotence fails inside a closed set (breaks the figures themselves, not just this reading).

No part of this solution may be upgraded to `grounded` until at least one of these has been run and has failed to fire, and until an independent record of treatment plus per-subject judgment has paid for the shape.

## 5. Recommendation

1. Lead with the residual and the refuter, not with a theory of legitimacy (Fig 10 :52, *"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."*).
2. Keep equal treatment and consistent enforcement as separate verdicts in every report. A blended "fairness index" is a pooled reading (Fig 10 :26) and destroys the diagnostic.
3. Measure per subject and per group. Do not report a single trust score as if it settled the question (Fig 1 :19).
4. Name the two anchors separately in any study design: per-subject judgment for the legitimacy question, independent record of treatment for the causal question (Fig 2 :36-37, Fig 10 :93).
5. Ask at each handoff which link the chain stops at and whether that was measured (Fig 3 :29). Do not fund above the break.
6. Use the procedural-justice literature as a set of budget filters and as one corroborating witness, not as the verdict. Tyler, Mazerolle, Walters, Jackson, and De Micheli and Taylor draw on overlapping literatures (Fig 2 :31, :40).
7. Declare the institution's stake in its own legitimacy rating, and deny that rating a verdict role (Fig 10 :59-60, :93).
8. Offer the formula as a method only after a verdict is recorded (Fig 4 :22). Each user writes their own refuter and states their own mode.
9. Stop at the shape. Do not name which reform a specific institution should adopt; the framework returns *"a kind of thing, never the answer itself"* (Fig 9 :32).

## 8. Updated verdict

The shape survives. **Unequal treatment and inconsistent enforcement do not act as two doses of unfairness. They are overlapping conditions in a union. Legitimacy is a property of subject-and-institution, and it erodes quickly when the channel to actual treatment decays even if the institution keeps treating people fairly. It persists when that channel stays reachable and when subjects meet a differing closure that disconfirms the expectation of unfairness. The same enforcement act changes sign with the group's residual, and the resulting compliance re-enters as input to the next judgment, so the loop can hold or collapse.** The two conditions are kept separate and never averaged. Which specific reform restores legitimacy in a given institution is a `gap`, not a hidden answer. Mode `specified`. No world source flipped a verdict; the one genuine conflict (Walters and Bolger 2019) was returned to the census and produced a narrower, mediated claim. No unresolved strain remains after re-census.
