## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are E and C isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Under a coarse vocabulary where S is just "signal", both arrangements use {A, P, S, L, F}. E bonds S by production; C bonds P by comparison. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. **They are isomers** in the figure's sense.
- Under a fine vocabulary where "contribution" and "preference" are distinct atoms, E requires an atom that C does not. Then they are not isomers. Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." E and C then share a functional group, for example "principal-to-agent signal", with different qualifiers.
- Which reading is correct depends on whether a contribution can be written as a preference under a single code. That is a measurement question about the instruments, not a question the figures decide.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either arrangement requires an atom that the other cannot be written with under an agreed code, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. If both can be written with the same atoms under that code, the functional-group reading is the weaker fall-back and the isomer claim survives as `specified`.
- **Verdict: `specified`, single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12), unmeasured at the ground.** Do not upgrade to triangulated by appealing to the political-science literature; that literature does not use Fig 12's bond vocabulary.

### Q2 - What order does the bond vocabulary require?

- Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."
- Fig 12 :112: "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."
- A list containing only "constituents", "donors", "party", "interest groups" can name what a situation contains. It cannot state what would count as getting somewhere from it.
- To write responsiveness at all, the vocabulary needs: ⋛ for the goal (what the constituency wants versus what the representative did), − for progress (the gap), → for the route (the sequence of institutional changes), and ¬ for the refusal of a claim that the gap has closed when it has not.
- Consequence for F3 in the audit: an instrument that only reports a level ("approval 52 percent") has a spectrum but no comparison, no subtraction and no route. It cannot settle responsiveness. This is why F3 requires an unmovable anchor, not just a number.

### Q3 - What is the order across scales?

- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world".
- A first-order congruence reading, once settled against the ground, becomes an atom at the institutional level. The second-order object is the selection rule that uses several such readings to choose among rooms. It needs no new primitive.
- Fig 7 :36: "The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced." The second-order rule is therefore not "more of the same measurement"; it is selection between differing readings against the same ground.
- **Falsifier:** show that a settled congruence reading cannot serve as an operand for an institutional selection rule, so the second-order composition is impossible. Then Q3 fails and the institutional claims in `07` lose their recursion.

### Q4 - Does the comparison bond have a type problem?

- The Operators table defines ⋛ as "a threshold relation between two quantities". Here the operands are a constituency preference and an action, which are quantities at the district-policy slot. That fits at first order.
- At second order the operands are settled readings now serving as atoms. Whether they count as quantities at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. **Named strain, not hidden.** Same strain the exemplar records for ⋛ at second order; here it is recorded, not repaired.

## CONCLUSION

Under a coarse atom vocabulary the two arrangements are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance (Fig 12 :117). Under a fine vocabulary they are members of one **functional group** with different qualifiers (Fig 12 :124). The choice is an empirical question about the instruments and is left open, with a falsifier for each. What is not open is the bond requirement: a vocabulary of nouns cannot state responsiveness at all. A goal needs a comparison, progress a subtraction, a route a sequence, and a correction a negation.
