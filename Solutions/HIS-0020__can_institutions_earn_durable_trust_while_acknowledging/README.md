## The answer in one line

Institutions earn durable trust not by projecting certainty but by staying disqualifiable: they keep a ground that does not update, host frames that are independent rather than merely agreeing, install what can be violated, declare their stake, and keep a record in which a wrong ruling can be located and corrected. Uncertainty and disagreement are the raw material of that mechanism, not a tax on it. The framework returns that shape, not a name of a policy.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree that institutions can be trusted.
- No bulk-send plan.
- No claim that any named institution has or lacks trust, and no measurement of trust.

Three figure-grounded reasons:

1. **A room does not upgrade the label.** Seat Map :283: *"A room reaching a conclusion without the ground is agreement, and agreement among frames is a weighted average of influence. Adding people raises the vocabulary and does not add an anchor."*
2. **Selecting for agreement disqualifies the verdict.** Seat Map :163: *"High correlation is disqualifying for a verdict and efficient for a convention."* A list chosen for receptiveness manufactures the correlation that would make its agreement worthless.
3. **Trust cannot be self-issued.** Fig 1 :26-27: *"predicates minted, and credibility accrued"* and *"They are frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."* An institution asking to be trusted on the strength of its own trust score is the named failure.

Legally: unsolicited bulk outreach to personal addresses breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules in many jurisdictions. Public opt-in channels avoid that practical failure and the structural one.
