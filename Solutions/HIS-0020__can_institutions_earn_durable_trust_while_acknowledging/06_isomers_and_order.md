# Institutional isomers and the order of the conditions

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The shape claim in `01` says trust depends on three conditions: ground contact (C1), independence under disagreement (C2), and correctability (C3). Two questions are owed before the shape can be checked: (1) what is the **order** among them, since a condition applied out of order may do nothing, and (2) are two institutions that hold the same formal atoms but arrange them differently **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or merely members of one functional group? Without an answer to (2), a comparison of institutions will treat a difference of arrangement as a difference of substance, or the reverse.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in Γ for this derivation: a ground that does not update; independent frames; an unauthored pool; a declared stake; a violation that can be installed; a provenance record; a room changed with the question. ## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the dependency order, and what breaks first?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 10 :16-17: *"the convention does not fix the bug"*, *"it makes the bug statable"*.
- Fig 10 :18-19: *"a declared meaning is not a grounded one"*, *"→ install what can be violated"*.

Order: **statability → violability → independence → ground contact → correction → carry**. An institution cannot install what can be violated until the failure is statable; cannot have independent frames until the pool is unauthored (Fig 10 :73-74); cannot keep ground contact if the ground has been made a party (Fig 2 :47); cannot correct without provenance (Fig 10 :62-66).

The middle link is statability, and it is the one without an instrument. Most reform effort lands after it, on disclosure templates and review calendars, because those are measurable. Fig 3 :29: *"Before asking whether a system is good, ask which link it currently stops at, and whether the answer was measured or assumed."* Prediction: an institution that fails at statability will look like it is failing at communication, and will be offered communication training, which Fig 3 :19 says cannot rescue the broken link.

### Q2: Does trust compose first or second order?

- Fig 13 :74 (as recorded in the exemplar's figure census): *"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."* Cross-kind composition is the figure's legal move; for an institution the analogue is not a physical kind but the crossing between a claim and the ground that settles it.
- Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"* (the full figure line ends *"and there is no bottom"*).

A claim settled against the ground is a first-order molecule. A trust judgment over many settled claims is a second-order composition: each settled claim becomes an atom at the institutional level. This is why claim-level accuracy does not compose automatically into institutional trust, and why `R_T` from the first level to the second must be measured, not assumed (Fig 4 :30). What is lost in the carry is exactly the per-claim provenance when claims are pooled into a score (Fig 1 :22, *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*).

### Q3: Are two institutions with the same charter isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Take two institutions with the same written charter, the same number of reviewers and the same published uncertainty policy. Atoms are identical. If one binds the reviewer to the ground and the other binds the reviewer to the institution's own standing, the arrangements differ and, by Fig 12 :117, so does the substance. The charter alone will not show it; the behaviour will. They are **isomers**.

The weaker reading is that both merely share a functional group, the `claim → ground` crossing, with different qualifiers. Under Fig 12 :123 that makes them two members of one kind rather than isomers. The stronger isomer claim requires both institutions to hold the full shared atom set, including the unauthored pool, the declared stake and the correctable record, and to differ only in which atom mediates which relation. That is the claim worth testing, because it is the one where arrangement, not resources, explains the difference in durability.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two institutions can be shown to hold the same full atom set with the same arrangements, and still differ in durability, the isomer claim fails and the difference lies outside the held vocabulary, which sends us back to the census for a missing atom. Conversely, if a difference in arrangement is found with no difference in durability, Fig 12 :117 is not governing this domain and the isomer framing is withdrawn.

### Q4: What is the union's order?

- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- Fig 2 :42: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."*

C1, C2 and C3 are not three doses of one remedy. They are three conditions of one union. C1 without C2 gives a ground with only one reader, so no triangulation. C2 without C1 gives argument with nothing to range against, which is Fig 2 :13-15's coherent drift. C3 without either gives a clean record of ungrounded rulings, which Fig 10 :66 calls *"an unimpeachable record of nothing"*.

The order is also a budget order. Fig 2 :42 says independence is bought in proportion to the cost of being wrong. An institution whose rulings are cheap to reverse needs little independence; one whose rulings are expensive needs a lot. That is a filter for where to spend, never a witness for whether the institution is trustworthy (Fig 9 :60).

### Q5: Analogues, and where they stop

- **Safety engineering (low overlap).** PSNet 2024: *"uncertainty is endemic, and no two accidents are exactly alike"*; Weick and Sutcliffe, quoted in Hopkins 2007: *"The signature of an HRO is not that it is error-free, but that errors don't disable it."* This corroborates the order: preoccupation with failure before resilience, deference to expertise before hierarchy. The analogue stops at the anchor: an aircraft carrier's ground is a physical accident rate, an institution's ground is a per-claim record that has to be reconstructed.
- **History of science (same field as the question).** Oreskes 2019: reliability comes from *"the collective or social process of critical scrutiny"* and from verification practices. This corroborates C2 and C3. It stops at independence: Oreskes's consensus is a social fact about a community; Fig 2 :40 requires evidence-pool independence before agreement means anything about the world. The two must not be treated as one witness twice.

## CONCLUSION

The conditions have a dependency order, with statability as the link that usually stops the chain. Claim-level settlement is first order and institutional trust second order, with a nonzero `R_T` that pooling into a score destroys. Two institutions with the same charter but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117; the weaker functional-group reading is available when only the `claim → ground` crossing is shared. The falsifiers above are stated in advance. What remains unstated is which arrangement wins in a given field: that is the `gap` from F8, and the instrument that would close it is a source discriminator, not a further analogy.
