# Are rationality, creativity and autonomy isomers? Union, bond and dependency order

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The question bundles three named assumptions into one lever: rationality, creativity, autonomy. Two derivations are owed. (1) Is this a partition of three independent effects or a union compound with its own loop? (2) When a culture bonds these atoms into a definition of intelligence, does the definition become a different substance or merely a different spelling of the same one? A third, smaller question follows: what is the dependency order, so effort is spent on the link that actually breaks?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: rationality, creativity, autonomy, convention, agreement, habitat, contact, residual. Bonds held: the seven (conjunction, disjunction, identity, sequence, production, subtraction, comparison) plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. The three named atoms are already in the vocabulary; the question is which bond arranges them, not whether to invent a fourth.

## Q1 - Partition or union?

Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 9 :55-58: a bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, a single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, and what remains is *"categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*

The three assumptions are not independent. A culture can privilege reasoning in one setting, novelty in another and self-direction in a third, and the intersections are where the definitional work happens: "creative autonomy", "rational creativity". Treating "cultural assumptions" as one variable will clear a single-condition region and leave the compound intact. Hence **union**, and the union has its own loop (one per compound, verdicts never averaged).

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** one cultural variable that predicts all three definitional effects with no interaction terms. If it holds, this is a partition and a single loop is correct.

## Q2 - Isomers or a functional group?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Two readings, kept separate:

- **Weak reading.** Culture A's definition uses rationality plus creativity, culture B's uses rationality plus autonomy. They share only one atom. By Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**; they are two members of the same functional group, where the shared substructure fixes the kind ("a definition of intelligence") and the qualifier fixes which member.
- **Strong reading.** Both definitions are written over the full shared atom set {rationality, creativity, autonomy, convention, habitat, contact}, differing only in which bond mediates which pair and which atom is treated as the ground. Then they are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour in the world. This is the claim worth testing, because it predicts that two cultures can hold the same terms and arrive at definitions that behave as inverses of one another.

Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."* Two cultures can write "intelligence = reasoning, novelty, self-direction" and mean different things; the ingredient list is not the definition. The bond is the missing half.

- **Falsifier:** if either definition can be written without an atom the other requires (for example autonomy absent with no replacement), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. This is the same falsifier form as the exemplar's isomer test, applied to a different subject.

## Q3 - Dependency order: where a definition actually breaks things

Fig 3 orders links by dependency, not importance: perception, vocabulary, the objective, planning, learn and carry. Fig 3 :19-20: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not. The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*

Fig 3 :25-27 locates the break for this question:

> **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments. A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

A definition of intelligence is exactly the vocabulary-to-objective link. It supplies terms for what a situation affords ("rational", "creative", "autonomous") and a comparison for what counts as progress ("more intelligent"). It has no instrument of its own. That is why cultural assumptions can enter there invisibly, and why an argument about the definition can run forever without touching the link below it. Effort spent refining a definition (above the break) cannot rescue a missing measure of contact (below it).

- **Falsifier:** an instrument that measures the definitional link itself, so that a cultural difference in definitions produces a distinguishable reading without reference to any capability outcome. That would move the link above the break and invalidate this placement.

## Q4 - A list of atoms cannot name a route

Fig 12 :111-113: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."** *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."* A system holding only ingredients can say what a situation contains and cannot say what would count as getting somewhere from it.

A definition of intelligence that lists rationality, creativity and autonomy as traits is a flat formula: it names a state. It cannot say how a person gets from that state to capability, which is the sequence, comparison and subtraction work. This is a notational failure, not a cultural one, and it applies to every culture's list equally. Fig 12 :43 *"one bond type costs nothing; seven cost 2.8 bits each"* applies only once the bond is made explicit; a definition that records only its ingredients has recorded half of itself.

- **Falsifier:** a definition written with an explicit typed bond that predicts a route to capability and is refuted by a ground reading. That would show the bond vocabulary is adequate and the failure is elsewhere.

## CONCLUSION

The three assumptions form a **union compound**, not three independent questions (Q1). Cultures that share the atoms but bond them differently are at least a **functional group**, and over the full shared atom set they are a real **isomer** claim with a stated falsifier (Q2). Their dependency position is the unmeasured vocabulary-to-objective link in Fig 3, which is why cultural assumptions enter definitions unnoticed and why refining definitions cannot fix a missing contact measure (Q3). A bare list of traits records ingredients and not the bond, so it can name a state and not a route (Q4). All four claims are `specified` and ungrounded.

This framing keeps what the question is really asking without inventing a new definition or treating cultural variation as noise. It also explains why the question is hard: it sits on the one link in the chain that has neither an obvious failure nor an obvious test.
