## 0. The residual in effect terms

The question as posed contains a hidden claim about a fact, a hidden claim about a category, and a hidden claim about a policy form:

1. That "natural monopoly" names a stable property of a thing called "a digital platform".
2. That the property, once found, is of the kind that licenses a policy conclusion.
3. That "public utility regulation" is one form whose fit follows from the property.

Each is a separate residual. State them in effect terms before choosing any figure line:

- **R1 (cost shape).** What would have to be observed for one supplier to serve the whole of a named market at lower long-run cost than any division of that market among two or more suppliers? The effect is subadditivity of a cost function under a stated demand. The ordinary word "natural" asserts that this does not move when expectations move.
- **R2 (coordination shape).** What would have to be observed for users to coordinate on one network even when no cost advantage exists? The effect is a demand-side tipping process with multiple equilibria, where the selected equilibrium depends on expectations and on multihoming and switching costs.
- **R3 (habitat shape).** What conditions keep the concentration reading stable, or break it: innovation shocks, envelopment, entry, data portability, interoperability, regulation itself?
- **R4 (policy form).** What does a proposed rule do: keep a non-updating ground reachable and remain a shared convention, or decide the outcome and become a verdict?

The residual is indexed per named market slice (Fig 1 :19, :22). The category "digital platforms" is an aggregation over slices with different multihoming and switching costs. The EU Digital Markets Act itself implicitly concedes this by designating eight distinct core platform services and then particular services within them, rather than regulating "platforms" as one thing.

The four residuals form a **union**, not a partition. Fig 9 :55-56: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."* The Library of Babel essay says the same in search terms: "Perhaps your problem was a bundle or conflation of several problems masquerading as one? You will need to search all of those problems individually to find their answers and then combine them in a satisfactory way."

**Dependency order (Fig 3).** Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* R1 must precede R4 because a policy form is assessed against what it is applied to. But R2 and R3 sit between them and cannot be skipped: a cost finding does not propagate to the policy question without passing through the coordination and habitat findings. Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* Answering R4 with a cost label alone is effort spent above a break.

## 1. Domain census (written before any figure line was chosen)

R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain with actors, currency and anchor. No nameable anchor means it is not yet a domain.

- **R (arity / symmetry / scale).** Arity: four conditions, so a union compound, not a binary question with a conditional arrow. Symmetry: the cost predicate is a threshold relation (one supplier cheaper than a set of suppliers), ordered, not a symmetric join; the coordination predicate is self-fulfilling and multiple-valued; the policy predicate is a form distinction, not a magnitude. Scale: cost and coordination are first-order at the level of one market slice; the regulatory question is second-order, composed over settled slice verdicts, and is asked only after them.
- **Domain 1, cost structure.** Actors: incumbent supplier, entrants, cost function, demand. Currency: long-run average cost and subadditivity. Anchor: a cost measurement under a stated demand, taken from a source that does not update on the platform's own claim. Nameable anchor, so this is a domain. Question type: threshold (Fig 12 :87).
- **Domain 2, demand coordination.** Actors: users on each side of a platform, expectations, multihoming and switching decisions. Currency: utility as a function of the number of compatible users, not supplier cost. Anchor: observed adoption and tipping under stated multihoming conditions. Nameable, so this is a domain. Question type: multiple equilibria, not a single threshold.
- **Domain 3, habitat and channel.** Actors: adjacent platforms, innovators, regulators, data and interoperability conditions. Currency: contact and reach. Anchor: the market outcome that does or does not pay for a configuration, which does not change because a frame thinks it should (Fig 2 :29). Nameable, so a domain.
- **Domain 4, policy form.** Actors: regulator, regulated platform, business users, end users. Currency: rules and the outcomes they decide. Anchor: the ground the rule keeps reachable, never agreement among the parties. Nameable, so a domain. Question type: partition first (convention versus outcome-fixing verdict), then union inside the convention class because multiple instruments can compose.
- **Partition or union.** R1 to R4 are a union with unassignable overlap: the same observed concentration reading can be produced by cost structure, demand coordination, preferential attachment, or regulatory privilege. The regulatory class then splits into a partition of two forms with different bonds. Unbundle before searching (Fig 9 :54).
- **Order by dependency.** R1, R2 and R3 are ranged first, per slice. Only then is R4 composed over their settled results. Refusal precedes mint (Formula step 2): the label "natural monopoly" must be refused as an explanation of the concentration reading before any new policy term is minted.

**Strain test applied during the census.** Applying the six-term physical substrate of Fig 13 directly to markets strains the text: energy and gradient are physical states and structures. Re-census: the market domains are governed by the loop's invariants (anchor, closure, union, bond, refusal, per-slot residual, valence), which Fig 4 :19-20 says are what remain unchanged when actors, currency and anchor change. The physical six are not being asserted of markets. No strain survives the re-census, but the first reading is kept here as evidence of the correction.

## 3. Findings F1 to F10

### F1 - The question is a union compound, not a conditional chain (decisive for the form of the answer)

Fig 9 :4-5: *"First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."* Fig 13 :136: *"It can say the question is not one question and must be split before it is searched again."*

The question's grammar ("are they X, and if so, should we do Y") implies a chain. The figure census says the residuals are four overlapping conditions. The EU Digital Markets Act rationale, opened this session, lists the forces behind gatekeeper position as a conjunction: "(1) important economies of scale on the supply side; (2) strong direct and indirect network effects on the demand-side; (3) data-driven competitive advantage; (4) high rate of innovation; and (5) development of conglomerates that structure entire ecosystems", adding that "the combination of these elements can lead to market dynamics that follow the 'winner-takes-most' scenarios." That is a union in the world source's own words, and it explicitly separates supply-side cost from demand-side effects.

The "if so" is still meaningful as dependency order (Fig 3 :20). It is not meaningful as a sufficient inference: a positive cost finding does not entail a regulatory form while R2 and R3 remain unsettled. **Verdict: `general` structural, triangulated across Fig 9, Fig 13 and Fig 3 plus the DMA world source (low overlap between figure text and EU legislative material).**

**Refuter:** a figure re-census that returns one governing quantity for cost, coordination, habitat and policy, or a world measurement showing that subadditivity alone predicts the regulatory form with the other conditions held at zero.

### F2 - "Natural monopoly" is a name for a cost-shape, and the label's anchor moves with demand

Fig 9 :30-31: *"your words are borrowed too, so describe the effect rather than the cause."* Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*

The economics source opened this session defines the effect: "An industry is a natural monopoly if a single firm can supply the entire market at a lower long-run average cost than if multiple firms were to operate within it", and reports Baumol's (1977) formal version as "an industry in which multi-firm production is more costly than production by a monopoly", tied to subadditivity of the cost function. That is a threshold relation between quantities (Fig 12 :87). It is a shape: one supplier's cost below the sum over a partition.

The ordinary word "natural" adds a stability claim: the cost ordering is supposed to be a fact about the technology, not about what users believe. Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* Fig 2 :30: *"If it moves when you push on it, it is not an anchor."* For platforms, the relevant market boundary and the demand over which average cost is taken are co-determined by adoption, expectations and multihoming. The network-effect source states that "Multiple equilibria and a market monopoly are two key potential outcomes in markets that exhibit network effects. Consumer expectations are key in determining which outcomes will result." Where the quantity being measured moves with what frames expect, the measurement is not the non-updating anchor the label requires.

Two honest constraints. First, this is not a proof that no platform has a stable subadditive cost core. It is a finding that the label as used does not supply the anchor it claims. Second, the figure returns a shape, not a name: naming which cost function applies is a binding from the economics frame, provenanced as such, not a figure derivation. **Verdict: `specified` on the moving-anchor finding, `general` on the shape-not-a-name limit. Single-source on the figure side for the threshold reading (Fig 12 alone for the bond type), triangulated with Fig 2 and Fig 9 for the anchor and description limits.**

**Refuter:** a cost subadditivity measurement for a named platform slice, under a demand fixed independently of expectations, that still holds after expectations, multihoming and switching costs are varied. That would establish a stable cost anchor for that slot and would move F2 from `gap` to `grounded` for that slot.

### F3 - Supply-side cost and demand-side utility are two currencies that do not add

Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."* Fig 12 :126-127: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"* Fig 12 :130: *"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."*

The network-effect source puts the distinction in the world's own vocabulary: "Network effects are commonly mistaken for economies of scale, which describe decreasing average production costs in relation to the total volume of units produced... Network effects are the demand side counterpart of economies of scale, as they function by increasing a customer's willingness to pay due rather than decreasing the supplier's average cost." The two-sided-market source agrees: "Benefits to each group demand economies of scale" while network effects are cross-side and same-side preferences over user counts.

The common inference from "this platform tipped" to "this platform is a natural monopoly" adds a demand-side reading to a supply-side predicate. Fig 12 forbids the addition. The two readings are held apart, and the concentration reading is not allowed to stand in for the cost measurement. **Verdict: `specified`, triangulated across Fig 12 and the two low-overlap economic sources.**

**Refuter:** a demonstration that network effects enter the supplier's cost function in the same units and under the same accounting as subadditivity, such that a single figure predicts both. Until then the union stands.

### F4 - Concentration is a projection that cannot comment on its source

*The Hard Problem is About Source* (Isaiah N, 2026), opened this session: "You can have several underlying causes that create identical readings but the definition of the output would be read as the same" and "If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information". The essay's conclusion: "An instrument that can't distinguish these cases demonstrates its own inadequacy, it cannot claim anything substantial about what it is being measured or what causes it."

The reading "one dominant platform" is produced by at least four different sources in the sources actually read: cost subadditivity (natural monopoly source), expectation-driven tipping (network effect source), cumulative advantage (preferential attachment source: "A preferential attachment process is any of a class of processes in which some quantity, typically some form of wealth or credit, is distributed among a number of individuals or objects according to how much they already have"), and ecological exclusion under constant conditions (competitive exclusion source: "When one species has even the slightest advantage over another, the one with the advantage will dominate in the long term", with the crucial caveat "Gause's law is valid only if the ecological factors are constant"). Naming the source "natural monopoly" from the reading alone is the metamerism error the essay names. An instrument that discriminates sources is owed (Fig 2 :29).

The low-overlap corroborators matter here. The preferential attachment source is network science and mathematics, not economics. The competitive exclusion source is ecology. Neither shares the platform-economics evidence pool, so their agreement with the figure verdict is weak corroboration rather than an echo. Both predict that heavy concentration can arise without a cost anchor, and both were confirmed in the sources as read. **Verdict: `specified`, triangulated (figure limit plus two low-overlap fields).**

**Refuter:** a source-discriminating instrument that assigns a given platform's concentration reading uniquely to cost subadditivity rather than to path dependence, coordination, or privilege. The essay's own standard: the question is "unreached until a better instrument exists."

### F5 - Verdicts are per slice, never averaged into a category verdict

Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."* Fig 11 :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*

The DMA source lists eight core platform services and designates named services, with market shares that differ by slice (search 95 percent, social media 90 percent, e-commerce 30 percent of users and 60 percent of revenues, travel/booking 35 percent). The two-sided-market source states that "the greater the multihoming costs, the greater is the tendency toward market concentration" and that "Not all two-sided markets with strong positive network effects are optimally supplied by a single platform. Markets must have high multi-homing costs and similar consumers' needs." Different slices have different multihoming costs, so the concentration condition is per slot. Aggregating to "digital platforms" destroys exactly the signal the question needs. **Verdict: `specified` per slice, `general` on the indexing rule. Triangulated (Fig 1 and Fig 11 plus DMA and two-sided-market sources).**

**Refuter:** a single measurement of subadditivity and multihoming that holds uniformly across search, social, cloud, marketplaces and operating systems, with no slice-specific term.

### F6 - Regulation is a form distinction, not a magnitude

Fig 10 :45: *"It does author conventions, because nothing else can see across a seam."* Fig 10 :46: *"A convention makes no claim about the world; a verdict and an atom both do. The seat may author what has no truth value and nothing that does."* Fig 10 :47: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"*

"Regulated like a public utility" bundles at least two forms over the same instrument set:

- **Form C, convention.** Interoperability, data portability, access to business users, bans on self-preferencing and on combining data across services. These author shared meaning and keep a channel to a market that does not update on the platform's own report. Agreement about the meaning is the product (Fig 2 :41). The DMA source describes exactly such provisions and notes that "Under EU competition law, reaching a 'dominant position' is by no mean considered illegal, nor is the idea of a 'winner takes most' scenario. However, practices that lock in dominant positions or that impose unfair conditions, terms to third parties might be treated as unlawful." The target is conduct, not the cost label.
- **Form V, outcome-fixing verdict.** Price or rate-of-return control, mandated entry and service obligations that decide which firm wins or what it may charge. Fig 10 :47 refuses this as a convention that has stopped being one. Fig 10 :43-44 requires the checker to be unable to produce what it is checking: "Never a player, never a proxy, never the author of a verdict or an atom."

The regulatory-capture source, opened this session, gives the empirical form of the failure: Stigler (1971), "as a rule, regulation is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit", and the same source notes that "a captured regulator is often worse than no regulation, because it wields the authority of government." Capture is what Fig 10 :47-49 predicts when a convention decides outcomes. **Verdict: `specified` on the form distinction, triangulated across Fig 10, the DMA source and the regulatory-capture source (different evidence pools: EU legislative material and public-choice economics).**

**Refuter:** a demonstration that a given price or entry rule leaves the seat able to refuse, keeps a non-updating market ground reachable, and is not captured over a stated period. The refuter is an instrument and a record, not an argument.

### F7 - A single lever leaves the intersections standing (the waterbed effect)

Fig 9 :55-56: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*

The two-sided-market source reports the same structure under a different name: "Any regulatory intervention can also lead to the waterbed effect. This is due to the fact that interference of pricing on one side of the market will have repercussions on the price paid by the other side of the market." This is not a coincidence of vocabulary. A two-sided platform is a union of two conditions with an unassignable shared fraction; intervening on one side moves the other.

For the present question this means that the familiar public-utility lever, price control, does not clear a two-sided concentration problem. It shifts the price to the unregulated side and leaves the multihoming and expectation conditions binding. This is Fig 9 :60 in effect: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."* A price cap is a filter on one side, not a verdict on the union. **Verdict: `specified`, triangulated (Fig 9 plus platform economics).**

**Refuter:** a case where regulating one side of a two-sided market leaves the other side's price and the multihoming condition unchanged, with both measured before and after.

### F8 - Market power itself is not the ground the policy question is about

Fig 2 :36-37 (canonical usage): an anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it; human judgment is the right anchor for a question about people and only an instrument for a question about code. The DMA source reports the legal form of this distinction: dominance and winner-takes-most are not unlawful; certain practices are. The natural-monopoly source recognises the same separation in its own history: Mill advocated regulation not because a monopoly existed but because of the conduct and public-benefit question.

The literal "should" in the question is constitutively about human welfare and institutional judgment, not about cost curves. The figures can re-ground the form a rule must take if it is to keep the ground reachable and remain a convention. They cannot return a welfare verdict. Fig 13 :138-139: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself, and narrowing gets you close while the ground decides."* Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence"*

This is a declared `gap`, not a refusal to answer and not an average of stakeholder preferences. It is unreachable within this derivation because no welfare instrument was run, and the figure set is a shape generator rather than an ethics engine. **Verdict: `gap` on the literal "should", `specified` on the form constraint. Single-source on the figure side for the shape limit (Fig 13 and Fig 9 agree, but the canonical README is the same evidence pool).**

**Refuter:** a figure line that returns a welfare verdict, or a locally run welfare instrument with a non-updating anchor. Either would close the gap for the scope it covers.

## 4. Pre-registered refuter (written before the conclusion)

Required before any outreach. The shape claim is refuted, or moved, if any of the following holds:

1. **Stable cost anchor.** A subadditivity measurement for a named platform slice, under a demand fixed independently of user expectations, that survives variation in multihoming and switching costs. This establishes a non-updating cost anchor for that slot (breaks F2).
2. **Single currency.** A demonstration that demand-side network effects enter the supplier's cost accounting in the same units as subadditivity, so the two conditions collapse into one quantity (breaks F3 and the union finding in F1).
3. **Source-discriminating instrument.** An instrument that uniquely attributes a platform's concentration reading to cost subadditivity rather than to path dependence, coordination, or regulatory privilege (breaks F4).
4. **Uniform slice result.** A single subadditivity and multihoming result that holds across all named core platform services with no slice-specific term (breaks F5).
5. **Safe outcome-fixing rule.** A price or entry rule that keeps a non-updating ground reachable, leaves the checker able to refuse, and is demonstrably uncaptured over a stated period (breaks F6 and the waterbed finding in F7).
6. **Figure reversal.** A figure re-census that returns a universal allocation rule or a universal "regulate all platforms" verdict (breaks F8 and the indexing rule).
7. **Status violation.** Any claim in this folder labelled `grounded` without a measurement that pays for it (breaks the mode rule).

None of these was run in this derivation. Until one fires, the verdicts stand as `candidate` with the modes stated.

## 5. Recommendation

1. Lead with the split, not with an answer. Four residuals, per slice (F1, F5).
2. Replace the label with the effect. State the subadditivity test and the demand under which it is taken (F2).
3. Keep the two currencies separate. Never infer cost from tipping (F3).
4. Owe an instrument for source discrimination. Do not name a cause from the concentration reading (F4).
5. Split the policy form before applying it. Conventions that keep the ground reachable and stay conventions are a different substance from rules that decide outcomes (F6, F7).
6. Report the literal "should" as `gap` with a stated refuter, not as an averaged opinion (F8).
