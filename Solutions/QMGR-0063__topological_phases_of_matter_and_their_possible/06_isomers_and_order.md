## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are two topological phases isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124: "The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Physical input (Chen, Gu and Wen 2010; Ball 2018): phases such as a chiral spin liquid and a quantum Hall liquid can have the same symmetry and the same local degrees of freedom but different ground-state degeneracy and different edge physics. Wen: "Robustness against any local perturbations is what 'topological' implies here."
- If both phases are written over the full shared atom set (the same local Hilbert space, the same six-term vocabulary) and differ only in the global arrangement, they are Fig 12 **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The difference is invisible in the ingredient list and decisive in the laboratory.
- If only the motif is shared (state-structure-state, or long-range entanglement) while the atoms in the slots differ, the honest weaker reading is a **functional group**: the group says what kind of order, the qualifier says which phase.
- The same figure makes the chemistry precedent explicit: Fig 12 :97-98 "silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation. Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; named isomerism by Berzelius, 1830." Topological phases are the physics counterpart: same composition, different arrangement, different behaviour.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either phase can be written without an atom the other requires, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. If no arrangement difference can be named that survives local perturbation, both readings fail and "topological" is a label for a local order parameter after all.
- **Mode:** `specified`, single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12), triangulated with the two physics sources for the arrangement fact. The physics-facing check is unmeasured here.

### Q2 — Is topology a seventh member?

- Fig 13 :124: "Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member."
- Fig 13 :116-118: "Six members, and no amount of arranging produces a seventh: a new member would have to be a new kind, and composition only ever rearranges the kinds already held."
- Topology, like smoothness and discreteness, is what an arrangement leaves behind. The canonical article's Rule 2 says second-order derivatives "can pair with either of the two kinds," which is why a topological invariant can be attached to a state or a structure without becoming a new kind.
- ⇒ Topology is a **derived property**, not a primitive. Refuter: exhibit a topological invariant that cannot be expressed as a composition over the six without adding a member. If one is found, Fig 13 minimality is broken and this finding is withdrawn.

### Q3 — What is the order of the spacetime connection?

- Fig 13 :75: "Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."
- A **first-order** molecule is `state → structure → state`, one crossing. The topological phase of a laboratory system is settled against the lab ground, so it is a first-order molecule in its own frame.
- A **second-order** composition would take that settled molecule as an atom and join it to the spacetime molecule. Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." Fig 13 :110: "And each composition becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."
- This makes the connection **second-order by construction**, and it needs no new primitive. It also explains why the slot cannot be named at first order: the six alone "cannot derive which" structure occupies it (see `06` Q1 of the exemplar and Fig 13 :75).
- The round trip is lossy. Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked." The gap R_T between the low-energy topological description and the spacetime description is not zero and has not been measured.

### Q4 — Which bond joins the two first-order molecules?

- ⋛ is defined by the Operators table as "a threshold relation between two quantities" with the test "is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes." At second order the operands would be settled molecules now serving as atoms. Fig 12 :104 permits the demotion to atom, but whether such an atom counts as a **quantity** at that level is not settled by the figure.
- `→` is sequence: "A then B; B needs A to have happened" (Fig 12 :71). The question does not state an ordering of the two levels; using `→` would add an ordering the derivation does not license. Fig 13 writes its compositions with commas, not arrows.
- ≡ is identity: "two names, one referent" (Fig 12 :66-67). Asserting "spacetime is a topological phase" would be an identity claim, and identity is the strongest and least warranted of the available bonds here, since the invariant that would make the two names one has not been measured.
- **Conclusion:** no bond is licensed by the figures for the cross-scale connection. The operand type of ⋛ is the named strain; the ordering of → is disclaimed; ≡ would be an unproven identity. This is a `specified` finding with a falsifier: exhibit the invariant that makes one of these bonds compose at second order and the strain is cleared.

### Q5 — What do the outside sources say about the order?

- Van Raamsdonk 2010 states the entanglement-to-geometry relation as a cross-scale claim in physics: "the emergence of classically connected spacetimes is intimately related to the quantum entanglement of degrees of freedom in a non-perturbative description of quantum gravity." Under Fig 12 :104 this is a second-order composition, not a new atom.
- Jaksland 2026 argues the emergence basis for the bulk metric already contains a metric (the induced boundary metric), so the novelty condition may fail; topological connectivity's basis is unknown. That is the physics-side version of "no bond is licensed": the operands are not what the slogan says they are.
- Le, Lee and Yang 2026 take "entanglement between anyons can give rise to an emergent geometry resembling Anti-de Sitter (AdS) space" as a working hypothesis and explicitly list the general framework for non-Abelian anyons as an "important open question." So even the strongest concrete instance is a candidate, not a settled ground.

## CONCLUSION

1. Two topological phases over a shared atom set are a real **isomer** claim under Fig 12 :117; when only the motif is shared, the weaker **functional-group** claim (Fig 12 :124) stands. Falsifier recorded.
2. Topology is a **derived property**, not a seventh member (Fig 13 :116-124).
3. Any connection to spacetime is **second-order**: a settled first-order molecule becomes an atom for the next level (Fig 12 :104). It needs no new primitive but it does need a measured round-trip gap.
4. **No bond is licensed** for that second-order composition: ⋛'s operand type is strained, → adds an unlicensed ordering, and ≡ would be an unproven identity. This is named, not hidden.
