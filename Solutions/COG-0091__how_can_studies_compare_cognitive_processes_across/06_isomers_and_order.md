# Emic and etic as isomers, and the order of a comparison

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two different research traditions reach structurally similar conclusions about comparing cognition across cultures: cross-cultural psychology's emic/etic and pseudoetic distinctions, and anthropology's insider/outsider debate. Two questions are owed: (1) are these two accounts **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, and (2) what is the dependency order of a legitimate comparison, so that a lever applied at one link is not mistaken for a repair at another?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the three conditions C1 (anchor legitimacy), C2 (frame independence), C3 (effect-first description), plus the observing frame and the local ground. Bonds held: the seven, including ⋛ comparison and → sequence. No new primitive is available without import. The two traditions above are frames whose closures partly overlap through the Boasian and Pikean source pool, so their agreement is one pool with two faces, not two independent witnesses (Fig 2 :31-32).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Are emic and etic accounts isomers, functional-group members, or complements?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**

As Mostowlansky and Rota report them, the emic and etic standpoints share the same atoms: an observer, a culture, a set of behaviours and a description. They differ in which relation is privileged. Emic privileges the relation *from the participant's own significance*; etic privileges the relation *from an outside classification system*. Under Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. Two researchers using the same field notes can produce different substances depending on which bond they record, and nothing in the ingredient list tells you which.

But Pike's own framing, as reported in the same entry, says the two standpoints are *"two elements of a stereoscopic image - one that combines two points of view on the same data to represent its object."* That is not an isomer claim; it is a **composition** claim. The two standpoints are operands that must both be present for the object to be represented at full depth. Fig 12 :94 makes the arrangement decisive, so the reading depends on the bond asserted:

- If the bond is a symmetric conjunction (+, *"both hold at once"*, Fig 12 :57), the two standpoints compose into one stereo term. This is the defensible reading.
- If the bond is a comparison (⋛, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, Fig 12 :87), the two standpoints are ranked, which is the pseudoetic error: one standpoint becomes the standard and the other becomes deficient.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single standpoint can represent the object at the same depth as the stereo pair, the composition claim fails and only the isomer claim stands. If the two standpoints cannot be distinguished by their bond at all, both claims fail and emic/etic is a labelling distinction rather than a structural one.

### Q2 : Are the two traditions (psychology and anthropology) isomers of each other?

- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**

Cross-cultural psychology's "pseudoetic" and anthropology's "etic category with no special status" share a functional group: *a researcher's own classification used as if it were the object's own structure*. The group fixes the outcome, which is the same failure of anchor legitimacy. The qualifier differs: in psychology the member is an unvalidated test; in anthropology it is an unexamined scholarly category. They are **members of a functional group**, not isomers, because the atoms differ (items and scores versus categories and field notes).

This matters for the verdict in F9 of `01`: the two fields corroborate each other at the level of the functional group, which is the shape level. They do not corroborate a specific instrument, and counting them as two independent derivations would overstate the evidence because they share the Boasian and Pikean pool. Fig 8 :50: **"But shared atoms are not the signature. Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence."** Here the atoms are partly shared by adoption, so independence is low.

**Falsifier:** if the two traditions can be shown to have arrived at the failure description through contact with entirely separate evidence and no shared conceptual lineage, the corroboration upgrades from one pool to two. If one simply borrowed from the other, it collapses to a single witness.

### Q3 : What is the dependency order of a legitimate comparison?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- Fig 9 :27-28: **"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"**

Order, with the three conditions placed where they actually bind:

1. **C3, effect description.** Perception and vocabulary: can the effect be observed in each habitat, and do we have terms for what the task affords there without importing a foreign category? This is the middle link of Fig 3's chain and the one with no instrument. Everything downstream depends on it.
2. **C1, anchor legitimacy.** Objective: can we name an outcome in each habitat that the instrument's authors cannot move? Without this there is no ground for the comparison, only an internal score (Fig 2 :29-30, Fig 1 :27).
3. **C2, frame independence.** Planning: are the sites drawn from genuinely different evidence pools, and does the next site differ from the last in a way that matches the residual the last one surfaced (Fig 7 :36)?
4. **Stake declaration and habitat enumeration.** These are cross-cutting, not later steps: declare what reading of the ground would serve the authors (Fig 10 :60), and enumerate the habitat including relations two steps out (Fig 11 :44, :52).
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it against the local ground, and carry the method rather than the recording to the next comparison (Fig 4 :22-23).

**Why C3 precedes C1 and C2:** the other two assume its answer. If the effect has not been described in travelling terms, then any anchor named for it is an anchor for the wrong thing, and any independence between sites is independence in mismeasuring. Fig 6 :30: *"outward: does another frame already hold it? The description travels. Fails by being sharpened into terms only we can read. Cast it in effect terms."*

**Falsifier:** a study in which C1 and C2 are demonstrably satisfied while C3 fails, and which nevertheless predicts a local ground prospectively and per site. If that happens, effect-first description is a preference and not a dependency.

### Q4 : Is the comparison a union or a partition?

- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

C1, C2 and C3 overlap. A study can satisfy C2 with strongly independent sites while failing C1 because all sites use the same shipped rubric. A study can satisfy C1 and C2 while failing C3 because the construct was fixed before any effect was described. These are different compounds with different instruments, so the question is a union. A single intervention, for example "add more countries", clears the C2 region and leaves the C1 and C3 intersections standing. This is why F12 of `01` keeps the verdicts per condition.

**Falsifier:** a single intervention that restores all three conditions in all failure types and leaves no categorically different residue. That would collapse the union into a partition.

## CONCLUSION

Emic and etic standpoints are **isomers** when read as alternative bonds over shared atoms, and a **stereo composition** when read the way Pike read them, as two operands both required. Cross-cultural psychology and anthropology are **members of one functional group** rather than isomers of each other, and their agreement is low-independence corroboration because they share a conceptual pool. The legitimate comparison is ordered C3 then C1 then C2, with stake declaration and habitat enumeration cross-cutting, and it is a **union** of three conditions, not one score. This framing rescues the part of the cross-cultural literature that is checkable without asserting that any particular construct is the right one.
