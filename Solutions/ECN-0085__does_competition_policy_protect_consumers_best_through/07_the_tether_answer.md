# The Tether's answer on lower prices, choice, innovation, or all three

**Mode: `general` on the shape, `specified` on the competition-policy application, not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a result. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 6), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

A regulator acts on a market habitat to reduce consumer harm. The residual is consumer welfare per consumer slot, not a national average: "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." (Fig 1 :19). The four offered options are three partial instruments and one compound. "Best" is a comparison (Operators table :51), so it needs a ground that decides, not agreement among specialists (Fig 2 :35). The question as posed assumes a menu or a sum; the figures decide that it is a union.

## 2. The shape

1. **The consumer is the anchor; the levers are instruments.** "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it." (Fig 2 :36). "instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure" (Fig 2 :38). Price, choice and innovation are readings on three axes, not three candidate grounds. A price-only policy is an instrument tuned to one axis.
2. **Readings are per slot, never aggregated.** "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." (Fig 1 :22). A policy can lower the average price while leaving a named cohort worse off on choice or innovation.
3. **The three levers are a union of overlapping conditions, not a menu.** "A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds." (Fig 9 :55). "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either." (Fig 9 :57). So "all three" is the honest structural form, but as a compound with its own loop and ground, not an average of three one-lever policies.
4. **The axes are two currencies and do not add.** "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." (Fig 12 :125). "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it." (Fig 12 :130). Price is cheap to state and settle; innovation is expensive. A composite score hides the hard axis behind the easy one. Publish a dashboard, not a number.
5. **The seat introduces and cannot subtract.** "The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not." (Fig 11 :61). "Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state." (Fig 11 :63). Enforcement is ongoing maintenance of contact, not a one-time choice of lever.
6. **No general ranking is available.** After a genuine re-census, no figure line names which lever is best in general. "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself" (Fig 9 :32). "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence," (Fig 13 :134). Which lever helps which slot, under what interaction, is a `gap` pending a source-discriminating instrument.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that one lever wins everywhere. No figure line licenses a general ranking.
- Not that "all three" means a composite welfare index or a flat list of ingredients. Fig 12 :125 and Fig 9 :55 forbid both readings.
- Not a fixed order such as price first, then choice, then innovation. `→` adds an ordering the derivation does not hold (Fig 12 :30).
- Not that specialist agreement settles the question. "A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict." (Fig 7 :35).
- Not `grounded`. No consumer-welfare measurement was taken.

## 4. What would settle it: the ground

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: an instrument that reads green "cannot comment on the source"; the spectrometer resolved which source was present. Here the corresponding questions are:

- For a named consumer slot and market, do price, choice and innovation readings separate, with known error on each?
- Does combining two levers produce an interaction term that is distinguishable from the sum of the single-lever effects (Fig 9 :57, World W5 and W6)?
- Are the weights of a proposed composite compensable at the ground for every slot, that is, does a price loss actually get offset by an innovation gain in the same slot?
- Standing falsifiers from `01` section 3: a lossless three-axis instrument; one intervention clearing the intersections; invariance of welfare to one axis across all slots; ground-verified compensability; permanent settlement by one introduction.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence." (Fig 8 :39). The missing thing is an instrument shape, recorded in effect terms in F8 of `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`: per slot, per market, three separate readings with known error. The framework "cannot say which primitive is missing, because naming it would mean already holding it" (local Fig 13 :135). The answer stops at the limit of the available instruments and records the lineage so the stop is checkable.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not pick a winner among lower prices, greater choice and more innovation. It says that consumer welfare is the anchor and the three are partial instruments that must be read per consumer slot, never averaged; that they form a union of overlapping conditions, so one lever clears the one-condition region and leaves the intersections standing; and that their axes are priced in two different currencies, so a composite score is the error, not the fix. "All three" is therefore a compound with its own ground, not a sum. A regulator introduces and cannot subtract, so this is permanent work rather than a solved state. The framework does not name which lever helps which slot: that is a declared gap until an instrument can read the three axes separately. What it offers is the shape any answer must have, four refuters written in advance, and the kind of instrument that would close it.
