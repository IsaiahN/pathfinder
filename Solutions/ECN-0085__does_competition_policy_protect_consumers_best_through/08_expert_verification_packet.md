# Expert verification packet: the Tether on competition policy and consumer protection

**What this is:** a claim in a form a competition economist or competition lawyer can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `general` on the shape, `specified` on the application, not `grounded`.** No consumer-welfare measurement is claimed. This is a shape and a route, not a result.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

Competition policy does not choose one lever for consumers in general. Consumer welfare is the anchor, because the question is about people; lower prices, greater choice and more innovation are three instruments that return partial readings, indexed per consumer slot and never aggregated. The three form a union of overlapping conditions, not a menu and not a score to be summed. Where two conditions bind, a case in both is categorically different, so a single lever clears the one-condition region and leaves the intersections standing. The axes are priced in two currencies and do not add, so a composite index is the error rather than the fix. A regulator introduces and cannot subtract, which makes this permanent work rather than a solved state. Which lever helps which slot is a declared gap until an instrument can read the three axes separately.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1 The anchor and the instruments
- **Statement:** the consumer is the anchor; price, choice and innovation are instruments on partial axes, and readings are per slot, not an average.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :36 "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it." Fig 2 :38 "instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure." Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." Fig 1 :22 "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."
- **What to check:** does the authority's own standard treat consumer welfare as the goal and the three axes as evidence bearing on it, rather than as three rival goals? Are outcomes published per affected cohort or only as a national average?
- **Refuter:** a single instrument that returns consumer welfare on all three axes per slot with no loss, verified at the ground; or a policy that raises all slots on all axes with no slot-level trade-off.

### C2 The union compound
- **Statement:** the three levers are a union of overlapping conditions with non-additive interactions; "all three" is a compound, not a sum.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds." Fig 9 :57 "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- **What to check:** in a case where price regulation and a choice remedy both apply, is the combined outcome predictable from the two single-lever outcomes, or is there a residual interaction that needs its own assessment? The ecology sources in `09_watson_answer.md` (W5, W6) report exactly this pattern for multiple stressors; do merger or conduct remedies show it?
- **Refuter:** one intervention that improves all three axes in every consumer slot with no categorically different residue; or a source with a genuinely different evidence pool showing per-slot welfare is fully predicted by one axis with no interaction term.

### C3 The two currencies
- **Statement:** the axes are priced in bits (description) and actions (cost of settling), and cannot be summed into one figure.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :125 "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." Fig 12 :126 "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds." Fig 12 :130 "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."
- **What to check:** in a proposed composite consumer-welfare score, are the weights compensable (a price loss offset by an innovation gain in the same slot)? If so, is that compensability verified at the ground, or assumed by the index construction? UNECE guidance (W7) recommends a dashboard precisely because compensability "may not necessarily be the case."
- **Refuter:** a single measure whose weights are shown at the ground to be compensable for every consumer slot. This is the one type question still open and it is named, not hidden.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not that one lever is best in general. No figure line names a winner.
- Not that "all three" is a composite index or a flat list of ingredients.
- Not a fixed order such as price first, then choice, then innovation.
- Not that agreement among antitrust specialists settles the question. "A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict." (Fig 7 :35).
- Not `grounded`. Nothing here is asserted as settled by a consumer-welfare measurement.
- Not that the regulator's institutional taste for cheap, visible price effects is neutral. "an interest in the outcome is unavoidable, an interest in the reading is disqualifying" (Fig 10 :59); the stake is declared.

## 5. What would settle it

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: an instrument that reads green "cannot comment on the source"; the spectrometer resolved which source was present. Here:

1. For a named slot and market, do the three axes separate with known error?
2. Is the interaction between two levers distinguishable from the sum of single-lever effects?
3. Are a composite's weights compensable at the ground for every slot?

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 2 :36-38, Fig 1 :19 and :22, Fig 9 :55 and :57, and Fig 12 :125-130. Six short passages.
2. Check C1 against the authority's own stated standard: is consumer welfare the goal and are the three axes evidence, or three rival goals?
3. Name one case where two levers bind at once, and say whether the combined effect was predictable from the single-lever effects.
4. Say whether a composite index used in practice assumes compensability, and whether that assumption was ground-tested.
5. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
