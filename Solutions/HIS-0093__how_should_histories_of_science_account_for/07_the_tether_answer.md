# The Tether's answer: how histories of science should account for chance, failure and abandonment

**Mode: `specified` on the prescriptive shape, `general` on the structural rule. Not `grounded`.**
Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

A historian holds the finished record and must explain how science reached it. The record shows the
settled result but drops the generative process: the actions spent, the refusals, the residuals that
registered where nobody aimed, and the programs that stopped short of settlement. The question
bundles three overlapping conditions, not three separable topics. Fig 9 :54:
*"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*
Each condition has its own bin and its own remedy, so one grand explanation cannot clear all three.

## 2. The shape

1. **Refusals stay in the record.** Fig 6 :42: *"What is recorded only grows. What is reachable is
   derived, and it can fall."* Fig 6 :43: *"A refutation adds a term that refuses; it removes
   nothing, and the reach falls because something was added rather than because anything was
   deleted."* A history that prunes failed projects makes it impossible to tell never-tried from
   tried-and-refused. The archive is not a store that deletes.
2. **Price results in two currencies.** Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and
   they do not add."* Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether
   it holds."* The published paper is the bits; the failed runs and the wrong instrument are the
   actions. A description that carries no action cost is a flat formula.
3. **Chance is a residual on an already-returning instrument.** Fig 6 :34-35: *"Where nothing is,
   there is no edge to extend from, and the search for a proto-instrument is a search for one that is
   already there."* What a history owes is the instrument that was already returning a failing
   reading and the observer's expectation, not the label "luck".
4. **Abandonment is not impossibility.** Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof
   of absence."* Fig 8 :40-43: *"Underivable-in-principle and unreachable-at-this-budget are
   different facts, and they issue different instructions."* An ended program is refuted, or
   unreachable at the budget, or has no proto-instrument anywhere. These must be reported separately.
5. **The past ground must not update on the present outcome.** Fig 2 :33: *"The anchor must not
   update."* Fig 2 :34: *"If it does, panel 3 turns into panel 1: the anchor becomes another frame,
   everything drifts together, and the drift is coherent, which is why nobody notices."* Presentism
   is the named coherent-drift failure, not a harmless change of emphasis.
6. **The lineage is the proof.** Fig 13 :140-141: *"not only what was concluded, but what could have
   been concluded, and what would have had to be true for the answer to differ."* Fig 13 :129:
   *"It is recoverable from the lineage."* A history reports the route, the refused alternatives and
   the counterfactual condition. Those are part of the term, not documentation of it.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not a verdict about any named discovery, program or historian. The framework returns a shape, not
  a name. A claim that a specific episode was lucky, or that a program was impossible, is owed to the
  dated record and is not derived here.
- Not "all failures are useful" or "every abandoned program should be revived". Refusals have
  standing to remain in the record, not standing to be composed over. Fig 5 :55: *"What it
  removes is not a term but a term's standing to be composed over."*
- Not a claim that the archive is the ground. The archive is the record; the ground is the dated
  artifact and the then-available instrument reading. A record can become a frame that updates.
- Not a periodization, a list of great episodes, or a new historiographic method. It is the shape any
  checkable history of failed work must have.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement settles a historiographic prescription.

## 4. What would settle it : the ground

The ground here is the dated primary record and the then-available instrument reading, checked by a
reader who does not hold the framework's interior. The five pre-registered refuters in
`01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §4 are the operational test. In plain terms:

1. Show an archive that reconstructs the counterfactual correctly while recording no refusals or
   stopped branches.
2. Show a discovery where no instrument at any resolution was returning a failing reading.
3. Show that "we searched and found nothing" legitimately proves absence.
4. Show that the past ground legitimately updates on the present outcome, so presentism is not drift.
5. Show a finished result from which the route and the counterfactual are recoverable by inspection,
   with no lineage.

If any fires, the shape is withdrawn or narrowed. The world literature provides corroboration only:
Butterfield on Whig history, publication-bias research on the missing file-drawer, Dunbar and
Baumeister on the prepared mind, and cold fusion on the difference between failed replication and
proof of impossibility. None of them overrule the figures, and most share evidence pools.

## 5. The honest stop

**Reached in shape, not settled at the ground.** The prescription stops where instruments cannot
distinguish: the figures say what a checkable history must contain, not which history is correct.
Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."* The lineage of this derivation is
recorded in `01`, `05`, `06`, `10` and `11`, so the stop is checkable. Fig 2 :45: *"Frames range
against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."*

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> A history of failed science research should be a lineage record, not a highlight reel. It keeps
> failed and abandoned work in the archive as refusals, so a reader can still tell never-tried from
> tried-and-refused. It prices each result in two currencies, the description that survived and the
> actions it cost to establish, so that a one-line discovery is not mistaken for a cheap one. It
> locates a chance discovery in an instrument that was already returning a failing reading and in an
> observer prepared to notice it. It distinguishes three different reasons a program ends: refuted,
> unreachable at the budget, or no instrument anywhere that was returning anything. It refuses to let
> the past ground update on the present outcome, which is the failure historians call Whig history.
> And it reports what could have been concluded and what would have had to be true for the answer to
> differ. That is a shape, not a verdict about any particular episode. Five refuters were written
> before the derivation; any one of them would narrow or withdraw the claim. The resulting history is
> checkable by a reader who holds only the residual and the dated record, not the framework.
