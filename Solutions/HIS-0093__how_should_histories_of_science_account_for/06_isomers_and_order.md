# Are two histories of the same episode isomers? Structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading
(see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two histories can report the same published result and the same list of episodes, yet be different
accounts. The question asks what distinguishes them. Under Fig 12, the difference is the bond and
the route, not the ingredients. Fig 13 :128: *"the order is not recoverable from the artefact. It is
recoverable from the lineage."*

Two questions are owed:
1. Can two histories with the same outcome set be **isomers** under Fig 12's definition?
2. What **order** governs the construction of a history so it does not run a lever on a union?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Isomer or functional group?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 13 :121:

> **"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."**

Take two histories of one episode with the same outcome set `{paper, instrument, result}`.
- If they share only the outcome set and arrange different atoms under different bonds (one places
  the instrument before the result as a cause, the other places a refusal between the two), they are
  **not isomers** under Fig 12's definition, because the atom sets differ. They are two members of
  the same **functional group**: a lineage with a refusal in the slot and a ground at the end.
- If both histories are written over the full shared atom set, including the refusals and the
  actions, and differ only in which bond connects which pair, then they **are** isomers: same atoms,
  different arrangement, different substance. This is the stronger and checkable claim.

Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says
what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The functional group here is the lineage shape; the
qualifier is the actual route. A history that records only the group says what kind of thing
happened, not which thing happened.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two accounts with the same outcome set can be shown to
carry the same refusals and the same actions under the same bonds and still behave differently as
histories, the isomer reading fails. If they cannot, the route is the difference, as claimed.

### Q2 : What order does the narrative have?

Formula step 2 sorts the residual before minting: REFUTED owes a refusal, not an explanation. Fig 9
:27-28: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."* Fig 12 :104:
*"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."*

So the construction order for a history is:

1. **Enumerate the habitat** (Fig 11 :43: *"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."*) List
   everything in contact with the episode, then what contacts those, outward until the cascade
   stops mattering. Do not select the actors first.
2. **Date the artifacts** so the anchor cannot update on the outcome. This is Fig 3's first link,
   perception, before vocabulary.
3. **Sort the bin for each stopped or successful path**: refuted, unreachable-at-budget, no
   proto-instrument, or transferred. This is the REFUTED bin before the MINT step.
4. **Price each term in bits and actions** (Fig 12 :125). A result without its action cost is a
   flat formula.
5. **Compose the narrative second order** only after the first-order episodes are settled. The
   narrative is a chain of settled molecules, not a new primitive.

A history written in a different order is not a weaker version of the same thing. It is a reading of
nothing below the break (Fig 3 :22-23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only
its predecessor can."*).

### Q3 : The three items as ordered bonds, not a flat list

The question bundles chance, failure and abandonment. Fig 12 :6 names the collapse: *"collapse 6:
the flat formula"*, where *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not."* Writing the
three as a comma-separated list commits exactly this collapse.

The relationship is better read as an ordered discrimination:
- chance registers where an instrument was already returning something (Fig 6 :34-35);
- a project fails when a term is offered and does not pay or does not settle (Formula step 5);
- a program is abandoned when search stops short of settlement (Fig 8 :39, Fig 6 :34-35).

These are different bins with different remedies. Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union
rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* The order matters because the bins are
nested: you cannot classify a program as abandoned until you have checked whether it was refuted
(SEARCHED is asked first, Formula step 7, because it is cheapest and the other two assume its
answer).

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single lever resolves all three conditions including
their intersections, the union reading fails and the flat list is the right form. The order claim
is refuted if a program can be correctly labelled abandoned before checking whether it was
refuted.

### Q4 : The analogue from *How a Society of Agents Would Work*

The essay states the decay rule: *"Things fade, so they can be tried again"* and adds that *"the
record is never deleted."* This is the negation operator in narrative form. A verdict loses its
standing to be composed over, while the term stays in the archive. Fig 6 :42-43: *"What is recorded
only grows. What is reachable is derived, and it can fall."*

The analogue is not borrowed as a new claim about history. It is a second field using the same
shape: a store that deletes cannot tell never-held from held-and-given-up. The source is recorded
in `10_source_ledger.csv` as secondary corroboration.

## CONCLUSION

Two histories of the same outcome set are isomers only if they share the full atom set including
refusals and actions and differ in bonds. As usually written, they are a functional group: same
shape, different member, and the difference is lost. The order of composition is enumerated habitat,
dated artifacts, sorted bins, priced terms, second-order narrative. The three items in the question
are a union of overlapping bins, not a flat list, and must be unbundled before any single lever is
applied. Two falsifiers were written before the run (Q1, Q3).
