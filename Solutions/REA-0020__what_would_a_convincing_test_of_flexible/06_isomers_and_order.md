## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- The two conditions are C1 (the anchor is reachable and does not move) and C2 (the test requires contact with a differing closure). They overlap when a real-shift benchmark is also used for model selection: the instrument enters the loop, and the test becomes a compound.
- ⇒ The question is a **union**. A single lever (for example "collect more test data") clears the region where only one condition binds and leaves the intersection standing. A test report must give two verdicts, never one blended score.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** a single intervention that restores both conditions across both failure types with no categorically different residue left over. That would collapse the union into a partition. This is the same test as F7 in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`.

### Q2 - What is the order of the three questions after a low OOD score?

- Fig 9 :6-8 names three questions in order: *"the verdict: is φ* in here at all?"*, *"the lookup: which frame holds it?"*, *"the narrowing: which one is it?"*
- Fig 8 :40-43 separates underivable-in-principle from unreachable-at-budget: underivable means go outside, unreachable-at-budget means more search, and importing against a budget limit adds an atom that was not needed.
- Fig 6 :27: "Which is why some unreachability is depth and some is genuine absence, and from inside the frame the two look the same."
- ⇒ Order: **SEARCHED first** (is the missing capability inside the reach and merely unfound? Fig 6 :49, *"more search at the same reach: is it in here and unfound?"*), then **INWARD** (extend an instrument from one already returning something, Fig 6 :29), then **OUTWARD** (import: characterise R in effect terms and find the frame whose closure predicts its shape, Fig 6 :30). The two latter are opposed and must never be read against each other (Formula step 7).
- **Falsifier:** a case in which the same low score licenses both "search longer" and "import a new atom" with no instrument to choose between them. Fig 6 :27 says the two look the same from inside; if an outside instrument also cannot separate them, the order claim is methodological preference, not law.

### Q3 - Is a synthetic corruption suite an isomer of a real-shift suite?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Suppose both suites are built from the same apparent atoms: noise, blur, lighting, viewpoint, wear. In a synthetic suite the bond is imposed perturbation (`⇒` production, *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, Operators table :36-40): the corruption is generated by a script. In a real-shift suite the bond is naturally occurring covariance with other conditions (`+` conjunction, *"both hold at once"*, Operators table :11-16): hospital, equipment, operator and patient population arrive together.
- Under Fig 12 they are **isomers**: same atom set, different bond, different substance. This is why a synthetic solve does not transfer (Taori et al. 2020, corroborating Fig 11 :70). The difference is *"invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour"*: both suites can report a 15 point drop, and only one is in contact with the deployment ground.
- Both belong to the same **functional group** (`habitat -> instrument -> ground`), which is why they are easy to confuse as variants of one thing. The qualifier, imposed versus naturally occurring, says which member.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** a demonstration that a synthetic suite and a real-shift suite built from the same atoms have the same failure modes on the same models at the same time, with no residue that distinguishes them. That would collapse the isomer claim into identity.

### Q4 - Which test is a catalyst and which is spent?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- A benchmark that is regenerated from a fresh habitat on each use is a catalyst: it survives its own use and keeps paying. A fixed test set that leaks into training is a spent part: it gives the same score across uses while no longer measuring what it first measured.
- Fig 1 :27: "They are frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces." A spent benchmark is the frame scoring itself. Recht et al. 2019 are the world-facing instance: *"Both benchmarks have been the focus of intense research for almost a decade, raising the danger of overfitting to excessively re-used test sets."*
- **Falsifier:** a fixed benchmark whose scores keep predicting fresh deployment outcomes across a decade of use. Then the catalyst/spent distinction does not bite for that case.

### Q5 - What order does the test report itself follow?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
- Order for a convincing report: 1 name the residual in effect terms -> 2 enumerate the habitat -> 3 name the anchor that does not move -> 4 state which closure differs and what would be imported -> 5 report per-slot R -> 6 report depth versus absence -> 7 record the lineage and what would reverse the verdict.
- Steps 1 to 3 are the middle link of Fig 3's chain, the one with no instrument (Fig 3 :27, *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*). Most effort in OOD evaluation lands at step 5, where the measurements already exist.

## CONCLUSION

The question is a union, not a partition, so the verdicts stay separate. The right order after a low score is SEARCHED, then INWARD, then OUTWARD, with INWARD and OUTWARD never read against each other. A synthetic suite and a real-shift suite built from the same atoms are isomers under Fig 12, not variants of one thing, and a benchmark is a catalyst only while it survives its own use. These are `specified` structural claims with falsifiers written in advance. They rescue the intuition that "harder tests are better tests" by replacing it with a checkable distinction: the bond between a test and its habitat decides what the test is.
