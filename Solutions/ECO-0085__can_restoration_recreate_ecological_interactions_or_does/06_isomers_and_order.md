# Isomers, order and cycles in restoration: supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`, with `general` structural rules.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6). It cannot settle any real restoration question.

## RESIDUAL

The main audit leaves a structural question open. If two sites share a species list and differ in their relations, are they the same restoration outcome written twice, or different outcomes that the species list cannot tell apart? And what order of work does the figure's bond vocabulary license? "Plant the list, then let interactions follow" is an ordered claim; the figures require the order to be derived, not assumed.

Atoms held in the argument: actors and conditions read off the habitat (Fig 11 :45-46), relations as typed bonds (Fig 11 :47), and the seven bonds plus negation (Fig 12 :49, Operators table). ## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  Are two sites with the same species list isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :95-96: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing."** The difference is **"carried entirely by the bond."**
- Fig 12 :16-17: **"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not"**; nothing downstream can tell the two apart and nothing signals the loss.

If two sites have the same species list but one has pollination and the other has predation as the dominant relation, they share the atoms and differ in the bond. Under Fig 12 they are **isomers**, not two spellings of one outcome. The species list is the flat formula that cannot see the difference. This is the cleanest structural statement of why Question 85's either/or is wrong: the missing thing is not another species, it is the arrangement.

Caveat, kept visible: a real site also differs in conditions (Fig 11 :46), so the strict isomer test requires holding the full atom set fixed. Where conditions differ, the weaker claim is a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123): *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Both sites belong to the class "restored community with a native list" and differ in which relation occupies the slot.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the full interaction matrix is determined by the species list plus conditions, so that two sites with identical atoms necessarily have identical bonds, the isomer claim fails and the species list is sufficient. That is also refuter 1 in `01` section 4.

### Q2  -  What order does the bond vocabulary license?

- Operators table :29-33: `→` is **sequence**, **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"**, and **"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."**
- Operators table :36-40: `⇒` is **production**, **"A produces B; B did not exist before"**.
- Fig 12 :111-112: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one. A plan is a sequence."**
- Fig 3 :19-21: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance.

The licensed order is: enumerate the habitat including relations (perception), acquire terms for what actors afford (vocabulary), state what counts as progress on an interaction slot (objective), then introduce actors and monitor (planning and carry). This is a **sequence**: the later steps need the earlier ones to have happened.

The common programme "plant a historically correct list and let interactions follow" treats planting as production, as though the relation did not exist before the plants and is produced by them. That is a type error. Planting produces actors; the relation is a different bond over those actors and needs its own objective and instrument (Fig 3 :27). Note also that the figure's order is dependency order, not prestige order: more planting cannot rescue a broken objective link.

**Falsifier:** a site where the relation is demonstrated to be produced by the planting act itself, with no separate objective, no separate instrument and no residual on the interaction slot, would collapse `⇒` and `→` for this case and break Q2.

### Q3  -  Is a restoration project a cycle or a chain?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- Fig 10 :24: **"the ground does not decay, the channel does"**
- Fig 11 :63: **"a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."**

A one-off planting is a chain. Its output (plants in the ground) does not re-enter as input to the next decision, so it cannot close by construction. Adding monitoring that returns interaction rates into the next decision turns it into a cycle, and the sign of the loop decides whether it closes: a channel that decays (stale percepts, saturated metrics, pooled readings, Fig 10 :26) gives a loop that runs in place. This is why the figure calls managed ecosystems permanent work. It is not a pessimistic add-on; it follows from the difference between a chain and a cycle.

**Falsifier:** a planting chain that stays at target across multiple seasons with no monitoring, no re-entry and no new readings would be a cycle that closes without feedback, breaking Fig 12 :121 for this case.

### Q4  -  Catalyst and functional group: which actors matter?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 11 :65: **"Introducing an actor for its cascade is ecosystem engineering, after Jones, Lawton and Shachak, 1994."**

An ecosystem engineer or a foundation species is the restoration analogue of a catalyst: it survives its own use and keeps modifying the habitat, so a spent part and a regenerating part behave differently across seasons and nothing in a species list records which one is present. The functional group is the class "actor that fixes the outcome by its presence"; the qualifier says which species occupies it at this site. Combined with F3 of the main audit, this forbids a standing good-species list: valence is relative to the residual, and the group's qualifier has to be read off the site.

**Falsifier:** an actor classified as an engineer whose effect vanishes after first use while the classification is retained, or an actor with invariant valence across two different residuals, would break the catalyst/functional-group reading.

### Q5  -  Two currencies, not one score

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- Fig 12 :130: **"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."**

A species list is cheap in bits and can be very expensive in actions: the list can be written in an afternoon and take a decade of interaction monitoring to settle. Conversely, a long site narrative is costly to describe and may be cheap to refute with one visit. Neither figure predicts the other. A single "restoration success score" that adds hectares, species count and a cover index is the error Fig 12 names, not a solution. This is the structural reason `03_public_contact_points.csv` is a contact list and not a ranking: a contact score is a budget filter, never a witness (Fig 9 :60).

**Falsifier:** a single scalar that predicts both description cost and settlement cost in the same units across several sites, with no residual, would collapse the two currencies and break Q5.

### Q6  -  The blend adds vocabulary, not an atom

- Fig 8 :4-5: **"collapse 4: the blend"**, **"→ vocabulary, never an atom"**
- Fig 8 :13-15: **"same atoms, different names"**, **"their union adds nothing"**, **"two names for one frame"**

Calling habitat reconstruction, species planting and interaction work by one name, "restoration," is the blend collapse. It gives a new word for three different conditions and adds no atom to what can be predicted. The union of the three names adds nothing unless a residual is described first and two frames with differing closures are brought against it (Fig 8 :19-21, :53). This is the structural counterpart of the union rule in `01` section 1.3.

**Falsifier:** a case where the blended term predicts an interaction residual that none of the three separate terms predicts, with no new measurement, would show the blend did add an atom and break Q6.

## CONCLUSION

Two sites with the same species list but different relations are isomers under Fig 12, not variants of one outcome; where conditions also differ the claim weakens to a shared functional group. The licensed work order is a sequence (enumerate, objective, introduce, monitor), not the production "plant then follow." A one-off planting is a chain and cannot close; monitoring makes it a cycle whose sign decides whether it closes. Engineers and foundation species are catalysts and functional-group qualifiers, with residual-relative valence. Bits and actions are two currencies and do not add. Blending the three kinds of work into one word adds vocabulary, not an atom. Each claim carries a falsifier written before the run.
