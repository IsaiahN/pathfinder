# Outreach draft: validate the output first, then decide about the theory

**Use:** one to one, or as a cover note with a submission through a public venue in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send. No personal addresses are used or stored by this package.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the stage-1 output below survives a friendly check against a real court dataset. If the shape claim cannot survive contact with a single matched-pair test, there is nothing to offer at stage 2.

---

**Subject:** Check one output about legal predictability before you decide whether the framework is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know about courts and case outcomes. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. I am asking what you would count as a failure. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`).** Predictability should be measured per case, not per country. Take two cases with identical legally relevant facts. Swap only the identity of the parties and then the identity of the decision-maker. If the outcome changes with either swap, the system is connection-dependent on that case, whatever its national rule-of-law score says. The derived shape says three conditions are jointly required:
>
> 1. an anchor that does not move when a party pushes on it, meaning the enacted rule, recorded precedent and written reasons function as one composite that the bench ranges against;
> 2. a habitat that keeps that anchor reachable, meaning published reasons, open court, a record, independent review and declared conflicts;
> 3. an observer who does not treat agreement among people sharing one social or professional evidence pool as two independent witnesses.
>
> This is a prediction of a **shape**, not a measurement, and not a claim about which legal family is best. It does not say predictability equals justice. A system can be predictably unjust; that is a different question with a different ground.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that common law is predictable and civil law is not; that a country index measures an individual litigant's prospects; that a well written code alone produces predictability; or that a published reason is a truthful reason. The framework returns a kind of thing, not a name and not a verdict about motives.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim**, written down before this message:
> 1. matched cases where outcomes are invariant to swapping connected and unconnected parties while the applicable rule demonstrably moves with influence;
> 2. a jurisdiction with no published reasons where outcomes remain predictable under the matched-pair test;
> 3. an appellate tier that never corrects trial error, yet trial outcomes remain fully predictable;
> 4. an improvement to a single judge that changes matched-case outcomes without changing record, review, reasons or conflict rules;
> 5. an aggregate index that predicts individual matched-case outcomes as well as the per-case test on disagreeing cases.
>
> If any one holds, I will withdraw or narrow the corresponding claim. A one line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** record your verdict on stage 1 first. Then, and only if you are curious, the sources are below. The figures, not my summary of them, are the authority.
> - The thirteen figures and the formula, public repository: https://github.com/IsaiahN/tether/tree/general-agent/figures
> - The architecture essay, which contains the figures and the citation list: https://adventuresinml.substack.com/p/arrival-of-the-fittest-the-tether
> - Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "The Rule of Law", for the independent vocabulary: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rule-of-law/
> - Holmes, "The Path of the Law" (1897), for the prediction thesis: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Harvard_Law_Review/Volume_10/The_Path_of_the_Law
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it. The author's own caveat is that outputs *"could never be verified if the solutions presented were not contrived to fit the formula."* A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> Thanks for reading this far,
> [name]

## Stage discipline

1. **Stage 1:** residual, shape, refuter. Ask what the recipient would count as a failure. Record their verdict before any framework material is shown.
2. **Stage 2, opt in:** only if they are curious, send the four links. Their later agreement is never pooled as corroboration.
3. **Stage 3, opt in:** offer the formula as a method to reuse, with the instruction that each user writes their own refuter and states their own mode.

A Track B reply, if one is ever sent under a separately labelled process, is a conversation, never corroboration. This package does not send anything.
