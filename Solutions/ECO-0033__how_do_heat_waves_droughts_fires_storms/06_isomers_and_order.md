# Order, structure and the disturbance analogue

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the distinction between a list and an arrangement. This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).**

## RESIDUAL

The question says the five disturbances "interact". An interaction can mean at least three different things: a co-occurrence at the same time, a link where one event changes the likelihood or severity of the next, or a compound where one event changes the recovery from the next. These are not the same object. Two questions are owed: (1) what makes two event lists with the same ingredients into different compounds, and (2) is the "same events, different order, different outcome" claim an isomer claim under Fig 12's definition, or only an analogy?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the derivation: the enumerated habitat entries (conditions such as water and temperature, actors such as survivors and partners, relations such as competition and dependency) plus the five named events. Bonds available: the seven typed relations of Fig 12 and the sequence relation specifically. No seventh primitive is available without import. The anchor is a measured recovery trajectory or state variable; the ground is not the event catalogue.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What turns an event list into a compound?

- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**

A list of five disturbances records the ingredients and not the arrangement. "Heat, drought, fire" as a set is one ingredient list; "drought preconditions fire, then heat amplifies the drought" is a bonded arrangement. The bond is where the difference lives. This is a structural claim about any typed composition, and applying it to ecological events is an analogue licensed by Fig 4 :20 (*"Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."*), not a quotation about ecology.

**Falsifier:** show that every ecological event list determines its compound outcome without any information about linkage or order, in every measured system.

### Q2 — Is order constitutive?

- Operators table: sequence is *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, with the test *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*
- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- World check, not a figure line: Buma (2015) distinguishes linked from compound interactions by what the first event alters (the next event's severity versus the recovery from it). Walden et al. (2023) test drought followed by wildfire in a resprouting forest and find evidence for linked and compound effects separately. NCA5 gives the fire-then-heavy-rain case producing mudslides and debris flows on burned landscapes (Singh et al. 2023).

Drought then fire and fire then drought are ordered terms, not spellings of one term. This is `specified` and falsifiable: paired event records at comparable sites should show different trajectories under reversed order. Triangulated for structure (Operators table plus Fig 3); single-source per world field, and the world fields share a pool.

**Falsifier:** reversed order produces the same trajectory and the same state in every measured system.

### Q3 — Are the compounds isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**

Two compounds built from the same event set, for example {drought, fire}, but with different bonds (linked likelihood change versus changed recovery trajectory) satisfy the figure's definition: same atoms, different bond, different substance. That is a real structural claim and it is checkable by asking whether the two arrangements produce distinguishable recovery trajectories.

Two compounds built from *different* event sets, for example {drought, fire} and {storm, flood}, are **not** isomers. They share no required event except the habitat they act on. By Fig 12 :123 (**"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**) they are members of the same functional group, "an ordered disturbance compound acting through legacies", with different occupants in the slots. This distinction matters: the question asks how five different disturbances interact, so most of its pairwise comparisons are functional-group comparisons, not isomer comparisons. Only same-set, different-bond comparisons earn the isomer label.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two arrangements of the same event set cannot be distinguished by any measured recovery trajectory or state variable, the isomer claim fails and only the weaker functional-group claim stands.

### Q4 — What is the union of the five disturbances?

- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."**

The union of five overlapping disturbances is not a sum of five regions. It is a set of intersections, each with its own bond structure. A lever that clears the heat-only region leaves the heat-and-drought-and-fire intersection standing. The operational consequence is that a single "compound disturbance index" is a budget filter, never a witness for the verdict (Fig 9 :60). The unbundling instruction applies before searching, so the correct first move is to name the pairwise and higher-order intersections, not to regress impact on a count of events.

**Falsifier:** show that a count of co-occurring events fully determines every ecological outcome at every site, so the intersections carry no additional residual.

### Q5 — What about repetition and cycles?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

A fire whose own legacy raises the next fire's severity is a positive loop; the dead fuel that suppresses the next fire is a negative loop. A remnant tree that survives its own use and later changes fire severity is the catalyst case: spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses. Burton et al. (2020) describe disturbance loops in which one disturbance leads to another that reinitiates the first, and Seidl et al. (2014) show the sign of a remnant legacy reversing over time. The sign of a loop is a ground question, not a figure output. Kept as `specified` analogue.

**Falsifier:** show a repeating disturbance system in which no output re-enters as input and every legacy is spent identically on first use.

### Q6 — What order is the derivation itself?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 10 :52: **"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."**
- Formula step 2: refuse before mint; step 5: settle before composing over.

The derivation order is: describe the compound in effect terms → enumerate the habitat → order the events and name the bond type → track the per-slot response → leave the direction to the ground → only then offer the framework. This is the same order as the outreach stage design. Reversing it, by presenting the framework first, would convert the check into a negotiation.

## CONCLUSION

The five disturbances are an arrangement problem before they are an impact problem. Same events under a different bond or a different order constitute a different compound; same-set, different-bond pairs meet Fig 12's isomer definition and are checkable by whether their recovery trajectories separate. Different-set pairs are members of a functional group, not isomers. The union has intersections that must be unbundled before searching, and repetition can close a loop whose sign is empirical. None of this supplies a direction or a magnitude, and none of it maps the five disturbances onto the six-term substrate. It rescues the structural part of the question without inventing a seventh primitive.
