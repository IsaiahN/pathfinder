# Is a brand a catalyst, a functional group, or an isomer? Supporting derivation

## RESIDUAL

The audit leaves three structural questions open. (1) Is a brand a catalyst, a part that survives its own use, or a spent part that behaves differently across uses? (2) Are "branded good" and "unbranded good" isomers under Fig 12, that is, same atoms arranged differently, or merely members of a functional group? (3) In what dependency order do the competing explanations (habit, switching cost, lack of alternatives) sit relative to the brand signal?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Is a brand a catalyst? CORRECTION TO MY OWN SECTION 4 OF `05`

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- A brand is a catalyst only while the experienced outcome regenerates it. A purchase that disappoints spends it; a purchase that satisfies regenerates it. Nothing in a survey records which, so a survey cannot tell a regenerating brand from a spent one.
- **I overstated the metamerism point in `05` section 4.** Fig 2 :37 makes human judgment the anchor for a person-level trust question. The correct scope: metamerism governs the product-quality sub-question, not the person-level trust sub-question. Retracted as applied to all consumer readings. Triangulated (Fig 2 :37 + Fig 12 :119).

### Q2: Are branded and unbranded goods isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As usually written, `{product, price}` and `{product, price, brand signal}` do **not** share the same atoms. By Fig 12's definition they are not isomers; they are two members of a **functional group**, the exchange `{state, structure, state}`, with a different qualifier in the signal slot.
- The stronger form: if both are written over the full shared atom set `{consumer, product, outcome, signal, repeated purchase}`, differing only in which bond the signal carries (a head-start bond versus a comparison bond), then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth testing.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the unbranded good can be written without an atom the branded good requires (for example a memory or credibility atom that has no counterpart), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3: What order are the conditions in?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- Dependency order for this question: (1) perceive the product and the outcome; (2) acquire terms for what the choice affords; (3) state what counts as progress, namely a smaller R; (4) plan a route using the brand signal as a head start; (5) settle and carry the result as trust. A brand that is injected at step 4 before step 3 is fixed is a lever applied above the break; it cannot use what has not arrived (Fig 3 :19).
- **Falsifier:** show a brand that reliably lowers R when the consumer cannot state what would count as a better purchase, which would mean planning does not consume the objective.

### Q4: Where do habit, switching cost and lack of alternatives sit?

- Fig 11 :43: **"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."** Fig 11 :44: *"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."*
- Habit, switching cost and lack of alternatives are not brands and not atoms of the brand term. They are other actors in the habitat with their own valence relative to R (Fig 11 :49, :51). Repeat purchase is therefore not a verdict about trust (Fig 7 :35): it may be produced by any of these actors.
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."* Once habit and switching cost are held fixed, the remaining brand case is a specific compound, not a smaller mixture.
- **Falsifier:** a repeat-purchase measure that does not change when all three habitat actors are independently varied.

### Q5: What is the union order of C1 and C2?

- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- C1 (contact changed) and C2 (percept shifted) overlap. A lever that clears C2 alone leaves the intersection standing, because C1 still binds. The residue is *"categorically different"* (Fig 9 :57), which is why a single marketing intervention cannot be scored as a single win.
- **Falsifier:** one intervention that clears both conditions with no categorically different residue.

## CONCLUSION

A brand is a **catalyst only while the outcome regenerates it**. Branded and unbranded goods fail the isomer definition as usually written and are a **functional group**; the stronger isomer claim holds only over the full shared atom set and has a stated falsifier. The dependency order puts the objective before the plan, so a brand signal applied before the consumer has a criterion is a lever above the break. Habit, switching cost and lack of alternatives are habitat actors, not brand atoms, and must be enumerated rather than folded into the trust term.

This framing rescues what is defensible without the part that fails: the catalyst, functional-group and dependency claims need no new primitive and no import that moves the wall. "A premium proves value" remains an unproven reading. "A brand is a regenerating catalyst in a functional group of exchanges" is a checkable structural claim.
