## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are RNA-first and cyanosulfidic protometabolism isomers?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

- As the sources write them, the atom sets differ at the first step. Robertson and Joyce 2012 begin from activated nucleotides and a template; Patel et al. 2015 begin from HCN and H2S and derive nucleotides, amino acids and lipids together. They do not share all atoms at the starting step, so by Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers as written**.
- The stronger form: put both frames over the full shared set, the six figure terms plus the chemical feedstocks, and ask whether they differ only in which structure mediates which pairing. Then they are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. This is the claim worth testing.
- The weaker, safer reading: both share a **functional group**, an arrangement that is copied and has a consequence, with different qualifiers naming which member. That is what the figure licenses directly.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one frame requires an atom the other demonstrably does not (RNA-first with no lipid precursor at the first step is the obvious case, or protometabolism with no information-bearing polymer), the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2 — What is the order?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

The dependency order is perception (chemical contact) to vocabulary (an information-bearing arrangement) to objective (a copy that persists) to planning (selection among variants) to learn and carry (heredity). This is **not** a chronology. It says what each link consumes from the one above it; it does not say which appeared first in time. The union of F1 is preserved.

The operators, from the Operators table:

- Storage is a state bonded to a structure, Fig 13 :75: *"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."* The state/structure/state crossing is the minimum storage shape.
- Copying is production, `⇒`: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, with the test *"is there a B before A fires? No."* It is ordered, not symmetric.
- Interpretation is a functional group, Fig 12 :123, and where it compares a threshold it is `⋛` comparison: *"a threshold relation between two quantities."*
- Compartment is the filter of Fig 4 :21: *"The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a filter with a direction."*

Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."* So `→` is correct inside copying and wrong for the union as a whole. A timeline is a sequence imposed on a union; that is the flat-formula error the figure names.

### Q3 — Cycle and catalyst: why the compartment is not optional

Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*

A ribozyme that helps copy its own sequence is both a catalyst (it survives its use) and a cycle (the output re-enters as input). A linear chain cannot close; only the cycle can. But the sign decides whether it closes: a variant that is a better template but does not catalyse its own copying is a parasite, and Joyce and Szostak 2018 report that in free solution such parasites crash the population. The vesicle supplies the boundary that keeps the beneficial cycle local. This is Fig 11 :40 (line 657): *"A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."*

### Q4 — Does recursion license a higher level?

Fig 12 :103: *"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."* Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"*

Once a first-order copy loop settles against the ground, it can become an atom for a second-order composition: a population of copy loops compared by a threshold `⋛`, which is where selection between compartments enters. This needs no new primitive. It does need the first-order loop to have settled, which is Formula step 5's necessity claim. Strained-for-now: `⋛` is defined as a threshold relation between two quantities, and whether a settled copy loop counts as a quantity at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. Named, not hidden.

## CONCLUSION

The two world frames are not isomers as written; the isomer claim survives only in the stronger form where both are written over the full shared atom set. As written they are members of one functional group. The order of the four conditions is dependency, not chronology, and the operators are `state-structure-state`, `⇒` production, functional group or `⋛` comparison, and a membrane filter. A chain cannot close, so copying must be a cycle; a cycle needs a catalyst and a boundary or parasites crash it. Recursion then permits a second-order comparison without a new primitive, with one operand-type strain named.
