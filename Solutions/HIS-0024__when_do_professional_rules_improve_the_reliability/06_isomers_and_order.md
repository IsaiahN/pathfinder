# Are the two regimes isomers? Bond structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified` on the bond assignment, `general` on the structural grammar.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

`01` F1 returned a union: reliability-improving rules and interest-protecting rules overlap in the same institution. Two questions are owed. (1) Are these two different kinds of thing, or the same atoms arranged differently? (2) What is the dependency order, so that a check of one does not get used to rescue the other?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Isomers or functional groups?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Take a single institution with a review rule. Write column A as:

```
(record) ⇒ (channel to ground) → (claim revised or refused)
```

Write column B as:

```
(record) ≡ (institution's own ruling) ⋛ (standing threshold)
```

Both molecules use the same four atoms: record, ruling, claim, institution. They differ in the bond. Column A binds the record to a ground by production (⇒, *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, Fig 12 :76) and then sequences to the claim (→, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, Fig 12 :72). Column B binds the record by identity (≡, *"two names, one referent"*, Fig 12 :66) to the institution's own ruling, which is precisely Fig 2 :47's failure, *"A ground listed as one of the parties has already become a frame."* Its ⋛ compares standing against a threshold, and Fig 12 :87 (*"a threshold relation between two quantities"*) governs the comparison.

By Fig 12 :117 the two are **isomers over the shared institutional atom set**: same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference invisible in the charter (both institutions call the rule "quality assurance") and decisive in behaviour. If we instead scope to the weaker shared substructure (a rule that produces a recorded decision), they are members of the same **functional group**, and the qualifier (which bond holds) decides which one.

**Why this matters.** A reform that changes only the atoms (hire different reviewers, add more peers, change the journal) leaves the isomer unchanged, because the bond is what differs. Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."* This is the structural reason for `01` F8: adding people raises the vocabulary, not the bond.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a case is found in which the same bond (not merely the same atoms) produces both reliability improvement and interest protection on a held-out ground, the isomer claim fails and the two regimes are one thing with two outcomes. That would also fire refuter 5 in `01` section 4.

### Q2: Which bond carries the failure in column B?

Column B does not merely fail to produce ground contact. It actively removes the outside claim's **standing to be composed over**. Fig 5 :54: *"What it removes is not a term but a term's standing to be composed over. Nothing leaves the library. What falls is the reach."* Operators table :58: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."*

So the characteristic move of column B is ¬ applied to an outside claim without the ground paying for it: a refusal with no anchor. Fig 10 :65 warns that a clean record does not make a wrong ruling right: *"a perfectly provenanced wrong ruling is an unimpeachable record of nothing, and it is still to be corrected."* A profession can keep immaculate minutes while refusing without settlement. That is the deepest form of the interest-protection regime, and it is why provenance alone (Fig 8 :51) is necessary but insufficient.

**Strain named, not hidden:** whether ¬ is the right operator for an institutional refusal is `specified`, not `general`. The figures define ¬ as *"this does not hold"* (Operators table :55); applying it to "this claim may not be composed over" is a binding onto an institutional frame. No figure line licenses the binding directly, so it is recorded as an analogy with a stated falsifier: if the institution's act is better described as a threshold comparison (⋛) than a negation, replace ¬ with ⋛ in column B and the isomer result survives, because the difference from column A is still the bond.

### Q3: What is the dependency order?

Three orderings, kept distinct:

1. **Refuse before mint.** Formula step 2: REFUTED is the fifth bin, and *"no arrangement of the other four reaches it."* If an institution holds a regularity that is false, the owed term is a refusal (¬), not a new standard. `01` F12 notes the question lives at Fig 3's middle link, where there is no instrument; writing a new standard above that link is effort that cannot arrive.
2. **Describe the residual before naming the frame.** Fig 9 :27-28: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."* So a rule cannot be assessed by asking which credential its author holds.
3. **Settle first order before composing second order.** Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."* A rule that has not been settled against a ground at the level of one claim cannot serve as an atom for a field-wide standard. Field-wide quality assurance built on unsettled per-claim decisions is a second-order composition over unsettled first-order terms.

Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."* A better reviewer pool (link 4) cannot rescue a rule that authors its own outcome at link 3.

### Q4: The round-trip gap as a structure check

Symbols table :77: R_T = *"gap(x, (T_E ∘ T_A)(x))"*, measuring *"what the coarser description cannot hold."* Fig 4 :30 requires it be measured rather than assumed.

Sent up (x): the verbatim figure set on anchor, convention, standing, rho, provenance and bond.
Abstraction (T_A): the two-column test in `01` F11.
Concretisation (T_E): a verdict on a named professional rule.

**R_T is nonzero.** Three constraints the abstraction holds do not survive the trip down to a named case:

- the isomer distinction (same charter words, different bond) is invisible in any document that lists only atoms;
- the union partition (an institution can run both columns) is lost if the case is reported as one verdict;
- the per-slot residual (Fig 1 :19) is lost if the case is reported as a field-level average.

One thing survives cleanly: a rule must be checked for contact and for standing before it can be called reliable. That is the residue worth sending. R_T was not measured numerically because no ground reading exists; it is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints. Treating "the two columns are the whole answer" would set R_T to zero and commit the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

## CONCLUSION

The two regimes are **isomers over a shared institutional atom set**: same atoms, different bond, invisible in the charter and decisive in behaviour. Column A binds the record to a ground by production and sequence; column B binds it by identity to the institution's own ruling and removes outside claims' standing to compose. The failure move in column B is a refusal without settlement (¬), recorded as a `specified` binding with a falsifier. The dependency order is refuse before mint, describe before naming, settle first order before composing second order. The round trip to a named case is lossy in three stated ways, so a case verdict cannot be read back as if it were the shape.
