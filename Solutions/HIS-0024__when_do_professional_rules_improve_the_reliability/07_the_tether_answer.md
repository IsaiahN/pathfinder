# The Tether's answer: when professional rules help, and when they protect

**Mode: `general` on the shape, `specified` on the placement of any named rule. Not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 6), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

A professional community installs a rule. Sometimes claims afterwards track the world better; sometimes the rule preserves the position of those it already favours. The question asks for the condition that separates those effects. That residual is a **union compound** (Fig 9 :54), not one hard question: the two effects share an institution but are not the same condition, so they cannot be averaged (Fig 1 :19, Fig 1 :22).

## 2. The answer: a per-rule partition test

**Column A, reliability-improving.** A rule belongs here when all of the following hold:

1. It changes contact with the ground, or keeps the channel to it reachable (Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."*; Fig 10 :24: *"the ground does not decay, the channel does."*). Pre-registration, open data, independent replication and recorded protocols are examples, not verdicts.
2. It buys independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong (Fig 2 :42: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."*). Independence is purchased by different frames drawing on different evidence, not by adding more people from one pool.
3. It carries provenance, so a later reader can tell a derivation from an adopted import (Fig 8 :51: *"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them."*).
4. It stays a convention and never decides an outcome (Fig 10 :45, Fig 10 :47). Shared units and shared vocabulary are the product, not a corruption (Fig 2 :41).
5. It measures the residual per claim, not as a field average (Fig 1 :19).

**Column B, interest-protecting.** A rule belongs here when any of the following holds:

1. It becomes the ground itself, so the institution's ruling is final (Fig 2 :47: *"A ground listed as one of the parties has already become a frame."*). A party cannot range itself against itself, so no correction remains.
2. A convention decides an outcome (Fig 10 :47: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"*): whose claim is published, who is licensed, which result counts as established.
3. It protects standing earned on an earlier result (Fig 10 :61: *"And standing is a stake that survives the outcome it was earned on."*; Fig 10 :59: *"an interest in the outcome is unavoidable, an interest in the reading is disqualifying."*). Credentials, rosters, citation records and prior findings are standing.
4. It raises correlation among the people checking a claim about the world (Fig 2 :40: *"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool."*). "More of us agreed" adds no anchor (Fig 7 :35).
5. It codifies one past success into a universal rule (Fig 4 :25: *"A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion."*).

**The two columns are isomers** (`06`): same institutional atoms, different bond. The charter of a column-A body and a column-B body can use the same words. The difference is carried by which atom the record is bonded to, a ground or the institution's own ruling, and that difference is invisible in the list of ingredients. This is why "add more peers" or "change the credential" does not, by itself, move a rule from B to A.

**Nothing is netted.** An institution can run both columns at once. A licensing regime can raise competence and create monopoly rents in the same act; peer review can certify quality and award prestige in the same act. The honest answer reports both.

## 3. What this explicitly does not say

- Not that professional rules are generally good or generally bad. That is the aggregation the figures forbid.
- Not that credentials are always a disguise or that standards are always capture. A shared convention is efficient for coordination (Fig 2 :41, Seat Map :163); correlation is only disqualifying for a verdict about the world.
- Not that a named profession has been measured here. Placing a case is `specified`, and requires a ground reading this run did not perform.
- Not that adding more reviewers adds an anchor. A room does not upgrade the label (Seat Map :283).
- Not that provenance proves correctness. It shows a ruling was reached legitimately; the ground shows whether it was right (Fig 10 :65).
- Not `grounded`. No measurement settled any of this.

## 4. What would settle it: the ground

A case-level ground reading, not a further argument. Concretely:

1. For a named rule, measure per claim (Fig 1 :19) whether the rate of ground-confirmed refutation of that community's claims rises after the rule, using an independent frame that did not share the evidence pool used to design the rule.
2. Test whether the institution's ruling ever serves as its own final arbiter. If it does, column B is active regardless of the quality of the minutes.
3. Check whether the rule's beneficiaries are the same people whose prior findings it shields. If so, the standing stake is structural and must be declared, not denied (Fig 10 :60).
4. Measure rho between checkers. High rho with high agreement on world claims is the named collapse, not corroboration.
5. **Standing refuters**, written before any outreach (`01` section 4): an interest-protecting rule shown to raise refutation of incumbents; a final-arbiter institution that beats a low-correlation frame on held-out data; correlated checkers whose agreement survives independent replication; a rule that improves prediction without changing contact.

## 5. The honest stop

**The shape is reached; the cases are unreached.** The partition is `general` and follows from the figures. Placement of a particular historical rule is `specified` and, without a case-level ground reading, `unreachable` at this budget. Unreachable is a verdict, not a silence (Fig 8 :39). Importing an outside verdict to fill the gap would add an atom that was not needed (Fig 8 :40-43). The three world sources from history of science, sociology of professions and economics corroborate that the two columns coexist; the metascience sources corroborate that column A is real. None of them decides. Agreement between them is partly one evidence pool talking (Fig 2 :40).

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> Professional rules are not one thing with a good and a bad end. They are two different molecules built from the same institutional atoms, and the difference is carried by the bond. A rule improves reliability when it keeps the channel to the ground open, buys independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong, records where its rulings came from, stays a convention rather than deciding an outcome, and measures errors per claim rather than as a field average. A rule protects established interests when it becomes the ground itself, lets a convention decide an outcome, shields standing earned on an earlier result, or makes its checkers draw on one evidence pool. Both can run in the same institution at once, so the answer is per rule, not per profession. This is a `general` shape from the figures, not a measured verdict on any named profession. What would settle a case is an independent, per-claim ground reading with the refuters written first, not more agreement among people who already share the frame.
