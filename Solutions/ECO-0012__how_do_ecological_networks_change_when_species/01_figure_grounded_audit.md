## 0. The residual, in effect terms

The question asks how an ecological network changes when its interactions vary along three axes: season, life stage, and environmental conditions.

Stated as a prediction failure (Formula step 1, `R = |Γ(b,a) − o'|` per slot): a network measured under condition set C1 is used as the library Γ to predict the interactions observed under C2. The prediction misses. The residual is *per interaction slot*: which species pair, in which condition, changed sign or strength, and what came with it. What is owed is not a new adjective for the network but a rule for how the habitat, the relations, and the network structure transform as the conditions move.

Effect terms before any frame is chosen:

- **Actors:** species at a time and at a stage; the conditions around them; the relations between them.
- **Currency:** contact and influence. An interaction is a relation that carries influence, and a change in conditions changes what is in contact.
- **Anchor:** the measured interaction under the specific condition. Not the network-level metric, not an expert's expectation, not a review article.

What an answer would look like, bound as an ordinary term before the run (Formula "Direction"): *a rule that maps a change in conditions to a change in the valence and arrangement of edges, read per slot, that cascades beyond direct contact, and that can be checked against a new observation.* Binding this in advance makes the shape refusable rather than a preference the search cannot see.

## 1. Domain census

**R by arity, symmetry, scale.** Arity: three named variation axes, but they are not independent. Season partly *is* a change in environmental conditions (light, temperature, water), and life stage is an internal state change of the same organism. This is a **union compound**, not a partition into three clean problems. Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."* Symmetry: an interaction change is not symmetric in general. Predation and parasitism are directed; mutualism is not the same object read backwards. Scale: three scales register at once, the edge (one interaction), the network (arrangement over edges), and the cascade (indirect effects several steps out).

**One line per domain.** A domain needs a nameable anchor or it is not yet a domain.

| domain | actors | currency | anchor | question type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D1 Network structure** | species as operands, interactions as typed bonds | influence travels between entities | the measured edge set under a condition | partition boundary: which compositions are legal across states and structures |
| **D2 Habitat and conditions** | species, conditions, relations (Fig 11 :46-47) | contact | the ground, which settles and does not update (Fig 11 :73) | enumeration: read the list off the world, never compose it |
| **D3 Time and life stage** | the same organism at successive stages | ordered succession of differences | the stage-resolved interaction record | ordered composition: a stage changes the operand, not just its label |
| **D4 Verification and outreach** | ecologists who can refute, data and instruments | residual and agreement | the ground, never agreement | triangulation of frames against a fixed reference |

**Partition or union.** D1/D2/D3 are a union with overlap; D4 is a checking frame around all three. The overlaps are compounds with their own loops: a seasonal change in a pollinator's diet is both a habitat update (D2) and a life-stage effect (D3), so it is not split into two easier questions.

**Dependency order (Fig 3).** The chain is ordered by dependency, not importance (Fig 3 :20). Enumerate the habitat and conditions first (perception), then classify the relations and their valence, then compose the network structure, then read change over time, then check against the ground. Effort spent on a network metric before the habitat is enumerated arrives at a step that cannot use it. Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*

## 3. Findings

### F1: The change must be read per interaction slot, not as one network number (decisive for measurement)

Fig 1 :19: **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."**
Fig 1 :22: **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."**
Fig 1 :27: **"They are frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."**

A network-level index (nestedness, modularity, specialisation H2') is a summary produced by the frame that is measuring the network. Under Fig 1 :27 it cannot be the ground. If a season changes three interactions strongly and forty weakly, a single mean can read as "no change" while the live slots are exactly where the biology is.

This is where the world literature and the figure come apart, honestly recorded. Souza et al. (2018) report that "there were no consistent differences between seasons in species-level indices, indicating that higher network level specialization is an emergent property only seen when considering the entire network." Under Fig 1 that is a statement about a metric, not about the residual. The two objects differ (metric versus residual), so this is a re-census, not a contradiction: the paper's network-level result does not discharge the per-slot residual, and the figure does not forbid the metric. The figure governs where to *look* for the change.

**Refuter:** a system in which a single global network metric carries all the live signal, and no per-interaction slot does. If that is measured, Fig 1 :19 is wrong for this domain and the whole per-slot requirement falls.

### F2: A network is a Structure, and the variation is a habitat update, not a ground update

Fig 13 :23-24: **"that entities relate, and influence travels"**
Fig 11 :46: **"conditions: light, water, temperature, season, terrain"**
Fig 11 :73: **"The habitat is not the ground. The habitat is where actors come from and it updates; the ground is what settles, and it does not."**

Fig 13 lists network as one of the three Structures: entities relate and influence travels. Species are the states over which this Structure composes; the interaction is the relation. This answers the *kind* of thing that changes. What varies with season, life stage, or environment is the habitat, the field of contact. The figure says the habitat updates and the ground does not. So "the network changes" is not a single object drifting; it is the habitat's contact field being re-read.

The enumeration rule follows: Fig 11 :43 **"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."** and :44 **"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."** You do not invent the list of seasonal changes from network theory; you read it off the system. Season, light, water and temperature are already on the figure's own list.

**Refuter:** derive the network as a state from the other five substrate terms, or show that a network is not a Structure but a fourth state. That breaks Fig 13 and the whole D1 census.

### F3: Valence is relative to the residual: the same interaction can change kind without changing partners

Fig 11 :49: **"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."**
Fig 11 :50: **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."**

This is the figure's most direct answer to the question. When conditions move, the residual against which an interaction is read moves with them. The same species pair can be mutualistic in one condition and parasitic in another; the actors are unchanged, the relation is re-valued. So a network can change substantially with *no change in nodes and no obvious new edge*, because the bond type itself changed.

Triangulated, with low overlap, by Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."** and Fig 12 :95: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."** The habitat figure governs ecology; the bond figure governs notation. They reach the same conclusion from different domains: the difference between two networks with the same species can live entirely in the relation.

This is a *shape*, not a name. The figures do not say which pair will flip or in which season. That requires measurement. Fig 9 :32: **"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."**

**Refuter:** a measured system in which every species pair keeps identical sign and per-capita strength across all seasons, stages and conditions, and all network change is explained by node addition or removal. If that is found, Fig 11 :49-50 is refuted for that system and the valence-relativity verdict must be withdrawn there.

### F4: The change propagates beyond direct contact

Fig 11 :52-53: **"Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."**
Fig 11 :31-32: introduction is available; exclusion is not (the seat adds an actor and *"you cannot subtract the habitat, because contact is a fact about the world and does not consult you"*).

A change in one interaction is not a local edit. Contact cascades, and the effect that matters can be two or three steps out. Any account of "how the network changes" that enumerates only direct partners is a first-order enumeration and will miss the residual that moved. This is also why a managed ecosystem is never a finished state: Fig 11 :63 **"Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."**

World corroboration, low overlap: Strona and Lafferty (2016) show that networks evolved for historical conditions become fragile when those conditions change. That is a cascade consequence of re-valencing under a new habitat, arrived at from artificial-life simulation rather than from the figures.

**Refuter:** a measured system in which all interaction change is strictly first order, with no effect beyond directly connected partners after the cascade has had time to run. That would break the contact-cascade claim in Fig 11 :52-53.

### F5: The changed networks are isomers over a shared species set

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
Fig 12 :124: **"A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

If the species set is roughly stable across seasons (the usual case in the plant-pollinator literature), then the spring network and the autumn network are Fig 12 isomers: same atoms, different bond arrangement, different functional substance. A module that fixes the outcome (a pollination submodule, a keystone mutualism) is a functional group, and the season or stage is the qualifier that says which member. Where the bond is a repeated, self-sustaining role, Fig 12 :119 applies: **"A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** A pollinator or a foundation species is such a part, and a seasonal account that records it as an ordinary edge loses the distinction.

Where the change re-enters as input (a stage-shifted consumer returning to the resource it had left), Fig 12 :121 applies: **"The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

**Refuter:** show that successive seasonal networks share no species set at all (complete turnover), so there is no common atom set over which an isomer claim could be made. The weaker functional-group claim would then stand instead, exactly as in the QMGR exemplar.

### F6: Life stage changes the operand, and a settled stage becomes an atom for the next

Fig 13 :12: **"the ordered succession of differences"**
Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."**

Life stage is not just a label on the same node. A juvenile and an adult of the same species can occupy different resources and different relations (de Roos and Persson 2013; Parish and Bazzaz 1985, both read). In figure terms the stage-shifted organism is a different operand, and the network composed over it is a different molecule. Once a stage-specific interaction is settled against the ground (measured, reproducible), it can serve as an atom for the next level of composition, which is how a seasonal sequence becomes a lineage rather than a list.

Fig 11 :66: **"Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."** Moving a residual to a new condition set is a substituted habitat, not a deletion; you inherit actors you did not choose. This is the figure's version of what ecologists observe when a species shifts range or phenology.

**Refuter:** a system in which all life-stage effects are changes of intensity within one state, with no new operand and no composition at the next level. That would collapse D3 into D1 and remove the dependency order in §1.

### F7: "Rewiring" is a binding, not a new primitive

Fig 6 :14: **"the only operator that moves the wall"** (import)
Fig 8 :50-52: convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents; only the record separates them.
Fig 5 :29-31: when nothing is new, the machinery worked and the answer was already known.

The ecological literature's central word for this question is *rewiring*: partner switching that reassembles interactions. Under the figures, rewiring is a binding from the ecology frame onto the held bond and relation vocabulary (Fig 11 :47, Fig 12 :46). It adds no new substrate kind, so it does not move the wall (Fig 6). That is not a criticism of the word; it is a provenance requirement. The papers that measure partner switching are supplying the ground reading that the figure cannot supply. The shape says *that* edges can be reassigned relative to a moving residual; the data say *which*, *when* and *how much*.

**Refuter:** demonstrate a network change that requires a seventh substrate member, or show that partner switching cannot be expressed as a change of bond over existing operands. Either would make rewiring a genuine import that moves the wall.

### F8: The three axes are a union compound, and the checking frame must not be stacked for agreement

Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
Fig 9 :57: **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**
Fig 2 :40: **"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"**
Seat Map :163: *"High correlation is disqualifying for a verdict and efficient for a convention."*

Season and environmental condition overlap; life stage overlaps with both for many taxa. Treating the question as three independent sub-questions would multiply a compound instead of solving it. Unbundle first: separate a pure habitat-condition change from a pure stage change, then examine their intersection as its own case.

For verification, D4 is not a room to be filled with agreeing ecologists. Two people reading the same review are one evidence pool (Fig 2 :31-32: *"Independence, between two frames, is how little they draw on the same evidence."*). Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong (Fig 2 :42: **"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."**).

**Refuter:** show that season, stage and environment are independent partitions giving additive effects with no intersection term. Then the union reading and the unbundling instruction are wrong.

## 4. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per Seat Map :287: *"a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."*

The shape claim is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. In a measured system, each species pair keeps identical sign and per-capita strength across all seasons, stages and conditions, and every network change is explained by node turnover. (Breaks F3, Fig 11 :49-50.)
2. A single global network metric carries all the live signal and no per-interaction slot does. (Breaks F1, Fig 1 :19, :22.)
3. All interaction change is strictly first order, with no measurable effect beyond direct contacts. (Breaks F4, Fig 11 :52-53.)
4. Successive seasonal networks share no species set, so no isomer claim is possible. (Breaks F5; leaves the functional-group reading.)
5. The network is derived as a state from the other five terms, or a seventh substrate member is needed to express partner switching. (Breaks Fig 13 or Fig 6, and the whole census.)

If any fires, the affected finding is withdrawn and this package is corrected in place. Until one is run, nothing here may be labelled `grounded`.

## 5. Recommendation

1. Lead with the shape and its refuter, not with a network metric. Ask an ecologist what they would count as a failure before showing them any of this (Fig 10 :52: **"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."**).
2. Measure per interaction and per condition. Keep the network-level index as a frame-internal summary, never as the anchor (F1).
3. Enumerate the habitat first: conditions, actors, relations, then the second and third-order contacts (Fig 11 :43-44). Season and stage are not one axis; unbundle the union before searching again (F8).
4. Treat "rewiring" as a binding with provenance, not a mechanism the framework discovered (F7). The empirical papers are the ground reading, not corroboration by agreement.
5. Send the method, not the recording. Fig 4 :22 **"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."** The method here is: per-slot residual, habitat enumeration, valence relative to residual, cascade check, pre-registered refuter.

### Three-question check (Formula step 7)

- **SEARCHED:** is the answer already in the held vocabulary? Yes, in shape form: habitat enumeration, residual-relative valence, bond rearrangement, cascade. No new primitive is owed.
- **INWARD:** extend the instrument from one already returning something. The existing per-interaction observation is the proto-instrument; resolve it seasonally and by stage rather than inventing a new kind of sensor.
- **OUTWARD:** import only what predicts the shape. The ecology literature does exactly this with partner switching and interaction turnover, and it is credited as ground reading with provenance, not as an atom that moves the wall.

These are kept distinct. The unresolved part is not underivable in principle; it is *unreached at this budget*, because no system-specific measurement was taken in this run.

## 7. Round-trip note

Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

Sent up: the verbatim figure set, chiefly Fig 1 :19/:22, Fig 11 :43-53/:73, Fig 12 :46/:117/:124, Fig 13 :23-24. Abstracted: the four-part shape in F2 to F5. Concretised: a claim about a particular ecosystem's seasonal network.

The gap R_T is nonzero and qualitative (no ground reading was taken here). What survives the trip: change is per slot, habitat-relative, bond-level and cascading. What does not survive: which edge flips, in which season, with what magnitude, and whether a given system's apparent stability is a real absence of per-slot signal or an averaging artefact. Treating the shape as if it were the empirical answer would set R_T to zero and be the dead-reckoning error the figure names. `11_audit.md` records the round trip in detail.
