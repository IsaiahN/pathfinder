# Seasonal networks as isomers, and the order of the union

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question says an ecological network changes when interactions vary with season, life stage or conditions. Two sub-questions are owed: (1) what *kind* of change is it when the species set stays roughly constant and only the interactions move, and (2) what is the dependency order among season, life stage and environment, given that they overlap.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: Space, Time, Energy (states), Gradient, Network, Spectrum (structures), plus the seven bonds and negation. Bonds relevant here: typed relation, functional group, catalyst, cycle, isomer. No new primitive is available without import.

Actors: species as operands, interactions as bonds, conditions as habitat states. Currency: influence travels. Anchor: the measured edge set under a condition, not a network-level summary.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What kind of change is a seasonal network?

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Plant-pollinator studies commonly find that the species pool changes less between seasons than the interaction list does (Souza et al. 2018; Yahaya et al. 2026, both read). Under Fig 12 that is an **isomer** relation: same operands, different typed bonds, different functional substance. A spring network and a dry-season network built from the same plants and pollinators are not two versions of one thing; they are two substances whose notation can look identical if only the species list is recorded.

Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."** Fig 12 :95: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."**

Supporting biological analogue (world, secondary): Fig 12 :118-119 gives the catalyst, **"A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** A pollinator or a seed disperser that persists across seasons while its partners turn over is such a part. Recording it as an ordinary edge loses the distinction between a spent and a regenerating role, which is exactly what a seasonal account needs to keep.

Fig 12 :121 gives the cycle: **"The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."** A stage-shifted consumer returning to a resource it had left is a loop, and whether it closes depends on the sign of the interaction under the new condition. Fig 11 :49-50 says that sign can flip. So a seasonal cycle can open or close without any node changing.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if successive seasonal networks share no species set at all, there is no common atom set over which an isomer claim can be made, and the isomer finding fails. The weaker functional-group finding below would then stand instead. This is the same move the QMGR exemplar made when its two cores shared only one atom.

### Q2: What is the functional group?

Fig 12 :124: **"A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

A pollination module, a mycorrhizal association, or a keystone mutualism is a substructure whose presence fixes an outcome. The season or life stage is the qualifier that says which member of the group is active. This is useful because it gives a checkable unit that is neither the whole network nor a single edge: ask whether the group is present under condition C2 and whether the same member occupies it.

### Q3: What is the order of season, life stage and environment?

Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
Fig 9 :57: **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

Dependency order from the habitat census (Fig 11 :46-47: conditions and relations are enumerated, not composed):

1. **Enumerate conditions first.** Fig 11 :44: **"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."** Season is a bundle of conditions (light, temperature, water), not a separate axis.
2. **Classify relations second.** Fig 11 :47: **"relations: pollination, predation, competition, dependency"**, and Fig 11 :49-50 sets valence relative to the residual.
3. **Compose the network third.** Fig 13 :74: **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."** A network is a Structure joining states; it cannot be composed before the states and relations are named.
4. **Read change over time fourth.** Fig 13 :12: **"the ordered succession of differences"**. Life stage is an ordered difference in the operand, not a different axis of the environment.
5. **Check against the ground last.** Fig 2 :45: *"Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."*

The order matters because of Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Effort spent on a network metric before conditions are enumerated lands on a step that cannot use it.

### Q4: Is the three-axis question one question or three?

It is a **union compound with overlap**, and the overlaps are their own cases:

- season ∩ environment: most seasonal effects *are* condition effects. This is a single loop, not two.
- life stage ∩ season: many organisms stage-shift on a seasonal schedule. This is a compound whose residual is different from either alone.
- life stage ∩ environment: a condition shift can accelerate or delay a stage, so the operand changes for two reasons at once.

Unbundle the union into its pure cases and its intersections before searching. Fig 9 :55-56: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

**Falsifier for the union reading:** if season, stage and environment produce independent, additive effects with no interaction term in a measured system, the union reading and the unbundling instruction are wrong. Record that as a refutation, not as a rounding difference.

### Q5: Round-trip gap for the word "rewiring"

Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

Sent up: Fig 11's residual-relative valence and Fig 12's typed bonds. Abstracted: *"a change in conditions re-values edges over a shared species set."* Concretised: *"rewiring"* as used in the ecological literature.

The gap is nonzero. What survives: re-valencing, shared operands, per-slot locus. What drops: the requirement that valence be read against a named residual, and the cascade beyond direct contact. The literature word *"rewiring"* is a compact, useful, lossy term. Treating it as identical to the figure-derived shape would set the gap to zero and hide what was given up.

## CONCLUSION

Seasonal networks are best read as **Fig 12 isomers over a shared species set**, with functional groups and catalysts as the checkable substructures. The order of the question is enumeration of conditions, classification of relations, composition of the network, then time, then ground. The three variation axes form a union compound and must be unbundled before search. The word *"rewiring"* is a lossy binding, not a primitive that moves the wall. No new substrate member is needed for any of this.
