# Bonds, order and analogues for a loss cascade

**Mode: `specified` on the bond reading, `general` on the structural rules.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7), not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The question says a loss alters `evolutionary trajectories` `enough to cause further losses`. Three things are owed before an answer can be stated: (1) which operator represents the loss and which the aftermath, (2) whether the order of the steps is part of the answer, (3) whether two communities with the same species list are variants of one thing or different things. A species count cannot decide any of the three.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting)

Atoms held (Fig 13): states space, time, energy; structures spectrum, gradient, network. Bonds held (Operators table): `+` conjunction, `∥` disjunction, `≡` identity, `→` sequence, `⇒` production, `−` subtraction, `⋛` comparison, plus `¬` negation as a unary operator. An ecological community is read here as a network-of-relations instance of the `network` structure, Fig 13 :24: `that entities relate, and influence travels`. This is a binding from the ecology frame onto a held atom, not an import that adds a member of the six.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What kind of thing is the loss?

The Operators table defines `−` as `A with B removed or absent`, with the identifying test `is the second operand's absence the point? Yes.` The primary extinction is exactly this: an operand is absent and its absence is the point. `¬` is not the right operator: `¬ is the refusal of a claim`, whereas the loss removes an operand. Keeping the two distinct matters, because the record of a refusal and the record of a loss behave differently under composition (Fig 6 :41-46).

**Falsifier:** a case in which the loss is adequately represented as the refusal of a claim rather than the absence of an operand, and where treating it as absence produces a wrong prediction. Written before the run.

### Q2 - What kind of thing is the aftermath?

Fig 12 :111: `A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one.` A before-and-after species count names two states. The route requires ordered bonds:

- `→` sequence: `A then B; B needs A to have happened`. The second loss needs the first to have happened. Operators table: `swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes.`
- `⇒` production: `A produces B; B did not exist before`. Where the cascade creates a novel interaction or a novel dominant, this is the right bond, not `→`. Test: `is there a B before A fires? No.` (Operators table).
- `+` is wrong: `both hold at once`. It cannot represent dependence.
- `+`'s symmetry is the trap. Fig 12 :92: `Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term`. Treating a cascade as a set of co-occurring species gives one term for many different routes and then charges for the spellings, which is the double error Fig 12 names.

**Falsifier:** a measured cascade in which the order of removal makes no difference to the second loss, while the interaction set is unchanged. Written before the run.

### Q3 - Does order matter?

Yes, for two separate reasons.

1. **The bond is ordered.** Fig 12 :92-93: `Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing. Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term`. Under `→` and `⇒` different orders are different terms. Removing the pollinator before the host is not the same molecule as removing the host before the pollinator.
2. **The habitat moves.** The architecture essay's declined mapping states: `A habitat here is defined by contact with a residual, and the residual moves so the same actors constitute a different habitat when the question changes.` Same species, different residual, different habitat, different cascade. So order is not only a notation choice; it changes what is in contact with what.

**Falsifier:** two different removal orders, in an enumerated habitat, with identical second losses and identical changed relations, measured. Written before the run.

### Q4 - Are two communities with the same species list isomers?

Fig 12 :117: `isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour.`

- **Strong form:** if two communities share the full set of actors and conditions but differ in which relations carry influence, they are isomers under Fig 12's definition: same atoms, different bond, different substance. This is a real, checkable structural claim.
- **Weak form:** if they share only some actors and differ in the species present, they are not isomers. They share a **functional group**, Fig 12 :123: `functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member.` The group is `a loss removing an influence-carrying relation`; the qualifier is which species and which site.
- **Why this matters:** the world literature's conflict (simulations predict frequent cascades, field observations rarely match them) is partly a bond-not-inventory problem. Two studies can count the same species and be studying different molecules.

**Falsifier:** two communities with identical actor sets and identical measured relations but different cascade fates, with no unmeasured condition left to explain the difference. Written before the run.

### Q5 - Which of Fig 12's four structures actually apply?

| structure | verbatim figure line | application here | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| isomer | `Same atoms, different bond, different substance.` | two communities, same species, different relations, different fate | `specified`, unmeasured |
| catalyst | `A part that survives its own use.` | a species whose effect persists after its population recovers | `specified`; needs an instrument to tell spent from regenerating |
| cycle | `The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction.` | second-order effects feeding back on the first loss | `general` (positive and negative feedback are distinguishable) |
| functional group | `A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome.` | `loss of an influence-carrying relation` as the repeatable kind | `general` |

The catalyst reading is the one most likely to be abused: a species that survives its own use and one that is spent look identical in a before-and-after census. Fig 12 :119: `Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is.` This is a standing warning to the world literature's `keystone` label.

### Q6 - Order of operations for the whole solution (Fig 3 dependency)

1. Enumerate the habitat (perception). Fig 11 :43: `The habitat is enumerated, never composed.`
2. Acquire terms for what the lost species afforded (vocabulary). This is Fig 3's middle link and the usual break.
3. State what counts as a further loss (objective): a second extinction, not a decline in an index.
4. Choose an intervention (planning). Only after 1 to 3.
5. Carry the method forward, never the recording (Fig 4 :22-25).

Effort applied at step 4 before step 2 is the error Fig 3 :19 names: `Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not.` Here step 4 (restoration) is the link that attracts the funding and step 2 (what the species did) is the link with no instrument.

## CONCLUSION

The loss is a subtraction (`−`); the aftermath is a sequence (`→`) and sometimes a production (`⇒`), never a conjunction. Order is part of the term, not a spelling. Two communities with the same species list stand as isomers if their influence-carrying relations differ, and as members of one functional group if only the kind of removal is shared. The four Fig 12 structures all apply, with the catalyst reading carrying the loudest warning. Every claim is `specified` or `general`, none `grounded`, and each has a falsifier written above.
