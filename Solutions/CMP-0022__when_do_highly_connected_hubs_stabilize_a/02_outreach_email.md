# Outreach draft - validate the shape first, then decide about the claim

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until you have named the anchor for your own network. The two-stage offer collapses on contact if "stable" is left undefined.

---

**Subject:** One shape claim about hubs and failure, with a refuter written in advance

> Hi [name],
>
> I am working on a narrow question in network stability research: when do highly connected hubs hold a network together, and when do they make it easier to break? I have one structural claim I would like you to check against your own work. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`general` structural, not `grounded`):** the valence of a node is relative to the residual, not a property the node carries. The same high-degree node is a benefactor to the question "does the giant component survive random loss?" and a malfactor to the question "does it survive targeted removal of the highest-degree nodes?" So "when" is answered by naming the perturbation and the measurement, not by measuring degree alone. Random failure and targeted failure are two well-formed questions, not two halves of one harder question, and their answers must not be averaged.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that hubs are always good, that hubs are always the Achilles' heel, that a power-law degree distribution is universal, or that this derivation settles any particular real network. Two papers I read report opposite conclusions about Internet hubs, and after checking the arrangements they are describing different structures with the same degree sequence. That difference, not a verdict between them, is the point worth checking.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. a measurement at a named anchor that gives the same sign for the hub effect under random and targeted removal, where degree rank and connectivity criticality were measured separately;
> 2. an anchor-independent definition of stability on which two independent instruments always agree;
> 3. a derivation of adjacency from degree ordering alone, or a result showing a seventh primitive is needed to explain the flip.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, I will retract the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures, not my summary of them, are the authority.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`. Relevant passages: Fig 11 :49-51, Fig 9 :4-5, Fig 13 :35-38, Fig 12 :117 and :123.
> - Canonical usage rule (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`.
> - Albert, Jeong and Barabasi, "Error and attack tolerance of complex networks", Nature 406, 378-382 (2000): https://www.nature.com/articles/35019019
> - Doyle et al, "The robust yet fragile nature of the Internet", PNAS 102, 14497-14502 (2005): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1240072/
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figure set. Run it on a network you know and can measure. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
