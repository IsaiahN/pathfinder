# Partition, union, isomers and order in classification

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the order, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The ordinary question treats "a classification" as one kind of thing whose interests can be read off. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) is the cut a partition of nature or a narrowing over a union of overlapping conditions, and (2) when two classification systems use the same category names, are they the same thing with different labels, different things, or members of one kind? A third question follows: in what order must the checks be run?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: categories (bins), criteria (the bond between a feature and a bin), a habitat (people and nature in contact with the residual), an anchor (what settles a category), and a lineage (the record of how the cut was made). Bonds held: the seven, especially ≡ identity, → sequence, ⋛ comparison, and the unary ¬ refusal. No new primitive is available without import. The four domains and their anchors are set out in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §1.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- Fig 9 :55-56: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."
- Bowker and Star 1999 define the ideal system as mutually exclusive and complete, then record that "No real-world working classification system that we have looked at meets these 'simple' requirements and we doubt that any ever could."
- Blumenbach, through Bhopal 2007, said varieties "run into one another by insensible degrees" while still publishing five of them.

⇒ A published classification claims a partition. The habitat it cuts is a union. The cut is a **reversible narrowing** over a union, not a discovery of natural joints. The interest enters at the choice of which overlapping condition becomes the criterion. Fig 9 :41: "so keep every cut ranked and reversible".
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit a classification whose bins are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive at the ground, with no residual at any slot and no second condition binding at the intersections. Then the union reading fails. Triangulated (Fig 9 plus Bowker/Star and Bhopal, two low-overlap fields).

### Q2 — Are two systems with the same categories isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- Fig 12 :97-98: "This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation."

Two systems can publish the same four human varieties while bonding them differently: one to geography and humours (Linnaeus, tenth edition), another to descent or self-identification (modern census categories). Same atoms, different bond, different substance, because the consequences at the slot differ. The category list cannot tell them apart; the lineage can. This is the structural reason why "the categories reflect interests" is a claim about the bond and the record, not about the nouns.
- **Falsifier:** if two systems use the same criterion and produce the same consequences at every slot, they are one frame wearing two names, and the isomer claim fails (Fig 8 :48: "How much two frames draw on the same evidence decides both whether their agreement means anything and whether their union has any surplus."). Single-source on the figure side (Fig 12), with the chemistry precedent as a figure-cited analogue, not an independent witness.

### Q3 — What makes different classification systems comparable?

- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Linnaean varieties, the International Classification of Diseases, and apartheid race classification share a functional group: cut the habitat into mutually exclusive bins that decide an outcome. The qualifier says which member. They are not the same substance, so their interests are not the same interest.
- Fig 8 :51: "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them, and the surplus lives only where the closures differ."

⇒ Comparisons across the group are legitimate at the level of shape, not at the level of verdict. A refuter in information science and a refuter in the history of race science can check the same separator without checking the same case. Their agreement means something only if their evidence pools differ (Fig 2 :31-32).
- **Falsifier:** if the two systems turn out to share the same evidence pool at origin, the agreement is one witness, not two, and the cross-field corroboration is withdrawn.

### Q4 — What is the order of the checks?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
- Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
- Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."
- Fig 11 :43: "The habitat is enumerated, never composed."

Dependency order:

1. Describe the residual in effect terms, per slot (Fig 1 :19).
2. Enumerate the habitat, including second-order contact (Fig 11 :44).
3. Declare the maker's interest in the reading (Fig 10 :59-60).
4. Cut, keeping every cut ranked and reversible (Fig 9 :41).
5. Range the cut against a ground that does not update (Fig 2 :29).
6. Only then check whether a naming has decided an outcome and must be treated as a verdict (Fig 2 :43).

A reading taken below the break is not a weak reading; it is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23). Asking whether a classification is "objective" before step 3 is a reading below the break, because the criterion that would make the word meaningful has not been declared.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a classification whose criteria can be settled before the habitat is enumerated and before the interest is declared, with no loss at any slot. That would invert the dependency order.

## CONCLUSION

A classification claims a partition over a union. Two systems with the same category names are isomers when their criteria differ and their slot consequences differ, and members of one functional group when they share the cut-and-decide structure. The checks are ordered: describe, enumerate, declare, cut, range, then judge outcome-fixing. None of this names a correct classification. It returns the shape a classification must have to be checkable rather than merely consequential.

The isomer claim is a real claim and not a metaphor, because the figure says so directly: "This is not an analogy." It is also conditional: same atoms must be held, and the bond must be different. Where the atoms differ as well, the comparison is composition plus import, not isomerism. That condition is a live constraint, named here rather than hidden.
