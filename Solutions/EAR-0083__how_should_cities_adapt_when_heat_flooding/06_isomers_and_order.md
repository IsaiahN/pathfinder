## RESIDUAL

The four named hazards are stated as a list. A list does not say whether they are independent operands, arrangements of one shared set, or ordered stages of a cascade. Two questions are owed: (1) are they a partition or a union, and are they isomers under Fig 12's definition; (2) does order among them change the term, and what does that imply for the shape of an adaptation route?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Partition or union? UNION

Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."* Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."*

The four hazards share drivers and infrastructure, so their intersections are non-empty. A partition reading would allow the residual to be split into four independent programmes and solved one at a time. That reading is refused. The compound cases are new objects with their own loops. Triangulated: Fig 9 plus IPCC Chapter 6's compound/cascading/systemic distinction plus the Library of Babel bundle passage.

**Falsifier:** a demonstrated partition of the four hazards into disjoint exposure sets with no shared infrastructure path and no shared driver in a given city. That would permit four separate loops and break F1 of `01`.

### Q2 — First-order and second-order

Fig 13 :74: *"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."* A first-order molecule is `state → structure → state`, one crossing. The four hazards can each be written in that form at first order:

| named hazard | first-order reading (provisional binding) |
|---|---|
| heat | Energy with a structure over Space or Time |
| flooding | Space with a structure over Energy or Time |
| water stress | Energy with a structure over Space |
| sea-level rise | Space with a structure over Time and Energy |

These are bindings, not derivations. Fig 13 :76: *"A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."* So heat-as-gradient-over-space (the urban heat island) and water-stress-as-gradient-over-space are different terms even though they share a structure and a state.

The intersection of two hazards is second-order. Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."* Each first-order hazard is settled against a ground (a gauge, a mortality record, a loss ledger), so it can serve as an atom at the next level. The compound is a second-order composition over settled first-order molecules, not a bigger first-order molecule. This dissolves the apparent strain with Fig 13 :75: Fig 13 governs the six primitives; second-order compounds are governed by Fig 12's recursion. Different objects.

### Q3 — Are the four hazards isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

- **As named**, the four do not share one atom set: heat reads Energy plus a structure, sea-level rise reads Space, Time plus Energy, and so on. By the figure's definition they are **not isomers**. They are members of a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123): *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The shared substructure is `state → structure → state`; the qualifier says which hazard.
- **Over the full shared atom set** `{Space, Time, Energy, Gradient, Network, Spectrum}`, each hazard is a different arrangement of the same six, differing in which structure mediates which state pair. In that stronger form they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. That is the claim worth testing, because it predicts that a single lever cannot clear all four (the arrangement, not the ingredient, carries the difference).
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if any hazard can be written without an atom the others require (for example heat with no Space, or flooding with no Energy), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 — Does order matter? YES, IT IS AN ORDERED BOND

Fig 12 :72: **"A then B; B needs A to have happened."** Operators table: *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*

Flood then heat is not heat then flood. Flood first saturates soil and damages power; heat after that removes cooling when it is most needed. Heat first desiccates and raises demand; flood after that finds a more vulnerable population and a more loaded drainage system. Same atoms, different order, different term. Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*

Consequence for the shape of the answer: an adaptation route must be written as a sequence, not a menu. Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A menu of measures cannot express the flood-to-heat ordering, and therefore cannot express the compound residual at all.

**Falsifier:** a demonstration that swapping two hazard orders in a real city produces no difference in realised loss or in the required intervention sequence. That would collapse → to a symmetric bond and break this finding.

### Q5 — Cycle and catalyst: what a route must record

Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."** A one-off master plan is a chain; it cannot close, so it cannot learn. A monitored, revisable pathway is a cycle whose sign depends on whether the compound residual trends down. Formula step 8 says the loop stops only when R stops arriving, and from inside a perfect prediction and a closed channel look the same. Hence a threshold and an explicit revisit are not administrative decoration; they are what distinguishes a cycle from a chain.

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** Some interventions regenerate (wetlands that accrete sediment, community networks that persist, trees that grow); others are spent (pumps, temporary shelters, emergency supplies). A route that does not record which is which will budget as if a spent part were still available. IPCC Chapter 6 notes that adaptation finance tends toward large grey engineering projects while neglecting maintenance, which is the same distinction in the field's vocabulary.

### Q6 — Comparison and threshold

Operators table: ⋛ is *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, with the test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present?"* Adaptation triggers are threshold relations: a defence holds until a level is crossed. Operators table :51 and Fig 12 :52: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one."* So a goal for the compound cannot be "be resilient"; it must be a comparison against a stated level on a measured compound residual.

**Falsifier:** a compound objective with no threshold at which an intervention is revisited, or one that cannot fail.

## CONCLUSION

The four conditions are a **union**, not a partition. At first order each is `state → structure → state`; at second order their intersections are compounds over settled first-order molecules (Fig 12 :104). As named they are a **functional group**; over the full shared six-term set they are a real **isomer** claim with a stated falsifier. Order is an **ordered bond**, so flood-then-heat and heat-then-flood are different terms and the answer must be a sequence. A route must also be a cycle (monitored and revisable) and must say which parts regenerate and which are spent. These are structural results about the shape of an answer, not a prescription of any measure.
