# Isomers, catalysts and order: a supporting derivation for BUS-0055

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

Which arrangement of the same corporate atoms distinguishes an improvement from a short-term extraction, and in what dependency order do the conditions fire?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the firm frame: capital, assets, talent, customer relationships, reporting metrics, board attention. Bonds held: the seven, especially → (sequence), ⇒ (production), − (subtraction), ⋛ (comparison). No new primitive is available without import (Fig 13 :115).

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are "extract" and "reinvest" isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

A payout and a retained investment can draw on the same pool of free cash. Written as ingredient lists they look identical. Written with bonds they differ:

- extract: `cash − capacity` (subtraction), then `cash → reported return` (sequence);
- reinvest: `cash ⇒ capacity` (production), then `capacity → payment` (sequence).

Same atoms, different bond, different substance. So the distinction the question asks about is an **isomer** distinction, not a difference of ingredients. This is why a campaign can be described truthfully by both sides using the same word "value": the ingredient list hides the bond.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either campaign can be written without an atom the other requires, or if the bond type cannot be recovered from the campaign's disclosed actions, the isomer claim fails and the two are merely different quantities of the same thing.

### Q2. Is the activist a catalyst or a spent part?

Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

An activist that installs a governance process that survives its exit is a catalyst: it changes the arrangement and persists. A campaign whose entire effect is the price pop and the exit is a spent part: it is consumed in its own use and leaves the arrangement unchanged. The finance literature supplies a checkable hinge: Brav et al. (2008) report that "target prices decline upon the exit of a hedge fund only after it has been unsuccessful," and Bebchuk et al. (2015) explicitly find "no evidence for pump-and-dump patterns in which the exit of an activist is followed by abnormal long-term negative returns."

**Reading, mode `specified`:** catalyst versus spent part is a structural distinction with a ground-facing test (post-exit persistence), and it is triangulated across Fig 12 and the two finance sources, which are a low-overlap pair relative to the figure. Refuter: a campaign whose post-exit persistence is indistinguishable from a price pop with no arrangement change.

### Q3. Two currencies that do not add

Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."

Fig 12 :126: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units"

A campaign is cheap to describe ("unlock value", "return capital") and expensive to settle (did capacity change?). A pooled abnormal return is cheap to compute and cannot price the description. Summing them into one score is the named error, not the fix (Fig 12 :128-130). This is a second reason the two-condition shape cannot be collapsed into a single verdict number.

**Falsifier:** a single measure that prices both the description and the settlement actions in the same units without loss.

### Q4. Dependency order

Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

A campaign that operates at the planning link (buyback, divestiture, board seat) cannot rescue a firm whose perception link is broken (the board cannot tell what the market pays for). Fig 3 :29: "Before asking whether a system is good, ask which link it currently stops at, and whether the answer was measured or assumed."

So the order is:

1. Is there a fact here? Split the union into its four cells (contact yes/no crossed with reach yes/no), Fig 9 :54-57.
2. Which link does the firm currently stop at? Fig 3 :29.
3. Is the metric a measurement or a target? Fig 10 :47, "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one."
4. Only then price the campaign's bond structure (Q1 to Q3).

**Falsifier:** an intervention that restores the broken link without first being sorted by the union cell it addresses, with no categorically different residue.

### Q5. The analogue, and its limit

Low-overlap analogue from ecology (MathisonSlee et al. 2022, opened 2026-11-22): a "rigidity trap" where a system becomes "highly connected, rigid, and inflexible" and "System capacity for novelty is diminished." Proposition 11 of the same paper: "Efforts to deliberately enhance adaptability can (unintentionally) lead to loss of resilience."

This corroborates the shape that a first-order improvement (tighter coupling, higher measured efficiency) can coincide with a loss of capacity to respond. But it is an analogue, not a firm measurement. Overlap with the finance sources is low, so it is a second witness to the mechanism shape only. It does not say which campaign wins.

## CONCLUSION

The improvement-versus-extraction distinction is an isomer distinction over the same corporate atoms, distinguished by the bond (subtraction and sequence versus production and sequence), not by the ingredient list. The activist can be a catalyst or a spent part; post-exit persistence is the checkable hinge. The two currencies, description and settlement actions, do not add, which forbids a single averaged verdict. The dependency order requires splitting the union and locating the broken link before pricing the campaign. The ecology analogue corroborates the mechanism at low overlap but names no intervention.
