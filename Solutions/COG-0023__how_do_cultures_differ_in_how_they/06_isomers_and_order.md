# Structure, order and analogues: are cultural divisions isomers?

**Mode: `specified` on the structure claims, `general` on the order rule.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

## RESIDUAL

The question "how do cultures differ" can mean two different structural things, and the distinction decides what an answer would even be:

1. Two cultures hold the same atoms and arrange them differently, so their divisions are **isomers** (same atoms, different bond, different substance).
2. Two cultures hold the same *kind of arrangement* but bind different atoms into the slots, so their divisions are members of one **functional group** (same schema, different variables).

The residual is the unchosen structure: no figure line in the primary audit selects between these two readings for the four domains. This file derives that selection.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Is a culture's division an isomer or a functional-group variant?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :123:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Two readings:

- **Isomer reading.** Fix the full atom set `{Space, Time, Energy, Spectrum, Gradient, Network}` for two cultures. If culture A's division of time binds `Space → Spectrum → Time` and culture B's binds `Time → Spectrum → Space` (or uses Gradient where A uses Spectrum), then they have the same atoms under different bonds. By Fig 12 :92 ("Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing") and :117, they are different substances with identical ingredient lists. The behavioral difference would then be "decisive in the behaviour," which is exactly what the priming and pointing tasks measure.
- **Functional-group reading.** If both cultures use the same schema `state → structure → state` and differ only in which atoms fill the state slots (time-via-space in one, time-via-vertical in another, social-via-hierarchy in a third), then the invariant is the substructure and the variation is the qualifier. Fig 12 :123: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

**Which one does the question need?** The functional-group reading is the safer one and is returned as the primary verdict. It follows directly from Fig 13 :75, "Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state," which fixes the *kind* of arrangement while leaving the *member* slots open. The isomer reading is a stronger claim and requires the full atom set to be shared across the two cultures being compared. It is a real, checkable claim (world data on kin-term feature dimensions and color foci suggest the atom sets do overlap heavily) but it is not returned as settled.

### Q2 — Are the four domains first-order derivatives of the six-term set?

A first-order molecule is `state → structure → state`, one crossing (Fig 13 :74: "Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one"). Read the domains:

| domain | candidate first-order form | status |
|---|---|---|
| time | `Space → Spectrum → Time` (time ordered by a spatial axis) | first order, legal if the mapping is a bond over held atoms; the *which axis* question is a binding, not a derivation |
| space | `Space → Network → Time`? No: frame-of-reference choice is a choice over how space is coded, better read as `Space → Spectrum → Space` or as the held space state with a network binding for landmark relations | first order at most; the frame choice is an arrangement over one state, not a new molecule type |
| nature | `Energy → Spectrum → Time` (ordering of substance over developmental or seasonal succession), with `Gradient` supplying directedness | compound, at least first order, possibly two molecules |
| social relationships | `Space → Network → Time` or `Energy → Network → Time`: relations as the structure that mediates between a state and an ordered succession | first order, legal under Fig 13 :75 |

Fig 13 itself licenses distinct first-order molecules over different states: "A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is" (Fig 13, cited in the local extract near :76 and in the exemplar's `06`). The principle is that the same structure bonded to a different state is a different term, so two cultures can share the structure and differ in the term without adding any atom.

**Conclusion Q2:** each domain can be written as at most a first-order derivative of the six-term set. This is a `specified` structural result. The particular atom bindings remain bindings, not derivations.

### Q3 — What order is the union?

Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again." The four slots overlap, but they are not a symmetric blend. Fig 12 :46 distinguishes ordered bonds from symmetric ones, and the cross-domain mappings are ordered: a spatial term is used *to talk about* time, not conversely in the reported cases. So the union has ordered edges.

Fig 12 :104:

> **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"**

Each first-order division, once settled against the behavioral ground, becomes an atom at the next level. A comparison between two cultures' divisions of a domain is therefore a **second-order composition**: `(settled division A) ⋛ (settled division B)` under the comparison bond, whose Operators-table definition is "a threshold relation between two quantities." That is a structural relation between two arrangements, not a new domain and not a unified "culture type."

This is also where the mode rule bites: nothing is settled here, so nothing in this folder may be composed over as if it were an atom at a higher level. The second-order comparison is a shape of a future result, not a result.

### Q4 — What is the status of the cross-domain mapping itself? (live strain)

The world literature describes spatial terms being imported into the time domain. Fig 6 :23 says "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs." If the mapping is a genuine import, Fig 8 requires a source gate (provenance) and a shadow test (explains a gap). If it is merely a bond over two atoms already held in Γ, it adds no atom and moves no wall (Fig 6 :21).

The figure text does not decide which. Fig 13 :75 permits a structure to mediate between two states at first order, which supports the bond reading. Lakoff and Johnson's "The essence of metaphor is understanding and experiencing one kind of thing in terms of another" supports the mapping as a cognitive relation but does not tell us whether the framework should count it as a typed bond or an import. This is recorded as **live strain S4** in `01` section 7. It is not smoothed over, and resolving it requires an instrument (a task that discriminates a mapping from a source domain), not another quotation.

## CONCLUSION

The safer structural verdict is the functional-group reading: cultures share the arrangement schema `state → structure → state` and differ in which held atoms occupy the slots and which typed bond connects them. The stronger isomer reading (full shared atom set, different bonds, different substances) is a genuine checkable hypothesis, not a settled result. Each domain is at most a first-order derivative of the six-term set; the comparison of two settled divisions would be second-order, but nothing here is settled. The cross-domain mapping's status as bond versus import remains a live type question (S4).

This framing preserves what the question can ask without inventing a seventh kind, without treating an aggregate style as a verdict, and without claiming a name the framework cannot supply.

## FALSIFIER (written before the conclusion)

The structure claim fails if:

1. Two cultures' divisions of a domain are shown to require different atom sets that are not compounds of the six (kills both the functional-group and the isomer reading).
2. A division is shown to require a direct bond of two states or two structures with no intervening opposite-kind term (breaks Fig 13 :75).
3. The full atom sets are shown *not* to overlap across two cultures whose divisions were claimed isomeric (kills the isomer reading only; the functional-group reading stands).
4. The cross-domain mapping is shown to add an atom with a source gate and shadow test, confirming the import reading and removing S4 in favor of a wall-moving import that must itself be provenanced.
