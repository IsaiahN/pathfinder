# Isomers, order and analogues in the pre-RNA handoff

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the bond definitions, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a chemistry measurement (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The handoff question has three structural sub-questions the main audit leaves open: (1) are two candidate polymers related as isomers or only as members of a functional group, (2) is the handoff a sequence or a production, and (3) do the laboratory analogues license any statement about the historical route? Each needs its own check and its own falsifier.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Are two candidate polymers isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Consider RNA and a sugar-modified analogue over the same nucleobase set. Under the strict figure definition:

- If both are written over exactly the same atom set (same bases, same linkage atoms, same phosphate count) and differ only in the bond arrangement (linkage position, anomer, ring size), they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The behaviour difference is decisive and the ingredient formula cannot show it.
- If they differ in atom inventory (one atom shorter backbone repeat, as in TNA relative to RNA; an amide backbone replacing ribose phosphate as in PNA; a sulfur substituted for oxygen in 4-prime-thioDNA), they are **not** isomers under the strict definition. They are different molecular species occupying the same functional role.

Fig 12 :123: **"A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** The pairing geometry plus the repeating backbone is the functional group that says "genetic polymer"; the specific sugar or backbone says which member. This is the weaker and safer claim.

Fig 13 :121 (compressed): different routes can arrive at the same substance, and Fig 12 :104 says a settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So two different chemistries that both settle into a readable genetic role are convergent arrivals at a role, not one substance wearing two names. Convergence is corroboration at maximum independence only if the records show they were reached separately (Fig 8 :50-52: **"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them, and the surplus lives only where the closures differ."**).

**Falsifier, written before the run.** If two candidate polymers with different atom inventories are shown to have the same behaviour in the handoff assay and the difference in inventory makes no difference to any measured property, the strict isomer distinction does no work here and the functional-group reading must carry the whole claim. If two polymers with the same inventory and different bonds behave identically, the isomer claim fails outright.

### Q2 : Is the handoff a sequence or a production?

Fig 12 :72: **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"**. Operators table :37: **"A produces B; B did not exist before"**. Fig 12 :77: **"A produces B; B did not exist before"**.

These are different bonds. A sequence requires that A happened first and B depends on it. A production requires that B was absent until A fired. The pre-RNA to RNA handoff is **production**: an RNA sequence that was not there comes into being, specified by the P sequence. Calling it a sequence alone understates it, and calling it a symmetric "exchange" erases the arrow.

This matters for the world sources. Yu et al. 2012 speak of TNA's *"ability to exchange genetic information with itself and RNA"*. The experiments behind that phrase are directional transfers with particular polymerases, not a symmetric mixing. The figure's distinction forces the reading: two directional production relations, with provenance deciding which happened first in any given experiment.

**Strain, named and not smoothed.** Operators table :51 defines comparison as *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*, and the Operators table defines ⋛ as a threshold relation between two quantities. If someone tries to write the handoff as a ⋛ comparison between two polymers, the operand type is wrong at first order: the operands are a template and a product in a production relation, not two quantities at a threshold. This strain is live, not a correction.

**Falsifier.** If a handoff is demonstrated in which the RNA sequence is present before the putative P instruction fires and is merely selected from a pre-existing pool, then the production bond is wrong and the relation is a comparison or a selection, not a production.

### Q3 : What do the laboratory analogues license?

Fig 11 :70: **"A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability."**

The P to RNA transcription of Schmidt et al. 1997 proves that a sequence in an amide-backbone polymer can specify RNA monomers opposite a complement, with reduced efficiency relative to DNA. That is wiring. It does not show a Hadean habitat, a closing replication loop, or a settled descendant. Fig 11 :66-67 (compressed): isolation substitutes a habitat, and what you failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails.

The in vitro evolution of a TNA aptamer (Yu et al. 2012) shows folding and function, which is a capability of TNA **in that substituted habitat** with engineered polymerases. It licenses the statement "a sugar-modified polymer can occupy the functional group" and not the statement "TNA came first".

The 2020 review's in vivo transliteration experiments (XNA segments read by E. coli machinery) are a further substituted habitat. They prove that a receiver can sometimes read a non-natural template, which is the C5 condition in a living cell, and they inherit all the caveats of an engineered host.

**Falsifier.** If a laboratory result is used to claim historical priority without an independent chronology instrument, that use is refuted by the substitution law. If a result is restricted to the shape claim, it stands.

### Q4 : What order is the union of conditions?

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**

Dependency order for the handoff:

1. **Vocabulary link.** Is there a distinguishable arrangement at all (C1, C2)? Without it there is nothing to transfer.
2. **Objective link.** Is there a term for what would count as transfer (C5, an RNA-side observable)? Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** This is where the pre-RNA literature most often stops: candidate polymers are named, but the readout that would count as a handoff is not always specified independently of the source sequence.
3. **Route link.** Is there a complementarity and a closing copying loop (C3, C4)?
4. **Settlement link.** Does the product persist and become composable over (C7), and in which habitat (C6)?

Effort spent on step 3 cannot rescue a missing step 2. A room of candidate polymers with no readout instrument is the middle break of Fig 3, and a reading taken below the break is a reading of nothing.

**Falsifier.** If an instrument-independent definition of transfer is supplied and shown to be measurable in existing experiments, the claim that the chain breaks at the objective link is refuted and the break moves downstream.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed atom inventory, two candidate polymers differing only in bond arrangement are isomers in Fig 12's strict sense; over different inventories they are different members of the same functional group. The handoff is production, ordered and asymmetric, not a symmetric exchange and not a first-order comparison. Laboratory demonstrations are substituted habitats that prove the wiring of C3, C4 and C5, not historical priority. The dependency chain's likely break is the objective link, the absence of an RNA-side readout independent of the source sequence, and that is where an instrument is owed.

The isomer claim and the production claim need no new primitive. The historical claim does, and it is reported as a gap.
