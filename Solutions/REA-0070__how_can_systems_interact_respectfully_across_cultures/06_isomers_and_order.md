# Isomers, flat formulas and order in cross-cultural interaction

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed after the main audit:

1. If two groups share the same basic human equippedness (bodies, language capacity, needs, histories of social learning), what makes them different in the way an interaction must respect? Is the difference a difference of atoms or of arrangement?
2. What is the dependency order of a respectful interaction, and which link is most likely to be assumed rather than measured?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is a culture an ingredient list? NO

Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**

Fig 12 :40: **"same two atoms, three substances"**

A demographic or trait profile records the ingredients. It does not record which relation holds between them, and the relation is half the object. A stereotype is therefore a *flat formula*: it has the atoms and lacks the bond, and nothing downstream can tell that anything was lost. This is the same failure Fig 8 names for two frames that overlap almost completely: they are *"one frame wearing two names"* (Fig 8 :49).

**Falsifier, written before the run:** a complete trait list that reproduces the lived meaning and the per-person predictions of an interaction without recording any relation between the traits. If one is produced, the flat-formula claim fails.

### Q2 - Are two cultures isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

- If two groups are written over the same shared atom set (persons, needs, signs, practices, histories) and differ only in which relation mediates which, they are **isomers** under Fig 12's definition. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in a trait formula and decisive in an interaction.
- If they are written with different atoms in the slots, they are not isomers. They are members of the same **functional group** (a social formation with a language, a history and practices), with different fillers.
- Which reading applies is an empirical question, not a figure question. The figure licenses the distinction; the ground decides the case. Mode `specified`.

This is the exact point the world sources corroborate at low overlap: the animal-culture literature calls culture *"a process, involving the social transmittance of behavior among peers and between generations"*, and Boas's *Science* article insists on the individual *"in its history and in its medium"*. Both refuse the ingredient list. They corroborate the shape of the derivation, not the specific binding.

### Q3 - What does flattening write?

The Operators table defines identity: *"two names, one referent"*, with the test *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."* (Operators table :25-27).

Flattening is writing `Γ_A ≡ Γ_B` when the two frames' closures differ. The test catches it: substituting one for the other loses the other person's account, the local meaning, and the per-slot residual. So flattening is a **bond error**, not a kindness and not a measurement. Mode `specified`. **Falsifier:** a case in which substituting one frame for the other loses nothing in the per-person predictions and the other's own account agrees.

### Q4 - What does stereotyping write?

Two errors, both identifiable in the notation:

1. **Aggregation:** writing a single group-level value where the figure requires a slot index. Fig 1 :19: **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."** Fig 1 :22: **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."**
2. **A recording as a generator:** Fig 4 :25 - *"A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion."* A stereotype is a record of one distribution used as if it generated the next person. It cannot.

**Falsifier:** a pooled parameter that preserves every per-slot residual and still predicts the next individual's own account. If it exists, the aggregation claim fails.

### Q5 - What is the order?

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**

The order for an interaction is:

1. perception: register the other's response as a slot, distinct from what was there before (Fig 3 :4-5);
2. vocabulary: have terms for what the other's actions afford (Fig 3 :6-7);
3. objective: say what would count as progress or respect here, in its own terms (Fig 3 :9-10);
4. planning: choose a route under incomplete information (Fig 3 :12-13);
5. learn and carry: name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next interaction (Fig 3 :14-15).

The break is between 2 and 3. Fig 3 :25-26: **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."** Most training and most "cultural competence" programs stop at link 2, then act as if link 3 existed. Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

**Falsifier:** show an instrument that reads link 3 per slot, is actually used by someone who can change the interaction, and leaves the residual flat. If that happens, the break is not where this derivation puts it.

### Q6 - What is the analogue for repeated interaction?

Fig 7 :36: **"A chain of mirrors is one long room. The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced."**

Fig 7 :26: **"Progress per handoff has two factors. Zero either and the chain runs in place:"** namely *"the bracket's fidelity across the handoff"* and *"the ground's pressure on the room"* (Fig 7 :38-39).

For repeated cross-cultural interaction, the "next room" is the next encounter, which must differ from the last and be chosen against the residual the last one surfaced. Replaying the same script with the same people is one long room: motion, no progress. If either bracket fidelity (did we carry the other's account across faithfully?) or ground pressure (did the lived outcome actually count?) is zero, the chain runs in place. Mode `specified` for the analogue; `general` for the two-factor rule.

Strain note: a room is not literally a physical room in a human interaction. The figure's terms are structural. Labelled as an analogue, no strain remains.

## CONCLUSION

The defensible residue:

- Cultures are not ingredient lists. The relation between the parts carries half the meaning (Fig 12 :5), so the same atoms under a different bond are a real isomer claim (Fig 12 :117).
- Flattening is an identity bond between non-identical closures; stereotyping is aggregation plus a recording used as a generator.
- The order is perception to vocabulary to objective to planning to learn and carry, and the un-instrumented middle link is the one that usually breaks.
- Repeated interaction is a room chain, not a replay. Progress requires both bracket fidelity and ground pressure.

All of this is `specified`, ungrounded. It gives the shape of what to measure, not a measurement and not a name for what respect is in a particular culture.
