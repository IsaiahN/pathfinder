# Structure, order and analogues: why different institutions are not the same safeguard

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two seemingly equivalent reform packages are observed: one provides public financing of elections, the other strengthens judicial review and disclosure. Both are described as "keeping money out of politics", yet one leaves the outcome unmoved under a wealth push and the other does not. Two questions are owed: (1) under Fig 12, are these **isomers** over a shared atom set, or merely members of a **functional group** with different atoms; and (2) what is the dependency order in which an institutional diagnosis must be made?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the two reform packages isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Public financing alone uses `{pinned outcome, disclosure record, counterweight}` with no independent enforcement atom. Judicial review plus disclosure uses `{pinned outcome, disclosure record, counterweight, enforcement body, rotation rule}`. Their atom sets differ, so by Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**: they are two members of the same **functional group** ("an anchor-maintaining arrangement") with different qualifiers.
- The stronger version: write both packages over the full shared atom set `{pinned outcome, channel, counterweight, disclosure, rotation, enforcement, wealth stock}`, differing only in which atom sits in which slot and which bond joins it. Then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it predicts that the two packages differ in behaviour for a structural reason rather than a degree reason.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if one package can be written without an atom the other requires, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. In that case the two packages should not be compared as variants of one reform; they are different compounds and need different instruments (Fig 9 :55-57).

### Q2 - What order must a diagnosis follow?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- Order: 1 **perception** (can the slot read the political response at all?) → 2 **vocabulary** (does it have terms for what wealth affords, and can it say what an unpinned outcome would look like?) → 3 **objective** (a stated threshold for "moved under push") → 4 **planning** (which counterweight to introduce) → 5 **learn and carry** (record the refuter and the lineage).
- The middle link is the one that usually breaks: having terms for what a situation affords was taken for a dictionary, when it is a grammar (Fig 12 :115). A reform programme that jumps to step 4 because disclosure metrics already exist lands above the break.
- **Falsifier:** if a slot with no stated objective and no vocabulary for the residual still returns a stable, unpinned outcome under wealth pushes, the dependency order is wrong for that slot.

### Q3 - Union or partition, and why it matters for the lever

- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- The three conditions from `01` (anchor moved, import crossed, feedback closed) overlap. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing (Fig 9 :55-56). This is why a transparency-only rule can succeed in the region where only disclosure is missing and fail where the feedback loop also closes.
- What remains after a big lever is *"smaller and... categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"* (Fig 9 :57). The residue becomes a specific compound, and it is finally describable.
- **Falsifier:** if one lever clears all three conditions in the same slot with no residue, the union reading is false for that slot.

### Q4 - Catalyst and cycle: the two structures that defeat a linear model

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- A linear accountability chain (donation → disclosure → review → sanction) cannot represent a loop in which the sanction itself becomes a new resource. A chain cannot close by construction, so a model built only from chains will report "no conversion" precisely in the slots where the loop is closed.
- The catalyst makes the loop possible: the stock is not spent by the first use, so the same resources can seed donations, media, and appointments in sequence. What is missing from ordinary accounting is not the amount but the mark of which use is spent and which is regenerating.
- **Falsifier:** if a closed influence-wealth loop is measured with a stock that is exhausted on first use and never regenerates, the catalyst-plus-cycle reading fails for that slot.

### Q5 - The two currencies are not interchangeable

- Fig 12 :125-127: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add. Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- For this question: the bits cost is the complexity of the institutional rule set (how many exceptions, how many disclosure categories); the actions cost is what it takes to actually test whether an outcome stayed pinned under a push (litigation, audit, data access, time).
- A rule set can be cheap to describe and prohibitively expensive to settle, and a long chain of cheap disclosure requirements can be expensive to state while taking one action to refute. *"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind"* (Fig 12 :128-129).
- **Falsifier:** produce a measurement in which bits and actions are commensurable for a slot, so that adding them yields the correct prediction. Then this reading fails.

### Q6 - Competing explanations, kept separate

| explanation | figure status | what would move it |
|---|---|---|
| The outcome moved because wealth pushed it (C1) | Fig 2 :29-30 anchor rule | a pinned-outcome measurement with an independent instrument |
| The outcome moved because a channel crossed the closure and was paid for out of independence (C2) | Fig 6 :21-24 import rule | a contact census showing no cross-frame path |
| The outcome moved because influence regenerated wealth and closed the loop (C3) | Fig 12 :121 cycle rule | a measured loop sign of zero or negative |
| The outcome did not move because the frames were independent (design) | Fig 2 :31-32, Fig 8 :47-49 | a measured overlap near one with no movement |
| The outcome did not move because the rule was declared independent | **refused** by Fig 10 :49: *"Declaring a meaning does not ground it."* | an instrument that returns a pinned outcome while the rule itself decides cases |

These are not averaged. A slot can fail C1 and pass C3, and that is a different compound, not a weaker case of C1.

## CONCLUSION

The two reform packages are **not isomers as commonly written**; they are members of a functional group with different atoms. Recast over the full shared atom set they become a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier. The dependency order is perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn, and the middle link has no instrument. The problem is a **union** of three overlapping conditions, so a single lever leaves intersections standing. Catalyst and cycle explain why a linear accountability model cannot see the conversion, and the two currencies (bits and actions) must be held apart. No new primitive is needed; the arrangement carries the difference.

This framing rescues what is defensible in the empirical literature without the part that fails: "institutions matter" becomes a checkable structural claim about which atom is in which slot and which bond joins it. A named culprit is still an import, provenanced as such (Fig 8 :50-52), and has zero evidentiary weight until it explains a described gap.
