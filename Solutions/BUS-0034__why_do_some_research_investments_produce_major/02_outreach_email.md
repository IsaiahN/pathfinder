# Outreach draft - validate the shape first, then decide about the theory

**Use:** one to one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** the stage-1 shape and its refuter must be stated exactly as in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`. Do not add the framework or the source links until a verdict on stage 1 is recorded.

---

**Subject:** Check one shape against what you already know about research productivity

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific shape I would like you to check against what you already know about research management and innovation economics. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the shape does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The stage-1 claim (`specified`, not `grounded`):** an investment in research is not a stock that buys an innovation. Its outcome is a property of the investment and its habitat. Three conditions have to hold for a project slot to yield a major, commercially paid innovation:
>
> 1. **Settlement and contact.** The project actually settles against a ground, a measurement or a market, and the organization's channel to that ground stays reachable. Loss happens through stale percepts, saturated metrics and pooled readings, not because the ground stopped paying.
> 2. **Reach renewal.** A new atom enters from a closure that differs from the organization's own. Composing harder inside the same library adds search, not new atoms. Without an import from somewhere else, a major innovation is underivable, not merely hard to find.
> 3. **Composition and payment.** A settled result is necessary but not sufficient for commercial value. The result has to become something that composes into a paying product, and the investor has to be positioned to receive the payment. A true result that cannot compose is exactly the case of "little commercial value".
>
> These are three conditions, not three degrees of one thing. A project failing only the first and a project failing only the second need different instruments. A blended "innovation score" pools the readings and destroys the diagnostic.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that this tells you which project to fund. It returns a shape, not a name, and I have no name to offer. I am not claiming that more research spending is the answer, or that talent and process are irrelevant. Those are candidate libraries whose reach has to be tested against the ground, not verdicts. I am not claiming this is settled by measurement.
>
> **What would refute it, written down before this message:**
> 1. an internal metric that predicts, prospectively and per named project, which investment will be commercially major, confirmed by the market;
> 2. a documented case where more search inside a demonstrably fixed closure produced a genuine new atom;
> 3. a settled term that always composes into a paying product with no further atom and no complementary asset;
> 4. a single lever that restores all three conditions in all failure types and leaves no categorically different residue.
>
> If any one holds, the shape is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** I can send three public sources, with the note that the figures, not my summary, are the authority. No request to endorse them.
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** I can point you to the public formula. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and label the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
