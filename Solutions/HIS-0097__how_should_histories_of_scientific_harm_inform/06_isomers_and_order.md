# Isomers and order: the structure of a scoped refusal

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two responses to the same historical record are on the table:

- **S:** a scoped refusal of the particular claim that was false, followed by a graded checking rule.
- **G:** a global verdict that research is inherently untrustworthy.

Both are built from the same ingredients: the harm record, a concern for safety, and a current decision. The question is whether they are the same thing spelled differently or different substances, and what order the response must be built in.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: a harm record, a current claim, an affected group, an institution, an independent checker, a ground for each slot. Operators held: the seven bonds plus negation. Negation is unary and joins nothing; it is not a bond (Operators table :70-73). No new primitive is needed for either response.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are S and G isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Write the two forms:

```
S = harm_record ¬(specific_claim)  =>  graded_check(risk ⋛ containment)
G = mean(harm_record across all slots)  =>  refuse(all claims)
```

- Both hold the same atoms: the record, the claim, the concern, the decision.
- The bond differs. S uses the unary refusal on one operand, then a comparison ⋛ between risk and the checking response. G uses an averaging operation across slots, then a global negation.
- Under Fig 1 :19-22 the averaging operation destroys the slot index, so the two have different behaviour even though the ingredient list is identical.

⇒ **They are isomers**, in the figure's strict sense: same atoms, different operator, different substance, and the difference is invisible in a flat ingredient list. The ingredient list "the harm record makes us cautious" is therefore not a description of either policy. It is the flat formula that Fig 12 :5 warns about: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if S and G can be shown to require different atoms (for example, G requires a new primitive "inherent untrustworthiness" that S does not), the isomer claim fails and the two are simply different molecules.

### Q2. Why the response is graded rather than absolute

Operators table :48-49 defines ⋛: *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, tested by *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."* Fig 12 :112: *"a goal is a comparison."*

A policy that matches checking intensity to estimated risk is exactly a ⋛ relation: compare the cost of being wrong with the independence bought. Fig 2 :42: **"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."** The Asilomar record (Wikipedia, *Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA*, 2026, opened 2026-11-23) gives the world-facing instance: the recorded principle was that *"the effectiveness of the containment should match the estimated risk as closely as possible"*, with levels from minimal to high, and some experiments prohibited outright because the hazard could not be contained. Graded, not blanket; prohibitions where the comparison fails.

Blanket distrust has no comparison. It is a constant function. That is why it cannot aim: it spends the same independence on a low-risk textbook claim as on a high-risk one.

**Falsifier:** show a class of cases where a constant refusal dominates every graded rule at equal cost. The isomer claim would survive, but the ⋛ governance claim would be refuted.

### Q3. Union or partition, and why one lever is not enough

Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."** Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**

The four conditions (correctness, acceptability, justice, independence) overlap. Fig 9 :55-56 explains the failure of a single lever: *"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."* An ethics-training requirement clears the acceptability-only region. It does not clear a case that is both ethically acceptable and biased by undisclosed funding. What remains is *"categorically different"* (Fig 9 :57): a specific compound, not a harder instance of either condition.

⇒ The answer must be four scoped refusals with four grounds, not one verdict. This is the structural reason the question cannot be answered by "trust more" or "trust less."

**Falsifier:** show that one of the four conditions is strictly implied by another, so the union collapses to a partition and a single lever does clear the intersections.

### Q4. Order by dependency: where a harm history enters

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**

| Fig 3 link | what it needs | what a harm history contributes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 perception | tell what is in front of it, and tell it apart from what was there before | the record itself: what happened, to whom, and whether it was visible at the time |
| 2 vocabulary | terms for what a situation affords | the mechanism terms: consent, conflict of interest, publication bias, cascade |
| 3 objective | say what would count as progress, in its own terms | what a safe and acceptable decision would look like for this slot |
| 4 planning | a route to the objective under incomplete information | registration, independent review, affected-community participation |
| 5 learn and carry | name what worked, verify it, carry it | the checked method, not the recording of the case |

The historical record is strongest at links 2 and 3. Fig 3 :26-27: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments. A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* Harm histories are instruments for the middle of the chain. Effort spent at link 5 (a new policy) before links 2 and 3 are settled arrives at a step that cannot use it.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a decision chain in which harm histories changed only the planning link and never the vocabulary or objective link, with the same outcome. That would relocate the contribution, not refute the dependency order.

### Q5. Refuse before minting

`THE_FORMULA.md` step 2 places REFUTED before MINT: *"REFUTED a term held is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation."* Fig 5 :53: *"refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so"*.

The correct sequence is: name the term the harm record refuses, then mint the checking method. A policy that begins with the new method and only later admits the harm has the order backwards; it risks building a method around the outcome it did not want rather than around the belief that was false. Fig 5 :63-64 names precisely that failure: a frame *"can refuse the right kind of thing and still aim the refusal at the outcome it did not want rather than at the belief it holds."*

**Falsifier:** show a case in which the checking method had to exist before the false term could be identified, and where no prior harm record named it.

## CONCLUSION

The two responses are isomers: same atoms, different operator, different behaviour. The legitimate response uses a unary refusal of a named term plus a graded comparison of risk against checking intensity; the blanket response averages away the slot index and then negates everything. The conditions form a union, so the union must be unbundled before any lever is applied. The harm history enters at the vocabulary and objective links of the dependency chain, and the refusal precedes the mint.

This rescues what is defensible in a cautionary reading of harm histories without the part that fails: a scoped refusal and a graded policy need no new primitive, no global negation, and no averaging of the residual.
