## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Does "shape, not a name" forbid naming the checker? CORRECTION TO MY EARLIER READING

- The figures README: "what a **missing** thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)." The subject is a primitive not held in Γ.
- Courts, inspectorates and ombudspersons are **not** missing primitives. They are atoms already in the institutional Γ, arranged in a particular way.
- Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." The difference here is arrangement, not inventory.
- Therefore naming a checker is **not** forbidden by the shape rule. What is forbidden is claiming the derivation selects one, or claiming that naming it moves the wall (Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."). Naming is a **binding** from the institutional frame onto atoms already held, and must be provenanced as such.
- **I overstated this earlier** by applying "shape, not a name" to all institutional content. Retracted as applied to held atoms. Still true for a genuinely new kind of checker not in the library. Triangulated (README + Fig 12 + Fig 6).

### Q2 - Are the two regimes isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 (quoted above) defines isomer: same atoms, different bond, different substance.
- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Contract regime as written: two private parties, a rule, a checker outside both, an enforceable outcome. Personal-protection regime as written: a person, a coercer (possibly the state), a rule, a checker that must be outside the state, an enforceable outcome.
- The atom sets are the same if "checker" is counted once in each. The bond differs: in the contract case the checker is external to both disputants; in the protection case the checker must be external to one of the disputants, the state, which also authors the rule. Under Fig 12 this is a **bond difference** and therefore an isomer reading.
- Where the rights regime requires an atom that commercial enforcement does not have (for example an external monitor with subpoena power over the executive), the atom sets differ and the **functional-group** reading is the honest one: both are "a checker over a rule", differently qualified.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the rights case demonstrably requires an atom with no counterpart in commercial enforcement, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. If the two can be written over one shared atom set, the isomer claim stands and the functional-group claim is the weaker background reading.

### Q3 - What order is the union?

- Fig 3 :20 to :21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- Order: (1) register the harm (perception); (2) have a term that can travel into a case (vocabulary, Fig 3 :7 "it has terms for what things afford: what can be moved, opened, joined, blocked"); (3) state an objective with an instrument (the break in D2); (4) plan a route with a checker outside the case; (5) settle and carry.
- Contract enforcement already runs all five links, which is why it is reliable. Personal protection stops at (3) when the objective is a declared meaning.
- Fig 9 :56: "A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds." A commercial-court lever acts on D1 only. A complaint-channel lever acts on D2 only. The intersection (the worker/tenant/debtor) needs both, and its own loop.
- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." A commercial enforcement regime that has settled against the ground can become an atom at the next level, but only if it can compose with the rights molecule. If its bond puts the state outside the commercial dispute and inside the rights dispute, that composition is not automatic. This is an order claim, not a policy claim.

### Q4 - Why the refusal is not a seventh bond

- Fig 12 amendment :139: "So the operators divide by arity, and this figure describes the binary ones."
- Operators table :58: "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."
- The seven bonds can write sequence (breach → judgment → execution), subtraction (damages) and comparison (which claim is larger). None can write "the state's own act here is false". That is unary negation, which joins nothing and must be supplied by a seat, not minted as a bond.
- **Falsifier:** if a legal system can carry a standing refusal of its own state's act without any external checker, the claim that a seat must supply the negation fails.

## CONCLUSION

The two regimes are best read as **the same institutional atoms under a different bond, with a genuine union overlap**. Under the strong form they are isomers (Fig 12 :117); under the cautious form they are a functional group (Fig 12 :124) whose qualifier is the checker's position. The dependency order puts the instrumented objective first, so adding rights language cannot rescue a chain broken at link 3. The refusal operation is not one of the seven bonds and must come from a seat outside the case. All three claims are `specified` and ungrounded. No new primitive is owed, no import that moves the wall has been made, and the particular institution remains unnamed.
