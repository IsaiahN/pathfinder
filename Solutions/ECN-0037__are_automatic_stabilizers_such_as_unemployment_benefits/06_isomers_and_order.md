# Isomers and order: cycle versus sequence in fiscal stabilization

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground
reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The two correctors share the same purpose but differ in arrangement. The residual here
is: what is the bond structure of each, is one a route to the other, and are they
isomers under Fig 12's definition? A secondary question follows: does the order of
refusal before comparison matter, as it did in the exemplar?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available: the six substrate terms, plus the fiscal atoms read from the world
frame: realised income, employment, a tax schedule, a benefit rule, a budget estimate,
an authorising seat, an outlay. Bonds available: the seven, plus negation. No new
substrate primitive is available without import, and Fig 6 :21 forbids minting one:
*"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."*

- Automatic stabilizers: `income(state) → benefit rule(structure) → disposable
  income(state)` with the output re-entering as the next input. That is a **cycle**.
- Discretionary stimulus: `perception → objective → plan → authorisation → outlay`.
  That is a **sequence** with a seat at its head.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Which bond does each use?

Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to
one."* Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a
subtraction."*

The discretionary route is literally a plan: an ordered `→` chain (Fig 12 :70-72:
sequence is *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*). It also contains a goal, because
the objective is a threshold between a current and a desired state: `⋛` comparison,
*"a threshold relation between two quantities"* (Fig 12 :85-87).

The automatic route is not a plan. It is a cycle: Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output
re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot
close by construction."* A chain cannot close, which is exactly why a sequence of
legislative steps cannot be the same object as a standing feedback loop. The difference
is carried entirely by the bond, not by the ingredients: both use income, a rule and an
outlay.

**Result:** rule versus discretion is a **bond-type difference**, not a quantity
difference. This is a `specified` structural claim. Falsifier: exhibit a discretionary
process that is a cycle rather than a chain, or an automatic rule that requires a
sequence of decisions. Then the bond assignment is wrong.

### Q2 - Are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference
is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

As usually described, the two correctors do **not** share an atom set: stabilizers use
the standing schedule and realised income; a stimulus uses an authorising seat and a
budget estimate. Over that set they are two different molecules, not isomers. They are
best described by the weaker Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose
presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
Both belong to the functional group "corrector of a demand gap," with different members.

The stronger isomer claim becomes available if both are written over the **same atom
set** `{realised income, employment, a rule, an outlay}` and differ only in the bond:
cycle for the standing rule, sequence with a seat for the new decision. Same atoms,
different bond, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, and it is a
structural claim about arrangement, not about effectiveness.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either corrector requires an atom the other
cannot use (for example, the automatic route requires no budget estimate while the
discretionary route cannot function without one), the isomer claim fails and the
functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3 - What is the order of dependence?

Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So
irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom."*

A standing rule that has paid for itself against the ground becomes an atom for the
next level. A newly authored decision has not yet settled; it is a candidate molecule,
not an atom. Therefore the two are **not at the same level** in a given episode:
the rule is already an operand, the decision is still being composed. Any comparison
that treats them as peers at one level commits a type error analogous to the exemplar's
mistyped comparison bond.

Order by dependency (Fig 3 :20): the counter must be reachable before the objective can
be stated; the objective before the plan; the plan before the outlay. Effort spent on
the outlay cannot rescue a broken objective link (Fig 3 :19). This is why the outreach
email in `02` asks about the anchor and the channel before asking about size.

**Falsifier:** show a settled discretionary decision serving as an atom for a later
composition at the same level as a standing rule. That would collapse the level
distinction, but it would not by itself make the decision automatic.

### Q4 - Refusal before comparison

Formula step 2 routes a residual by what is missing and by what is wrong. The fifth
bin, REFUTED, *"a term held is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation,"*
distinguishes a wrong term from a missing one. The Operators table :58: *"a refutation is
a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."*

For ECN-0037, the term to refuse first is the framing "more reliable" as a single dial.
It is false in the figure sense because the question is a union of two conditions with
different anchors (Fig 9 :54-57). The refusal is owed before any comparison is minted.
Minting a winner first and refusing the framing afterwards reverses the order: it
accumulates a verdict that cannot then be removed without withdrawal.

**Falsifier:** show a figure line that directly ranks two policy instruments on one
scale. No such line exists in `03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`; if one were supplied, the refusal
would be withdrawn and the mint would be licensed.

### Q5 - The catalyst case

Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts
behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*

A standing rule is a catalyst in this sense: it survives its own use and is available
for the next shock without re-authoring. A discretionary package is a spent part: once
enacted and disbursed it is an occasion, and the next shock needs a new one unless the
package itself installed a standing trigger. The distinction is not recorded by naming
both "stimulus." It is recorded by the line of reuse, which is exactly what the record
in this folder is for (Fig 13 :127-129: the lineage is the proof).

**Falsifier:** show a discretionary package whose instrument is regenerated by its own
operation, so that it survives its use as a catalyst does. Then the catalyst assignment
collapses into the automatic domain and F3's refuter fires.

## CONCLUSION

The two correctors differ in **bond type**: cycle versus sequence, with a comparison
goal inside the discretionary chain. Over a fixed atom set they are a genuine isomer
claim; as normally described they are members of one functional group with different
atoms. They are not at the same level inside an episode, because the standing rule has
settled and the new decision has not. The framing "more reliable" must be refused as a
single dial before any comparison is minted. The standing rule survives its own use as
a catalyst; the discretionary package is an occasion.

All of this is arrangement, not magnitude. No figure line ranks the two by size, so the
isomer claim is a checkable structural claim with a stated falsifier, not an empirical
result.
