# Union, isomers and loop order in the grounding question

**Mode: `specified` on the application, `general` on the structural claims.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks for one answer to an either/or. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) is the choice a partition or a union of overlapping conditions, and (2) do the two candidate answers, over a fixed set of atoms, differ by bond (isomers) or by ingredient list (different functional groups)? A third question follows: in what order must the loops run?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (compressed; full census in `01` §2)

Atoms held, in effect terms: tokens and their distributional relations, a library Γ, a prediction function, a user community, an outcome that can be recorded, and the operators sequence, comparison, subtraction and negation. No new primitive is available without import (Fig 6 :21).

Bonds held: the seven plus negation. The relevant ones are sequence (→), comparison (⋛), production (⇒) and negation (¬), which is a unary operator and not a bond.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- Fig 2 :39: *"Correlation has valence."* Fig 2 :40 and :41 give opposite valences of the same agreement quantity.

The two options in the question are not mutually exclusive properties of one scale. A system can hold a shared convention (condition C, anchor = user agreement, Fig 2 :41) and lack an anchored term (condition G, anchor = a non-updating ground, Fig 2 :40). Adding sensorimotor contact clears G where only G binds; adding a user community clears C where only C binds; the intersection needs both. This is a union, so one lever leaves the intersection standing.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single instrument measures C and G on one scale and removing either condition always removes both in the same proportion across all tested systems, the union reading fails and a partition may be intended.

### Q2. Are conventional meaning and grounded meaning isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*
- Fig 12 :46: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands. A"*

As written in the literature they often look like different ingredient lists: "distributional meaning" lists tokens and co-occurrence; "grounded meaning" lists sensors, actions, outcomes. Under that description they are two members of a **functional group** (Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member"*), not isomers.

The stronger and more useful reading holds the atom set fixed: tokens, a library, a community, an outcome, negation. Then the two differ only in bond. Conventional meaning binds the term to users by agreement (a symmetric + over shared evidence, Fig 2 :41); grounded meaning binds the term to an outcome that can refuse it (a comparison ⋛ plus a negation ¬, Fig 12 :112). Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in a list of ingredients and decisive in behaviour: the first system can be perfectly fluent and never wrong against a ground, the second can be terse and refusable.

- **Status:** triangulated for the definition across Fig 12 (isomer and bond) and Fig 2 (valence of agreement). The binding itself is a proposed mapping, `specified`, ungrounded.
- **Falsifier:** if both cases can be written with the same bond type and no behavioural difference under a source-discriminating instrument, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3. What is the loop order?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*
- Operators table :58: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*

Dependency order for this question:

1. **Perceive and name tokens**, then build a vocabulary of distributional terms. This link works in text models and has an obvious test (Fig 3 :24-25).
2. **State the objective for grounding as an ordinary term** (Fig 5 :65). This is the broken middle link: without an instrument, a link with no instrument is not a link that works (Fig 3 :27).
3. **Refuse before mint.** The "declared meaning" route is a term already held that must be refused, not a new term to add (Formula step 2; Operators :58). This is the one half of the question the figures settle directly: Fig 10 :18 and :49.
4. **Open a two-way loop.** The tacit-knowledge essay's Clark and Brennan source describes grounding in conversation: the listener shows they have understood, a puzzled look is the correction, and the speaker tries again. That output re-entering as input is Fig 12 :121's cycle. A one-way text flow is a chain and cannot close by construction.
5. **Range against an anchor that does not update.** Fig 2 :45: *"Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."* A loop that closes only between the two participants is a mirror chain (Fig 7 :35).

The sign matters: a loop whose corrections all confirm the frame is a collapse, not a grounding loop (Fig 2 :33-34). The observable that distinguishes them is whether the correction ever changes a term's standing rather than only its wording.

- **Falsifier:** a one-way text pipeline that closes the loop (a chain that closes by construction) would break Q3, or a two-way loop with no anchor that nevertheless corrects a world-facing error would break step 5.

### Q4. Is there a catalyst in this arrangement?

- Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*

A teacher, a benchmark or a conversation partner can act as a catalyst: it survives its own use and keeps making corrections available. But the same part can become spent. A partner who has converged to the model's closure is a spent catalyst: still present, no longer a differing frame. Fig 10 :80: *"Frames whose independence is renewed by separate experience can be maintained"*; Fig 8 :49: *"completely are one frame wearing two names."* A verification packet can only ask whether the checker's independence is renewed; it cannot manufacture it.

- **Status:** `general` structural, with no measurement of any particular partner's catalyst state. Recorded so that "add a social partner" is not treated as a one-time fix.

### Q5. What order is the union?

- Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."*
- Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."*

A term settled against a ground can become an atom for a later composition, so a community's settled vocabulary is a head start for the next learner. What crosses is the method and the head start, never the recording of one occasion (Fig 4 :25). A written corpus is that head start, which is why text is valuable and why it is insufficient: the deriving does not cross with it (Fig 12 :107-110). This is the precise sense in which the answer is neither "text alone" nor "no text."

## CONCLUSION

The question is a union of a convention condition and an anchor condition, not a partition. Over a fixed atom set, the two answers are best read as isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference invisible in an ingredient list and decisive in behaviour. Their observable difference is refusability. The loops must run in dependency order: vocabulary, then an instrument for the objective, then refusal of the declared-meaning route, then a two-way cycle, then anchoring against something neither party can move. A teacher or partner is a catalyst that can become spent, so independence must be renewed rather than assumed. No figure line names the sensor or the community, and no measurement here settles which binding a given model needs.
