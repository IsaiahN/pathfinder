# Outreach draft: check the shape before deciding whether the framework matters

**Use:** one to one, or as a cover note attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the stage-1 shape below survives the check. If the shape does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.

---

**Subject:** Two conditions for when direct participation helps, and what would refute them

> Hello [name],
>
> I am writing about one narrow question in institutional design: when does direct public participation improve policy, and when does it privilege the best-organized groups? I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`).** I treat the question as two overlapping conditions, not one. The framework's own rule is that a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different"* (Fig 9 :57). So the two conditions have to be checked separately.
>
> The shape I get is this:
>
> 1. Direct participation improves policy when the vote stays a **convention** for coordinating what the public wants and remains answerable to an outcome nobody in the process can move. The figures say a convention that decides an outcome *"has stopped being one"* (Fig 10 :47), and that a thing which moves when you push on it *"is not an anchor"* (Fig 2 :30). A vote can be pushed by framing, agenda control, and turnout. A measured policy outcome cannot.
> 2. It privileges the organized when the participant pool is **self-selected**, so the people who walk in are the ones with the time and vocabulary to do so. The figures call that an *"authored room"* (Fig 10 :73-74) and require the pool to be *"unauthored first and examined second"* (Fig 10 :73-74). Three other conditions follow: participants drawing on one evidence pool (Fig 2 :40), a single aggregate that hides a live minority slot (Fig 1 :22), and a ballot that names options but cannot name a route, a goal, or progress (Fig 12 :111-112).
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that participation is always capture, that organized groups are bad by kind, or that this settles the empirical literature. An actor's *"valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries"* (Fig 11 :49), so the same group can help one policy and harm another. This is a shape, not a verdict from the ground. Nothing here is `grounded`.
>
> **What would refute it, written down before this message:** (1) a vote that does not move under agenda control, framing, spending or turnout changes and is used only to coordinate meaning; (2) a self-selected pool statistically indistinguishable from the affected population, with per-group outcomes improving afterward; (3) participants whose evidence overlaps almost completely and whose agreement still tracks measured outcomes per group in repeated cases; (4) a reform that changes outcomes while the surrounding habitat of interests and rules is measurably unchanged and needs no maintenance.
>
> If any of those holds, the corresponding part of the shape comes down. A one-line failure is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **Before you answer, one question:** what would you expect to see in a participation mechanism you know well if condition 1 were doing real work, and what would you expect if condition 2 were? I would like your verdict on that before showing you where the shape came from.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the source material is below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - The figures and formula (public repository, branch `general-agent`): https://github.com/IsaiahN/tether/tree/general-agent/figures
> - The architecture article, including the citation list: https://adventuresinml.substack.com/p/arrival-of-the-fittest-the-tether
> - Participedia, public cases and methods of participatory and deliberative innovation: https://participedia.net
>
> **If you want to reuse the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a mechanism you can check against a ground, and write down what would refute your result *before* you run it. The architecture article warns that outputs *"could never be verified if the solutions presented were not contrived to fit the formula."* A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.

## Why this version is ordered this way

| the move | figure-grounded reason |
|---|---|
| Shape and refuter first, theory second | Fig 10 :52  -  **"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."** The reader checks the output without holding the framework's interior. |
| Ask what they would expect to see before showing the source | Fig 9 :27-28  -  **"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."** Decoding Tacit Knowledge: **"When contact isn't possible, the best you can send is a plain description of what you'd expect to see, because anyone can check that against the world."** |
| Do not ask for agreement | Fig 2 :35  -  **"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."** |
| Pre-registered refuter | Seat Map :287  -  **"And a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."** |
| Mode stated on the output | `THE_FORMULA.md`  -  **"Mode. general · specified · grounded. State which."** |
| Two conditions, not one averaged score | Fig 9 :57  -  **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"**; Fig 1 :22  -  **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."** |
| Organized groups are not named as a kind | Fig 11 :49-51  -  valence is relative to the residual; **"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."** |

## Two-stage discipline (stage 3 is opt-in only)

1. **Stage 1:** send the two conditions, the shape, and the four refuters. Ask what the recipient would expect to see in a mechanism they know. Record their verdict **before** showing any theory.
2. **Stage 2, opt-in:** if they are curious, offer the three links. The figures, not this summary, are the authority. Their later agreement is never pooled as corroboration, because by then they have seen the interior (Fig 2 :40; Fig 10 :42).
3. **Stage 3, opt-in:** offer the formula as a method to reuse. Each user writes their own refuter and states their own mode before running it.
