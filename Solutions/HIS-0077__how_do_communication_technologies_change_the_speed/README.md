# Solution HIS-0077 - figure-grounded package

**Question:** How do communication technologies change the speed, reach, and credibility of competing claims?
**Source:** history of science and knowledge.txt line 94
**Section:** 8. Transmission, education, and public knowledge
**Mode:** `general` on the structural shape (channel versus ground), `specified` on the per-domain verdicts and on the claim that credibility is a channel-amplified, frame-internal signal. Nothing here is `grounded`: this run took no measurement.
**Status:** done
**Registry status:** pending

## What is being decided, in effect terms

A communication technology is introduced into a field where claims already compete. Three effects are
observed: claims move faster, they reach more people, and the credibility attributed to competing
claims shifts. The residual is not "is the new technology good or bad." It is: **which of these three
effects is a change in the channel, which is a change in the ground, and what would settle each?**

- Option A: treat all three as changes in the standing of a claim. Faster, wider, more-credited
  claims are taken to be better supported. This leads to a verdict made from reach and repetition,
  which the figures disqualify as a self-score (Fig 1 :26-27) and as a shared-pool agreement
  (Fig 2 :40).
- Option B: treat speed and reach as channel properties, treat credibility as a frame-internal signal
  that the channel amplifies, and reserve the ground (measurement, experiment, instrument, board) for
  the question of which claim holds. This leads to a checkable shape, refuters written in advance,
  and one legitimate way technology can matter: by changing **contact** with independent frames.
  This is the option the figures license, and it is the one developed here.

What an answer would look like, written down and bound as an ordinary term before the run (formula
step "Direction"): *for each of speed, reach and credibility, name the quantity changed, name the
anchor that settles it, and state what observation would reverse the claim.* That binding is carried
in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 2 and tested in `07_the_tether_answer.md`.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with the shape claim.
- No bulk-send plan.

Three reasons, in the figures' own words:

1. **Agreement is not a verdict.** Fig 7 :35: *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes."* A list selected for receptiveness manufactures the agreement it would then cite.
2. **Correlation disqualifies.** Fig 2 :40: *"agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool."* This is the central risk in a question about credibility and reach.
3. **An unprepared checker is the only usable checker.** Fig 10 :42: *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* Sending the framework before asking for a verdict spends the independence that makes the verdict worth having.

Legally: scraping personal emails for unsolicited bulk outreach breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules
in many jurisdictions. Public institutional and opt-in channels avoid that failure as well as the
structural one.
