# Are a rule and discretion isomers? Bonds, order, and the unary gap

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question "rules, discretion, or both" assumes the three options are alternatives over one object. Two structural questions are owed: (1) are a rule and discretion **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, that is, the same atoms with a different bond, or are they different **functional groups** with different atoms in the slots; and (2) what is the dependency order of the chain they sit in, and can a rule-only vocabulary refuse one of its own rules?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms are taken from the economics frame as bindings, not from the figure substrate. Held atoms: `target`, `instrument`, `model`, `data`, `commitment`, `judgment`. Bonds held: the seven, plus the unary negation operator ¬. No new primitive is available without import. Anchors: outcome data for D2, the convention's checkability for D3, a differing closure for D4 (see `01` section 0.2).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

- Written in the strong form, over the full shared atom set `{target, instrument, model, data, commitment, judgment}`, a rule and an exercise of discretion use the same atoms and differ only in which bond mediates them: the rule binds target to instrument through `commitment` with a sequence bond →; discretion binds target to instrument through `judgment` at each decision. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. They are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117.
- Written in the weak form commonly used in the literature, the rule regime lacks the atom `judgment` and the discretion regime lacks the atom `commitment`. They then share only `target`, `instrument`, `model` and `data` but differ in a presence, not merely in an arrangement. Under Fig 12 :124 they are different **functional groups**: the presence of `commitment` fixes the kind "rule," the presence of `judgment` fixes the kind "discretion."
- The isomer claim is the stronger and the more checkable one. It is the claim worth testing, because it predicts that a fully specified rule regime must still contain a `judgment` atom (to read the slots and to trigger the escape), and a fully specified discretion regime must still contain a `commitment` atom (to keep the anchor from updating). If either prediction fails, the strong claim fails.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either regime can be written without an atom the other requires, that is, a rule regime with no `judgment` atom or a discretion regime with no `commitment` atom, then the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 :117 and :124 are two distinct figure structures), single-source for the atom list, which is a binding from the economics frame.

### Q2 - Which bond is which?

Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."
Fig 12 :112: "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."
Operators table: → sequence, ⋛ comparison, − subtraction, ¬ negation.

- A fixed rule is a **plan**, hence a sequence →. It says what to do next given what was observed.
- The target is a **goal**, hence a comparison ⋛: "a threshold relation between two quantities."
- Progress toward the target is a **subtraction** −: the gap between actual and desired.
- Therefore a rule alone states a route and not the goal, and not the gap. Fig 3 :9-10 says the objective link says "what would count as progress here, in its own terms." That link is *above* planning in the dependency chain. The rule cannot supply it; the rule consumes it.
- Consequence: "pure rules" is not a complete vocabulary. It needs the goal and the progress term supplied from outside the rule. This is a structural result of the bond types, not an empirical claim about any central bank.

**Falsifier:** show a rule vocabulary that states its own goal and its own progress without a comparison or a subtraction operator, that is, using sequence alone. If that is possible, the distinction collapses and Q2 fails. Triangulated (Fig 12 :111/:112 and the Operators table).

### Q3 - Can a rule-only vocabulary refuse one of its own rules?

Fig 12, amendment: "Without a unary operator a system can write what a situation contains, what follows what, and how far along it is - and it cannot write that any of those is false. A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."
Operators table :77-78: "What a unary operator costs is open: an atom slot, nothing, or the choice of what to negate."
Fig 5 :53: "refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so."

- The seven bonds are binary. Negation is unary and is not a bond. A vocabulary holding only the bonds can add rules, amend rules and sequence rules. It cannot write "this rule is false."
- To refuse a held rule, the framework needs ¬. That is why escape clauses and review clauses are not decorations: they are the institutional form of the unary operator. Without them, a rules regime can be corrected by accumulation and cannot carry the correction.
- Fig 5 :55: "What it removes is not a term but a term's standing to be composed over. Nothing leaves the library. What falls is the reach." A refused rule stays on the books as a record; what falls is the set of policies composable over it. This is exactly what a published exception does.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a rule-only framework that refuses one of its own rules using only the seven binary bonds, with no negation and no external operator. If that succeeds, the unary-gap claim fails. Triangulated (Fig 12 amendment, Operators table amendment, Fig 5 :53-56).

### Q4 - What order is the union?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
Fig 13 :115: "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."
Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom."

- Dependency order: perception -> vocabulary -> objective -> planning (Fig 3). The rule sits at planning. The objective (a comparison) and the gap (a subtraction) sit above it. So the question "which rule?" is downstream of "what counts as progress?" and "which conditions do we have terms for?"
- The two loops are not ordered by rank. The coordination loop in D3 (rule as convention) and the adaptation loop in D2/D4 (discretion as channel maintenance and import) are in lockstep in the sense of Fig 13 :4-5: "the two kinds are in lockstep, a crossing is a change of role rather than a step upward, and no member of the pair is senior to the other." Neither is the senior answer; a crossing between them is a change of role.
- Once a first-order arrangement has settled against the ground, it becomes an atom for the next level (Fig 12 :104). A settled convention can be composed over; a rule that was never checked against the ground cannot. That is the order that matters, and it is an order of settlement, not an order of preference.
- Blending the two into one hybrid is Fig 8 :4 "collapse 4: the blend", not a crossing. A crossing changes role; a blend changes vocabulary and adds no atom.

**Falsifier:** show that one of the two loops can be ranked as permanently senior to the other across all mandate states, or that their union is an average rather than a pair of crossings. Triangulated (Fig 3 dependency order, Fig 13 lockstep, Fig 8 blend).

## CONCLUSION

Rule and discretion are **isomers** when both are written over the shared atom set with different bonds; they are different **functional groups** when one lacks an atom the other holds. The stronger isomer claim carries a falsifier and is the one worth testing.

A rule is a sequence and therefore cannot state the goal (a comparison) or progress (a subtraction). A rule-only vocabulary can accumulate amendments but cannot refuse a held rule without the unary negation operator, which is not one of the seven bonds and whose price is open. The two loops are ordered by dependency, not by rank, and their legitimate combination is a crossing, not a blend.

This rescues what is defensible in the "combine both" intuition without the averaged hybrid: two loops with different bonds, different offices and separate verdicts, neither senior, and no new primitive claimed.
