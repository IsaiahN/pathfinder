# Isomers, currencies, and the order of remedies

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6), not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

Two systems can show the same performance shortfall. Are those shortfalls the same thing under a different name, or different things that happen to read the same? And once a shortfall is on the table, in what order must the remedies be tried? The question as asked ("architecture or resources") supplies no order and no structure, so both are owed.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: `closure(Γ)`, `atoms(Γ)`, the seven bonds, budget, instrument, habitat. Bonds used: `→` for the order of remedies, `⋛` for the threshold decision, `−` for what a budget removes, `¬` for refusing a wrong classification. No new primitive is introduced.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Same shortfall, same cause? Isomer versus functional group

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two shortfalls that share the same closure atoms but differ in arrangement are **isomers** in the figure's sense: same atoms, different bond, different substance, and therefore different remedy. Two shortfalls that share only the pattern "some residual persists" but not the atoms are members of one **functional group**, not isomers. The measured curve cannot tell them apart, which is exactly F4 of `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two shortfalls that were classified as isomers turn out to have identical internal arrangements and identical remedy response at fixed ground, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2 — The order is a sequence, not a menu

`→` is sequence, "A then B; B needs A to have happened" (Fig 12 :71). The formula's step 7 orders the three questions: SEARCHED ("is it in here and unfound?"), then INWARD (extend the instrument), then OUTWARD (import). Fig 6 :49 supplies the reason: "more search at the same reach: is it in here and unfound? The closure does not change. Same atoms, same wall, more of the space actually visited." Fig 8 :30-31: "underivable, and searchable" and "Neither answer tells you much about the other. Run them as two tracks, and never read one against the other."

So budget buying comes first, then instrument sharpening, then import, and the last two are run as separate tracks rather than read against each other. The order is not a preference: a step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed (Fig 3 :22).

**Falsifier:** show a case where OUTWARD succeeds reliably while SEARCHED was skipped at comparable budget, and the imported atom is later found to have been in the original closure all along. That would make the order a convention rather than a dependency.

### Q3 — The two currencies and why one number cannot hold them

Fig 12 :125-126: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add. Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."

Fig 12 :127: "They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them and will not notice the shortfall. It proposes arrangements it cannot afford to test, and the budget is what decides how much of the space is reached at all."

Fig 12 :130: "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."

Data is bits; compute and energy price the actions that test an arrangement. Chinchilla's result is a re-allocation inside one compute account, not a new currency. The IEA and Brookings sources show that the action account is bounded physically by power and grid capacity, not only by money.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a single unit in which description cost and test cost are commensurable, such that optimizing their sum never proposes an arrangement the agent cannot afford to test.

### Q4 — Refusal is negation, not subtraction

Operators table: "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate." Fig 12 amendment: "A vocabulary without ¬ can say what is missing from an arrangement and cannot say that an arrangement is wrong, so it can only ever accumulate."

If "this limit is a resource limit" is later refuted, the framework must be able to refuse that classification. `−` only removes an operand: "A with B removed or absent." `¬` refuses a claim. The pre-registered refuter in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §3 is this operator applied to the classification, written before the run.

**Falsifier:** show a system that corrects a wrong cause attribution by adding a new term while leaving the old attribution's standing to be composed over intact, with no observable change in what it subsequently predicts. That would mean the refusal did not happen.

### Q5 — Dependency order across domains

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

1. Describe the shortfall in effect terms (D4) before choosing a frame.
2. Decide whether the term is in `closure(Γ)` at all (D1). This decides whether D2's question is well posed.
3. Run SEARCHED, then INWARD, then OUTWARD (D2).
4. Check whether contact with the habitat changed (D3).

A reading taken below a broken link is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :23). A benchmark result obtained before step 4 is such a reading.

## CONCLUSION

The shortfalls are **isomers when they share closure atoms and differ in arrangement**, and members of a **functional group** when they share only the pattern of a persisting residual. The remedies are ordered by dependency, not by popularity: search the current closure, sharpen the instrument, then import, with the last two kept as separate tracks. Two currencies are held apart, never summed. A wrong classification is refused with negation, not merely supplemented. All four claims are `specified` and ungrounded.
