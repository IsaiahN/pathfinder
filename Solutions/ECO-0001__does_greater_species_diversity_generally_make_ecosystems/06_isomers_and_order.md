# Symmetry, bonds and cascade order in the diversity-stability question

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks whether a count of species predicts stability generally, or whether identity decides. Both options treat the community as a list. The figures say a list is not a molecule and that the bond carries information the operands do not. Two questions are owed: (1) what is the symmetry of the count versus the symmetry of the mechanism, and (2) if the cast is fixed, can the arrangement still change the answer, and at what order of contact?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: species as slots, plus conditions (light, water, temperature, season, terrain) and relations (pollination, predation, competition, dependency) from Fig 11 :45-47. Bonds held: the seven from Fig 12, of which +, ∥ and ≡ are symmetric and →, ⇒, − and ⋛ are ordered. No new primitive is available without import. The habitat is enumerated, not composed (Fig 11 :43).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What is the symmetry of species richness S?

- Operators table: conjunction *"both hold at once"*, symmetric, *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."*
- Species richness is a count of slots. Under any permutation of the species identities, S is unchanged. It therefore has the symmetry of the + bond: k operands have k! spellings and one term (Fig 12 :92: *"Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term"*).
- Fig 1 :19 *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated"* and Fig 1 :22 *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*
- **Result.** S is a symmetric aggregate. It is legal as a description of how many slots are occupied. It cannot encode which slot is occupied, what relation each slot is in, or where to act. ⇒ Using S as the predictor of a general rule discards exactly the ordered information that decides stability.

### Q2 — What is the symmetry of the mechanism?

- Predation, competition, pollination and engineering are ordered relations. "A eats B" is not "B eats A"; the Operators table's ordered bonds ask *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*
- Fig 11 :50: *"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."*
- Allesina and Tang 2012 find a stability hierarchy that depends on interaction type: predator-prey stabilizing, mutualistic and competitive destabilizing. This is a world-facing check, not a figure line.
- **Result.** The mechanism is asymmetric. A symmetric predictor cannot carry an asymmetric mechanism. This is a type mismatch, not a matter of degree. **Triangulated** for the structural claim (Fig 12 + Fig 11), `single-source` for the ecology instance.

### Q3 — Are two communities with the same species set isomers?

- Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Two communities sharing the identical species list but differing in who eats whom, who competes with whom, and who engineers the habitat, are **isomers** under this definition: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The species list is the flat formula that records the ingredients and not the arrangement.
- The weaker case: communities with the same species list but a different *abundance distribution* are still the same atoms with a different weighting. Fig 12 :119 *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* A species that persists through a perturbation is not the same term as one that is spent by it, and a single count records neither.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two communities with identical species sets but different interaction networks always have identical stability under every measured perturbation, the isomer reading fails and the flat formula stands.

### Q4 — At what order of contact does the effect appear?

- Fig 11 :44: *"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."*
- Fig 11 :52: *"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."*
- A species count is first order: it lists what is present. Stability is often decided at second or third order: a predator removed releases a grazer, which changes plant cover, which changes soil and nutrient flow. Jones, Lawton and Shachak 1994 supply the mechanism for the figure's own example of habitat modification.
- **Result.** First-order enumeration is necessary and not sufficient. The count cannot represent cascade order. **Triangulated** across Fig 11 and Jones et al. 1994.

### Q5 — What order is the union of the three conditions?

- The three conditions (anchor, habitat/cast, bonds) overlap rather than partition (Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*).
- Dependency order from Fig 3: name the anchor first (no anchor, no settlement), enumerate the habitat second, then evaluate the count. The count is a third-order quantity in this chain, not a first principle.
- Fig 9 :41: *"a wrong cut removes the answer and speeds up so keep every cut ranked and reversible"* is the reason the decomposition is offered with refuters rather than as a closed verdict.

## CONCLUSION

Species richness has the symmetry of a conjunction: permutations of the cast leave it unchanged. The mechanism has the symmetry of an ordered bond: who relates to whom decides the outcome. That is a type mismatch, so a general rule built on S alone cannot carry the mechanism. Two communities with the same species list but different interaction networks are isomers under Fig 12, and stability effects commonly sit at second or third order of contact, beyond what a count can see. The actionable shape is the enumerated habitat plus the bond structure, with the anchor named first.

This framing keeps what is defensible in both options without forcing a fit. It does **not** say that diversity is irrelevant, that identity alone decides, or that no general law exists. It says the question as posed has a union shape, and that the lever must be described in effect terms before the ground can settle it.
