# Expert verification packet, authority conflicts in the history of knowledge

**What this is:** a claim in a form a historian, philosopher of science, or sociologist of expertise can check. Mode, derivation, and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so the reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `specified`, not `grounded`.** No case-by-case historical audit is claimed. This is a shape and a route, not a verdict on any named dispute.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

A conflict between academic, professional, religious, and traditional authorities closes only when a subquestion is ranged against an anchor that is legitimate for that subquestion and does not move when any party changes its mind. Most long-running conflicts are unions of several questions that were never split, so each authority keeps working well on a different subset and effort does not shrink the disagreement. Everything else that looks like resolution is one of three things: a truce with no new ground contact, a refusal that removes one authority's standing to compose over the question, or a settlement whose record has decayed and been reopened. Which authority is the right anchor depends on what the subquestion is constitutively about, so no authority type is globally supreme.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1, framework-internal, checkable by reading Figure 2

- **Statement:** an anchor is defined by non-updating and question-legitimacy, not by institutional standing. The four authority types cannot be ranked globally.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :29, "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks." Fig 2 :36, "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it." Fig 2 :47, "A ground listed as one of the parties has already become a frame." Fig 11 :51, "No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."
- **What to check:** take a dispute you know. Name the thing each party appealed to. Would it return the same answer if that party changed its mind? Is the subquestion constitutively about that thing? If both answers are yes, the anchor test passes.
- **Refuter:** a conflict that closed by a verdict both parties could move by changing their own minds, and that stayed closed with an intact record. Or a stable ordering of the four authority types that predicts closure better than the question-relative rule.

### C2, history-facing, checkable against a case you know

- **Statement:** a long-running four-authority conflict is usually a union of subquestions, not one question, so a single verdict cannot close it.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 9 :5, "are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search." Fig 9 :57, "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound." Fig 9 :4, "First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset,"
- **World witness (secondary):** Oberheim and Hoyningen-Huene, "The Incommensurability of Scientific Theories", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2025 archive: "one must become bilingual, learning to use (and keep separate) the incongruently structured lexical taxonomies underwriting different laws and theories." And John Paul II, 1992 address to the Pontifical Academy of Sciences: "the different branches of knowledge call for different methods."
- **Refuter:** a genuine four-authority conflict with one predicate, one ground, and no subset structure that nevertheless resists an honest search. Then it is a hard question and the split claim fails for that case.

### C3, mechanism-facing, checkable against a record

- **Statement:** a settlement announced as "resolved" has four possible sources, and the outcome alone cannot tell you which. The three non-triangulation sources leave different traces in the record.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :35, "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." Fig 5 :55, "What it removes is not a term but a term's standing to be composed over. Nothing leaves the library. What falls is the reach." Fig 10 :24, "the ground does not decay, the channel does." Fig 12 :117, "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- **World witnesses (secondary):** Vile 2023 records that in Kitzmiller the court took no position on the truth of Intelligent Design, so its ruling was jurisdictional, not metaphysical. Collins 2021 describes disputed results settling "more like a social agreement than a transfer of information or a formula." The 1992 Galileo address calls the received story "a sort of ‘myth’, in which the image fabricated out of the events was quite far removed from reality."
- **What to check:** after a ruling, can the losing claim still be used as a premise for new rulings in the same domain? Do the reasons survive in the record, or only the verdict? If claims cannot be recomposed, a refusal fired; if only the verdict survives, the channel decayed.
- **Refuter:** a ruled conflict where the losing claim remained fully composable, the reasons survived intact, and agreement among the parties did not grow. Then the four-source distinction does not bite for that case.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not a ranking of the four authority types, and not a claim that any one of them is generally right.
- Not that a court or commission settled a metaphysical question when it settled a jurisdictional one.
- Not that a long-standing settlement is correct. Clean provenance is not truth (Fig 10 :65-66).
- Not a case-by-case historical audit. No named dispute was settled by this derivation.
- Not `grounded`. Every substantive claim is `specified` or `general`.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 2 :29, :36, :47, Fig 9 :4-5 and :57, Fig 5 :55, Fig 10 :24, Fig 12 :117. Eight short passages.
2. Pick one conflict you know well. Write it as two subquestions with different anchors, or say why it cannot be split.
3. Name one thing in that case that would have to be false for C1 or C3 to fail.
4. Send the refutation. A named failure is more useful than an endorsement, and agreement is not pooled as corroboration (Fig 2 :40).
