# Outreach draft, validate the output before you decide about the framework

**Use:** one to one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through a public venue in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** the stage-1 output below must survive the reader's check before any framework material is offered. If it does not, the two-stage offer collapses on contact.

---

**Subject:** One checkable claim about how authority conflicts close

> Hello [name],
>
> I am writing about a narrow question in the history of knowledge: when academic, professional, religious, and traditional authorities conflict, what actually closes the conflict, and what leaves it open? I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** a conflict closes only when the subquestion is ranged against an anchor that is legitimate for that subquestion and does not move when any of the parties changes its mind. Everything else that looks like a resolution is one of four other things: an unsplit bundle of different questions, a negotiated truce with no new ground contact, a refusal that removes one authority's standing to decide the matter, or a settlement whose record has decayed and been reopened.
>
> In the figures' own words, an anchor is "whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks" (Fig 2 :29). And "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you" (Fig 2 :35).
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that any one of the four authority types is generally more reliable than the others; that a court or a commission settled a metaphysical question when it only settled a jurisdictional one; that a truce counts as evidence because many institutions endorsed it; or that I have audited the cases. Which authority is the right anchor depends on what the subquestion is constitutively about. A fixed ranking of the four is not part of the claim.
>
> **What would refute the output, written before I sent this:**
> 1. a conflict that closed by a verdict no party could range against, and stayed closed with an intact record;
> 2. a multi-authority conflict that is genuinely one question, yet resists a honest search;
> 3. a fixed ordering of the four authority types that predicts closure better than the question-relative rule;
> 4. a chain of institutions handing down a settlement with its reasons intact and no growth in mere agreement.
>
> If any one holds, I will narrow or withdraw the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** I can send the sources. The verbatim figure text is the authority, not my summary of it. I will also say plainly that this is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency, not a ground reading, so it cannot settle the history question it reports on.
>
> **If you want to use the method:** run it on a conflict you know well and can check against a record. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state whether your claim is general, specified, or grounded. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.

## Two-stage discipline

Stage 1 sends the claim and the refuter. The recipient's verdict is recorded **before** any framework is shown. Stage 2 is opt in: the sources go only to someone who asks, and their later agreement is never pooled as corroboration (Fig 2 :40, "agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"). Stage 3 offers the formula as a method to reuse, with the instruction that the user write their own refuter and state their own mode (Fig 4 :22-25). There is no stage that asks for endorsement.
