### Q2: What is the structure that changes? Three structures, not one

1. **A threshold (⋛) on access.** The Operators table defines ⋛ as "a threshold relation between two quantities." Access is not a `+` conjunction of customer and seller; it is a gate at which one side may or may not reach the other. Fig 12 :87 "is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes." A firm's "we have users and we have data" is an ingredient list that cannot state the gate (Fig 12 :111 "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one").
2. **A cycle (Fig 12 :121) on network effects.** "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Data improves matching, better matching attracts users, more users produce more data. The loop is the structure. Armstrong 2006 (RAND Journal of Economics) describes the cross-side coupling in economics terms: "To be able to compete effectively on one side of the market, a platform needs to perform well on the other side (and vice versa)." The cycle reads the same from a low-overlap distance: Vital and Martins 2009 (Ethology) name gatekeepers as controllers of "information flow in a group." The world sources corroborate the *shape*, not the market fact.
3. **A substitution of habitat on isolation (Fig 11 :66).** "Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another." Moving an entrant to a different platform does not remove the gate; it reproduces some contact, loses some invisibly, and imports actors it did not choose (Fig 11 :68, :69).

These three have different bonds. Reading A flattens them into one stock variable. Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

**Falsifier:** a single bond that writes all three with the same operand order and the same symmetry test. If that works, the three-structure claim collapses.

### Q3: Are platform business models isomers?

- **Definition.** Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Over the atom set {customers, sellers, attention, ranking, data-as-observation, access rule, payment}, an ad-funded model, a subscription model and a transactional marketplace are three arrangements. Same atoms, different bonds (who pays whom; whether ranking is a sequence or a threshold; whether data is a catalyst or a spent part), different substance. They are **isomers** over that fixed set.
- If instead the models use different atoms (one needs a subscription contract the other does not), they are not isomers; they are compounds with different closures, related by composition plus possible import. Rochet and Tirole 2006 style price-structure results corroborate that the arrangement, not the level, changes outcomes, but do not establish isomerism.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if one model can be changed into another by a change of quantity only (level, volume, price) with no bond change, the isomer claim fails. If one atom can be removed without changing the substance, the fixed-atom condition fails.

### Q4: Are they a functional group instead?

- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Two models may share the substructure "access rule plus ranking plus observation" and differ only in how the payment atom attaches. Then the shared substructure is a functional group: it says *what kind* (a platform), and the payment bond says *which member* (ad-funded, subscription, transactional). This is the weaker claim and it survives even when Q3's fixed-atom condition fails.
- The isomer claim is the stronger, checkable one; the functional-group claim is the fallback. Both are recorded, and the falsifier chooses between them.

### Q5: What order are the conditions in?

Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Dependency order, not chronological order:

1. **Vocabulary before objective.** Can the frame name the gate at all? Fig 12 :111 (above) and Fig 12 :112 "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond." Most platform debates stop here: they can name ingredients and cannot state what would count as independent progress.
2. **Anchor before channel.** The market ground must be identifiable and non-updating before the channel can be judged (Fig 2 :29 "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks").
3. **Channel before data.** Data is valuable as a reading *for a frame that has a channel to range against the ground*. Without a channel it is a store with no verdict (Fig 11 :40 "An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves").
4. **Cycle last.** Network effects amplify whatever the first three conditions produce. The sign of the loop (Fig 12 :121) is measured at the slot, not assumed.
5. **Refusal before mint.** If a held term such as "data stock equals moat" is false, the formula's route owes a refusal first, not a new metric (Formula step 2: REFUTED, "a term held is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation").

This order predicts why single-lever remedies fail: they act at step 4 on a problem created at step 3. Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."

### Q6: Catalyst or spent part? A live type question

- Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- Data and access can be catalysts (survive use, compound through the cycle) or spent parts (consumed in one transaction). The ingredient list is identical; the term is not. Fig 12 :125 "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." A description cost (the ranking) and an action cost (testing the harm) are different units; summing them into one "value of data" is the named error.
- Which one a given datum is, at a given slot, is a measurement, not a figure verdict. Named live in `01` F6, kept open here.

## CONCLUSION

The structure that changes under platform control is threefold: a threshold on access, a feedback cycle on network effects, and a habitat substitution on isolation. Over a fixed atom set, platform business models are isomers; where the atom set changes, they are functional-group variants with a composed or imported atom. The dependency order runs vocabulary, anchor, channel, cycle, with refusal before mint. Single-lever remedies act at the last link on a problem created earlier, which is why one lever leaves the intersections standing. The isomer claim has a written falsifier; the catalyst-versus-spent-part type question remains open.

What this derivation **rescues** without the part that failed: "control over access and data" is a real structural change, but it is a change of bonds and of channel ownership, not a new resource stock and not a wall-moving import. The failed part is the stock picture and the inference from data volume to competitive advantage.
