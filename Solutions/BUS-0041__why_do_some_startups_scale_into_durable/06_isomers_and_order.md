## Q1 - Are two similar startups isomers?

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :124)
> **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** (Fig 12 :92)

Two startups with an identical list of founding assets and capabilities have the same atoms. If their bonds differ, that is, if one composes those atoms as a repeated sequence (a fixed plan executed harder) and the other as a reconfigurable network (commitments that reshape the goal), they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with the difference invisible in the resource list. This is the figure-grounded version of "it is not what you have, it is what you do with it," but stronger: the arrangement is itself a substance and carries information the ingredients do not.

If the two share only a pattern (both run a `build - measure - learn` cycle) while differing in which atoms occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: they are members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), the qualifier saying which member. The presence of the pattern fixes the kind, not the outcome.

The distinction is testable. A resource-list comparison that finds no difference between two startups cannot distinguish isomer from functional group; the bond has to be recorded. Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two startups with identical atoms and identical bonds nevertheless diverge in durability with no difference in ground contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient, and the difference must be habitat (D2), moving the explanation out of D3.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated for the legality of the reading (Fig 12 and Fig 6 :23, different figures), single-source for the startup application. The world corroboration is Sarasvathy's premise that expert entrepreneurs start from a given set of means ("Who I am / What I know / Who I know") and CB Insights' finding that failure follows patterns, but both are the same business evidence pool, so one witness.

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20-21)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)

Order for a startup facing a changed market:

1. **Perception.** Can it read the market's return at all? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests (sales, retention, payment).
2. **Vocabulary.** Does its Γ have terms for what the changed situation affords, and can it say what would count as progress there? This is the middle link. Fig 3 :26: "The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."
3. **Objective.** Can it state a comparison that would count as getting somewhere? Fig 12 :112: "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction." A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot state a goal.
4. **Planning.** A route to that objective under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next problem.

The two conditions from the primary audit map onto this chain: C1 (contact) is mostly a failure of links 1 and 2; C2 (reach) is mostly a failure of link 5, because the venture names what worked but never imports a new atom. That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear both (Fig 9 :55).

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." The market-facing link (1) has accounting and cohort data; link 5 has almost none. So budget piles up at links 1 and 4 while the break sits at 2 and 5. The lean-startup "build-measure-learn" loop is an attempt to instrument links 1 to 3, which is why it helps early and not late, and why the source itself reports weak validation and a critique of incremental outcomes.

**Falsifier:** a startup whose chain demonstrably works at 1, 2 and 3 and fails only at 4. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim of F7 in the primary audit.

The point is not to pick one; it is to notice that the first four are all in one evidence pool (Fig 2 :31) and that the two low-overlap fields corroborate mechanism, not the firm-level fact.

## Q4 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the means-driven emphasis on starting with what you have, as Γ under a closure that has not yet met a differing closure;
- the product-market-fit emphasis on payment, as C1 and Fig 2 :29;
- the timing emphasis, as a habitat condition in D2 (Fig 11 :46);
- the survivorship warning, as Fig 11 :36 and the source-discrimination limit;
- the resilience emphasis on renewal across scales, as Fig 11 :44-54 and Fig 4.

It cannot generate the *name* of the specific move that will make a given startup durable next. That is the `gap` recorded at F10 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 ("Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,") and Fig 8 :39 apply. The source-discrimination rule states why: an instrument that reads an outcome "cannot comment on the source," and "the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists."

## CONCLUSION

Two startups with the same atoms but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117; two startups sharing only a `build - measure - learn` pattern are members of one functional group under Fig 12 :124. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the middle link (vocabulary and objective) and the carry link the usual breaks, which is why C1 and C2 need different instruments. The standard entrepreneurship explanations are mostly bindings of one evidence pool and are generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim has a written falsifier; the order claim has one; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
