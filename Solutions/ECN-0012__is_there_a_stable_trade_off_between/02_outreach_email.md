**Subject:** One checkable claim about the inflation-unemployment relation, before any theory

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified` on each part, not `grounded`):** the question "stable trade-off or shifting relation" is a union of overlapping conditions, not a two-way choice. Two things decide what the observed inflation-unemployment relation is: a short-run spare-capacity channel and the position of expectations, plus whether the driver was demand or supply. A single curve that pools countries, decades, and expectation regimes loses the per-case signal that says where the relation is breaking.
>
> **The decisive test is an anchor test.** An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks. A trade-off that is used as a policy lever fails that test: expectations respond to the lever, so the curve moves when you push on it. That is why a stable exploitable coefficient is refused, while the short-run inverse relation is kept as a useful rule of thumb. A rule of thumb is a convention; a policy-invariant structural coefficient is a verdict. The two have different standards.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that there is no short-run relation, that inflation expectations do not matter (they are the updater), that a central bank's inflation target is useless (it is a coordinating convention), or that I can name the slope, the sacrifice ratio, or a NAIRU value. No figure line returns those numbers, so I report them as a gap with an instrument owed, not as an estimate.
>
> **What would refute this, written before this message was sent:**
> 1. a relation that keeps its coefficient across a deliberate policy-regime change without expectations updating on the change;
> 2. a per-case residual distribution that collapses to one stable curve with no regime shift;
> 3. independently measured expectations that move while the short-run relation does not;
> 4. supply shocks shown never to move inflation and unemployment together.
>
> If any one holds, the union reading is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Canonical figure text with line numbers (local copy used for this derivation): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - The eight-step loop and the mode rule: `FRAMEWORK/figures/THE_FORMULA.md`
