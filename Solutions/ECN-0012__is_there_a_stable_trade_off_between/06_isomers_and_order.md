### Q2 - Are demand-driven and supply-driven episodes isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- Over the fixed atom set {spare capacity, expectation position, price-setting response, supply shock}, a demand-driven disinflation binds spare capacity to price setting with a slack sign and expectations anchored; a supply-driven inflation binds the supply shock to price setting with a cost sign and expectations possibly de-anchoring. Same atoms, different bonds, different outcomes. They are **isomers**, not different amounts of one curve.
- The flat-formula failure: if both are written as "inflation = f(unemployment)", the bond information (which atom drives which, with what sign) is lost. Fig 12 :6 names "collapse 6: the flat formula": "the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not". That is exactly the pooled Phillips curve.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either episode can be written without an atom the other requires (for example supply-driven inflation with no supply-shock atom, or demand disinflation with no expectation atom), the isomer claim fails and the episodes are merely different parameter values of one curve.
- **Live strain S1 (fixed-atom condition):** if a new expectation regime or a new labour-market institution is introduced, the atom set changes, and the relation is composition plus import, not isomerism (Fig 6 :23 "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."). The fixed-atom condition is required for the isomer reading and is named here, not hidden. Triangulated for the structure; strained on the atom-set boundary.

### Q3 - What is the order of the union?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :57: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."
- The four conditions that compose the observed relation: (a) spare capacity channel, (b) expectation position, (c) driver type (demand or supply), (d) measurement convention (which price index, which unemployment measure). They overlap; a single lever (for example "raise rates to cool demand") clears only the region where the driver is demand and expectations are anchored, and leaves the intersections (supply shock with de-anchoring) standing.
- Fig 9 :60: "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict." A sacrifice-ratio estimate is a filter for how much budget a policy has; it is not a witness for the structural claim.

### Q4 - What is the dependency order, and where does the chain break?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
- The chain: perception (measure inflation and unemployment) → vocabulary (terms for spare capacity, expectations, trade-off, regime) → objective (what counts as a non-updating stability test) → planning (identification strategy) → learn and carry.
- The break is between vocabulary and objective, exactly the middle link Fig 3 :24-27 describes: "having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments." Economists have excellent terms for inflation, unemployment, and expectations, and no instrument that returns a policy-invariant coefficient without circularity.
- Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." More precise estimates of a curve whose position moves with the estimator's own policy do not rescue the objective link.
- **Falsifier for the order claim:** if a published method returns a policy-invariant coefficient from data that do not include the policy reaction function, the objective link has an instrument and the chain is not broken there. That would break F7 of `01`.

### Q5 - What is the round trip gap R_T here?

- Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."
- Sent up (x): the verbatim figure set, chiefly Fig 1 :19/:22 (per slot), Fig 2 :29/:30/:33-34 (anchor must not update), Fig 9 :54-57 (union), Fig 10 :24/:47 (channel decays, convention stops being one when it decides an outcome).
- Abstraction (T_A): the shorthand "the trade-off shifts because the anchor updates; the number is a gap".
- Concretisation (T_E): the concrete claim "there is no stable exploitable relation and policy cannot trade inflation for unemployment at all".
- **R_T is nonzero.** Three constraints held in the shorthand do not survive the trip down: (a) the per-slot indexing (the concrete claim drops it and becomes global), (b) the convention valence (the short-run relation is still useful as a rule of thumb, Fig 2 :41), (c) the existence of a genuine within-regime regularity (Fig 10 :24, the ground does not decay). One thing survives cleanly: a non-updating anchor is required for a structural verdict, and the observed curve fails it. R_T was not measured numerically; it is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints. Treating "the formula is the condensed version of the metalaws" as identity would set R_T to zero, the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

## CONCLUSION

The observed inflation-unemployment relation is a **union of four overlapping conditions**, not one curve with two properties. Over a fixed atom set, demand-driven and supply-driven episodes are **isomers** (same atoms, different bonds, different outcomes) with a named fixed-atom strain. The chain breaks between vocabulary and objective, where a policy-invariant coefficient would need an instrument that does not infer it from the readings it is meant to explain. The round trip from figures to concrete claim is lossy in three named ways. This rescues what is defensible in both campaign positions: the short-run relation is real and useful as a convention, while the stable exploitable coefficient is refused as a verdict.
