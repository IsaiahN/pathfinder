## The question restated as an effect

What is being decided is not the *amount* of ocean heat but the *shape of the response curve* from heat forcing to ice flow. In effect terms: an ice-sheet/ocean model library predicts flow Γ(b,a) given a belief b about the ocean state and an action a (a heat or melt-rate forcing at the ice-ocean interface); the ground returns an observation o' (satellite velocity, altimetry, grounding-line migration); R = |Γ(b,a) - o'| is measured per glacier or grounding-line slot, never averaged across the ice sheet (Fig 1 :19, Fig 1 :22). The question asks whether a small increment in a can produce a large R or an R that does not trend down (persistence) at a slot.

- Option A, "ocean heat is a linear driver": leads to the prediction that flow response is proportional and recoverable when the forcing is removed; a small heat change gives a small, reversible flow change.
- Option B, "ocean heat can act at a threshold": leads to the prediction that response is conditional on ice configuration (bed slope, buttressing, cavity geometry); above a threshold a small heat change gives a large, self-sustaining, possibly irreversible response (Fig 12 :87, Fig 12 :121).

What an answer would look like, written down and bound as an ordinary term before the run (Formula, "Direction"): a *shape* naming the conditions that must both hold for a small forcing to yield a large or persistent response, plus an instrument that would discriminate a threshold response from a large-but-linear response. That shape is the sought-for term; until it is bound it predicts nothing and no check in the loop can see it.

The verdict recorded in `09_watson_answer.md` is Option B in `specified` mode, kept per domain and never averaged, with the specific empirical question ("does this glacier tip at this forcing?") declared a `gap` that only the ground can close.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree.
- No bulk-send plan.
- No claim that this derivation is `grounded`, no claim that it settles whether a particular ice sheet is about to tip, and no claim that the framework is the only true account of ice-ocean stability.

Three figure-grounded reasons, plus one practical one:

1. A room is not an anchor. *"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."* (Fig 2 :35). Gathering agreeable readers manufactures the correlation that disqualifies a verdict (Fig 2 :40).
2. The signal is per slot. *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* (Fig 1 :19). A list selected for receptiveness is one witness wearing many names.
3. A verifier must be outside the interior. *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* (Fig 10 :42). The first contact therefore carries the residual and its refuter, not the framework.
4. Legally, unsolicited bulk email to scraped addresses breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules in many jurisdictions and gets the sending domain blacklisted. Public opt-in routes avoid that failure as well as the structural one.
