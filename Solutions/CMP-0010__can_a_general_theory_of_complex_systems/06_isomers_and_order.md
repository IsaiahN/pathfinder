## RESIDUAL

Two supporting questions are owed after `01`:

1. If a general theory transfers a kernel, what exactly is identical between two domains, and what is merely similar? Under Fig 12's definitions, is the relation between two domain applications an isomer relation, a functional-group relation, or something weaker?
2. In what order must the transfer run, and which link is most likely to break without an instrument?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What is identical between two domains?

Fig 4 :19-20 "The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked, whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."

Three readings of "identical" must be separated:

- **Token identity of the cast.** The same actors, currency and anchor in both domains. This is Option A from the README and it is the erasure the question is worried about. Refused by Fig 4 :19-20 and by Fig 10 :68 "Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between. That is why there is no top rung and no regress: a generator crosses, an instance does not, and the level it lands on remakes it."
- **Type identity of the bond structure.** The same arrangement of relations over different atoms. Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Under this reading the two applications share a grammar, not a vocabulary.
- **Identity of the loop only.** The same perceive/route/mint/accept/settle/promote/repeat cycle, with even the bond vocabulary differing. This is the weakest reading and the one that survives in every domain by construction.

The answer binds C1 to the second and third readings. What crosses is the method and the rule for naming what worked. What does not cross is the cast.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a cross-domain prediction can be shown to depend on token identity of the cast, for example the same measurement procedure and the same quantity definition in both domains with no re-specification, then C1 is not the kernel transfer claimed and must be withdrawn.

### Q2 - Are two domain applications isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Apply the definitions:

- If each domain application is written over the **full shared kernel set** (loop, bonds, six-term substrate, rule for naming) and differs only in which structure mediates which local actors and currency, then the two applications are **isomers** in the figure's sense: same atoms, different bond arrangement, different substance (prediction). The difference is invisible in a formula that records only the shared kernel, which is exactly the risk the round trip M measures.
- If each application is written over a **different local atom set** with only a shared substructure (for example a threshold comparison against a local ground), then they are not isomers. They are members of one **functional group**: the group says what kind (a cross-kind composition settled against a ground), the qualifier says which member (this domain's actors).
- As typically presented, a general theory of complex systems makes the weaker functional-group claim. The stronger isomer claim is available only when both applications are written over the same kernel vocabulary, and then it becomes testable, because the two predictions must differ in behaviour while being invisible in the shared formula.

Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom" licences the recursion: once a domain application is settled against its ground, it becomes an atom for the next cross-domain composition. The comparison ⋛ between two settled applications is therefore second-order, and needs no new primitive. The Operators table defines ⋛ as "a threshold relation between two quantities"; whether two settled applications count as quantities at that level is a type question that needs an instrument, not an argument. This strain is named, not hidden.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one application can be written without an atom the other requires, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. If a settled application cannot serve as an atom at the next level, the second-order comparison fails as well.

### Q3 - Structure versus semantics

Marks, Gerrits and Marx 2019 establish empirically what Fig 9 states structurally. Their conclusion: "the more the model is tailored towards the target domain, the more exploratory and explanatory power it will have, the less it will resemble the original model." And: "the properties of the target domain allow for a transfer of the syntactic structure but don't tolerate the semantic transfer."

Map this onto the figure vocabulary:

- **Syntax transfers** = the bond structure, Fig 12 :5. The arrangement is the substance.
- **Semantics do not** = the actors, currency and anchor must be re-specified in the target domain, Fig 4 :19-20.
- Fig 9 :30-31 "your words are borrowed too, so describe the effect rather than the cause." The effect description travels; the cause vocabulary does not.
- Fig 6 :30 "The description travels. Fails by being sharpened into terms only we can read. Cast it in effect terms."

This is why "without erasing important domain-specific differences" is not achieved by keeping the source domain's interpretation alive in the target. It is achieved by letting the target's semantics stay in the target and reporting the round-trip gap.

**Falsifier:** a transfer that keeps the source semantics unchanged and is confirmed by the target's ground. Under the figure and Marks et al this should fail; if it succeeds, Q3 is narrowed to allow semantic transfer where the two domains share an evidence pool, and the low-overlap requirement must be restated.

### Q4 - What order is the transfer?

Fig 3 gives the dependency order: perception of the target's residual, vocabulary for what the target affords, objective (what counts as progress there), planning the transfer, learn and carry. Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

The order for a general theory transfer is therefore:

1. Describe R in the target domain in effect terms (arity, symmetry, scale). Fig 9 :27-28 "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."
2. Name the target's anchor and check that it does not move when the theory pushes on it (Fig 2 :29, :33).
3. Decide what would count as progress in that domain, in that domain's own terms (Fig 3 :10 "it can say what would count as progress here, in its own terms").
4. Only then plan the transfer of the kernel, and only then accept or refuse the term.
5. Measure the round trip and carry the method upward, never the recording (Fig 4 :22-25).

The middle links (2 and 3) are where this usually breaks. Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." For a general theory of complex systems those links are the ones that need an instrument, and the instrument is the per-domain residual table plus R_T.

Dependency is not importance. Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it." A general theory polished at the kernel level cannot rescue a missing anchor in the target domain.

**Falsifier:** a transfer that works when the target's objective is undefined or its anchor is unmeasured. Under Fig 3 that is a reading of nothing, not a weak reading.

## CONCLUSION

The transferable content of a general theory of complex systems is the bond structure and the loop, not the cast and not the source semantics. Two domain applications stand as isomers when written over a shared kernel vocabulary and as members of one functional group when only a shared substructure is present; the stronger claim is the testable one. The transfer must run in dependency order, with the target's anchor named and its objective stated before the kernel is moved. The round-trip gap is the instrument for the link that usually breaks. The operand type of ⋛ at second order remains a named strain, not a correction.
