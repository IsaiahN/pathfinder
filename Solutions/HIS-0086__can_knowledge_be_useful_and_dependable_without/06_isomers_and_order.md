# Are institutional and practical knowledge isomers? Structure, order, analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

We have two frames from `01`: D3, the institutional science frame, and D1/D2, the practitioner and transmission habitat. Both can hold dependable knowledge. Two questions are owed: (1) are they **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, that is, the same atoms arranged with a different bond, or only members of one **functional group**; and (2) in what dependency order must they be read, so that a comparison is made after a link actually works and not before.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Isomer or functional group?

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

- As actually encountered, the two frames do **not** share all atoms. Institutional science has journals, peer review and statistical forms; a navigational or craft tradition has apprentices, songs and swell readings. Under Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers** as written. They are two members of the same functional group: a claim bonded to a ground through some maintained medium. The group says what kind (ground-contacting knowledge); the qualifier says which member (proof-bonded or practice-bonded).
- The stronger version: if both are written over the **full shared atom set** {claim, slot, outcome, practitioner, record, second checker, channel}, differing only in which bond mediates claim to outcome, then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The behavioural difference is decisive and invisible in a formula that lists only ingredients. This is the claim worth testing, because it is falsifiable by enumeration.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either frame requires an atom the other cannot hold and cannot import (for example, a practice with no nameable second checker, or an institution with no per-slot outcome), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. The result is then weaker but still useful: both are ground-contacting, and the specific arrangement is open.

### Q2: What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**

Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*

The order for this question:

1. **perception**: can the frame tell what is in front of it and tell it apart from what was there before (a swell change, a soil colour, a failed replication);
2. **vocabulary**: does it have terms for what the situation affords; this is the middle link where the two frames most often differ (`01` F6);
3. **objective**: can it say what would count as progress in its own terms;
4. **planning**: a route to that objective under incomplete information;
5. **learn and carry**: name what worked, verify it, and carry it to the next problem.

Consequence: adding more proof form (step 4 instrumentation) cannot rescue a frame with no vocabulary for what the situation affords (step 2). This is why the institutional reading of a local practice often lands above the break. A reading taken below a break is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23), so the first question is always which link the frame currently stops at, measured rather than assumed (Fig 3 :29-30).

### Q3: Union or partition?

Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

A claim that fails institutional proof and also fails local practice is not a harder case of either; it is a specific compound. So the census in `01` remains a union. The isomer question applies to the region where both conditions hold, which is exactly the intersection a single lever would leave standing (Fig 9 :55-56). Searching that intersection requires both frames, and their agreement counts only if their evidence pools differ.

### Q4: The navigator analogue

The essay *Decoding Tacit Knowledge*, read 2026-11-23, gives the cleanest low-overlap analogue: "Two navigators don't find their position by agreeing with each other. Each takes a bearing on the same fixed landmark, and nobody can talk the landmark into moving."

This is Fig 2 :35 in another vocabulary and Fig 8 :50 in another setting:

> **"Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence."** (Fig 8 :50)

Two bearings that land on the same landmark are convergent derivation, not adopted import. The record of where each bearing came from is what makes that claim checkable. Without it, two identical bearings are one navigator wearing two names (Fig 8 :48-49).

### Q5: What order is the verification?

Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**

Each first-order claim, once settled against its ground, can serve as an atom at the next level. So the cross-system comparison is second order: `(settled institutional claim) ⋛ (settled practical claim)`, a comparison of two settled molecules, not a merger into one theory. It is legal only because each claim first paid at its own ground. Formula step 5 is explicit: settling is necessary for composition and not sufficient, so the comparison still needs its own ground test.

Type caveat, named and not hidden: ⋛ is defined in the Operators table as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*. At second order the operands are settled molecules now serving as atoms. Whether an atom counts as a quantity at that level is a type question that needs an instrument, not an argument. I record it as a strain, not a correction.

## CONCLUSION

The two frames are **not isomers as encountered** (different atoms), but the stronger form, both written over a shared atom set with only the claim-to-ground bond varied, is a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier. Until that enumeration is performed, the honest label is **functional group**: both are ground-contacting knowledge systems, and the arrangement that makes one dependable is open. Dependency order matters more than the label: the vocabulary link must work before any proof form is added. The navigator analogue shows what valid agreement looks like, two separate bearings on one movable-by-nobody landmark.
