# Isomers and order: same institutional atoms, different bond, different target

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two accountability bodies hold the same formal atoms (a mandate, investigators, a budget, sanction powers, a reporting duty) and differ in whom they actually sanction. The question asks whether they are different *substances* built from the same atoms, and what order the conditions stand in.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held (Fig 13: the six terms and everything settled that becomes an atom at a level): a ground (misconduct facts and law), a checker, a target set (insiders and opponents), an appointment rule, a removal rule, a budget, an information channel. Bonds held: the seven of Fig 12, including sequence → and comparison ⋛, plus negation ¬. No new primitive is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the two bodies isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

If the formal design is held constant (same atoms) and only the controlling relation changes (elected by the coalition versus selected from an unauthored pool; removable by the agency under review versus fixed tenure; budget controlled by the target versus protected), then the two bodies are isomers by the figure's own definition. Their written mandates can be identical, which is why the difference "is invisible in the formula", and their targeting differs, which is why it is "decisive in the behaviour".

Fig 12 :96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

This is the strongest structural restatement of the question. "Why do some constrain the coalition while others police opponents?" becomes: which bond joins the checker to the party it grades?

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two bodies with identical formal atoms and different controlling relations show no systematic difference in targeting, the isomer claim fails. If instead they differ because one lacks an atom the other holds (say, no power to open cases against insiders), they are not isomers but different molecules, and the claim must be restated as a missing-atom finding.

### Q2 — Is this a functional-group claim?

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

The functional group is the checking arrangement itself: investigate, compare against the ground, sanction, report. Its presence fixes what kind of thing the body is, a checker rather than a service agency. The qualifier says which member: a checker whose appointment bond runs to the coalition, or one whose appointment bond runs to an unauthored pool. (Correction carried forward: the exemplar cites this line as Fig 12 :124, but the verbatim extract places the sentence at :123 with the item number "4." at :124. The quote is unchanged; the line citation was corrected by re-reading the extract.) So the weaker, safer claim is: both bodies are in the same functional group, and the qualifier determines behaviour. The stronger isomer claim requires the full atom set to match.

### Q3 — Which bond is which?

Fig 12 :77: "A produces B; B did not exist before" (production ⇒). Fig 12 :71: "A then B; B needs A to have happened" (sequence →). Fig 12 :87: "a threshold relation between two quantities" (comparison ⋛).

An independent checker runs comparison: does the target's conduct cross the sanction threshold? A captured checker runs sequence: appointment by the coalition, then loyalty, then a case opened against an opponent. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. Negation matters too: the captured checker must be able to refuse a case against an insider, and Fig 12 :112 notes "A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond." A vocabulary that can only accumulate cases cannot record the refusal as a refusal; it records it as a closed file.

**Strain, named:** comparison ⋛ is defined as a threshold relation between *quantities*. Whether misconduct and a sanction threshold count as quantities at this level is an application question, not a figure question. Mark strained-for-now, not smoothed.

### Q4 — What order do the conditions stand in?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."

1. Ground reachable (can misconduct be read at all?).
2. Seat outside the slice (can the checker own the reading of the party it grades?).
3. Unauthored membership and protected removal (was the room stacked before the first case?).
4. Habitat enumeration (which actors actually act on 1 to 3?).

Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." Enforcement-distribution data is the missing instrument for link 1; without it, claims about links 3 and 4 are readings taken below the break.

### Q5 — Union, not partition

Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Insider impunity, opponent targeting and their intersection (case choreography) are three regions of one union. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersection standing: Fig 9 :56: "intersections standing, because the second condition still binds." The compound is categorically different from either condition alone.

### Q6 — The analogue, and its limit

The analytical analogue is isomerism in chemistry: Liebig and Wöhler found two substances with the same composition and different properties, and Berzelius named it isomerism in 1830 (Fig 12 :98). The structural fact transfers: arrangement carries information the parts do not.

**Limit of the analogue.** Chemistry has an instrument (crystallography, spectroscopy) that discriminates isomers. Institutional analysis has no equivalent instrument in this package. Without enforcement-distribution data, the isomer claim is a shape, not a measurement. The analogue licenses the question, not the answer.

## CONCLUSION

The two bodies are best read as **isomers over the same formal atom set**, differing in the bond that joins the checker to the party graded; at minimum they are members of the same **functional group** with different qualifiers. Their conditions are ordered by dependency, not importance, and the residual is a union compound, not one hard problem. The isomer claim has a stated falsifier and no ground reading. It is `specified`, candidate.
