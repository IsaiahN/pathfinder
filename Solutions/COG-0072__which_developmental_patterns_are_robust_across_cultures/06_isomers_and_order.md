Two supporting questions are owed: (1) under what structural relation can the same capacities yield different developmental outcomes, and (2) in what order must the analysis run so that a reading below the break is not mistaken for a weak reading of the whole?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Under what relation can the same capacities give different outcomes?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."
- Fig 4 :34-35: "A seed carries no small tree: it carries what will grow one, and the conditions it lands in decide what it becomes."

⇒ Two developmental pathways can share every starting capacity and still be different substances, because the bond (who attends to whom, what is practised, what is rewarded) is part of the term and is not recoverable from the atom list. This is why a "same capacity, different age of onset" finding (Liu et al.) and a "same capacity, different goal of sensitivity" finding (Rothbaum et al.) are not contradictory: they differ in which bond is being reported. Triangulated (Fig 12 + Fig 4), low overlap.

**Falsifier:** show two habitats with the same atom set and the same bonds producing different developmental behaviour under a discriminating instrument. That would collapse the isomer reading.

### Q2 - What is the biological low-overlap analogue?

- Fusco and Minelli (2010), *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* (**world source, secondary**): "Phenotypic plasticity can be defined as 'the ability of individual genotypes to produce different phenotypes when exposed to different environmental conditions'."
- Same review: "Canalization (Waddington 1942) is the term used for the process of change in the direction of a reduction of the sensitivity of an organism's phenotype either to allelic (genetic canalization) or environmental (environmental canalization) variation."

⇒ Biology already distinguishes a buffered trait (same phenotype across perturbation, canalization) from a conditional one (different phenotype in different environments, plasticity). That is the same distinction the figures make between the kernel and what the conditions decide, reached in a field with no contact with the framework. It predicts that a developmental pattern can be robust in one respect (present and sequenced everywhere) and local in another (timing and expression), which is exactly what Callaghan et al. and Liu et al. report. Single-source for the figure reading, corroborated (not decided) by biology.

**Falsifier:** find a trait that is simultaneously fully buffered and fully environment-dependent under the same instrument at the same level. The distinction would then be an artifact of description.

### Q3 - Is the pattern itself a functional group rather than an isomer?

- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Fig 12 :117 (above): isomer requires the same atoms with a different bond.

⇒ If two cultures differ only in *which member* fills a slot (which caretaker, which setting) but preserve the same substructure (a child learns by apprenticeship and correction), they are members of one functional group, not isomers. The stronger isomer claim requires the same atom set with a genuinely different arrangement. As with the QMGR exemplar, the weaker functional-group claim is what the existing evidence supports; the stronger claim needs a test that holds the atom set fixed. Single-source (Fig 12) for the definition.

**Falsifier:** exhibit two cultures with identical bond structure but different developmental outcome, which would break the functional-group reading; or show that holding atoms fixed is impossible in principle, which would make the isomer claim untestable and therefore not a claim.

### Q4 - What order does the analysis run in? (Fig 3 dependency)

- Fig 11 :43-44: "The habitat is enumerated, never composed." and "List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."
- Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."
- Fig 2 :40: "agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"
- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."

⇒ The order is: enumerate the habitat cascade first (a caregiver's work schedule can matter three steps out, as DDT reached the eagles, Fig 11 :52-53); describe the per-slot residual second; seek low-overlap habitats third; only then classify the pattern. A settled pattern in one habitat can become an atom for the next, so traditions and school routines accrete, but each accretion is a new composition, not a new primitive. Reading the classification before the enumeration is a reading below the break and therefore a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23: "It is a reading of nothing."). Triangulated (Fig 11 + Fig 9 + Fig 2), low overlap.

**Falsifier:** show a case where classifying a pattern first, then enumerating the habitat, produces the same ground-settled result in a second low-overlap habitat. That would not make the order wrong, but it would remove the practical penalty the order claims to avoid.

## CONCLUSION

The kernel/conditions distinction is structurally an isomer/functional-group distinction: same atoms, different bonds, different substance, with a functional-group fallback when only the member changes. Biology provides a low-overlap corroboration in canalization versus plasticity. The analysis is strictly ordered (enumerate habitat, describe residual, triangulate, classify), because a classification taken before the enumeration is a reading below the break. Both the isomer claim and the order claim carry written falsifiers.
