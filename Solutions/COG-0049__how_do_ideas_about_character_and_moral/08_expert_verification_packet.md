# Expert verification packet: the Tether on character, moral worth and self-interpretation

**What this is:** a claim in a form a researcher in moral psychology can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `specified` on the four-part shape, `general` on the skeleton, `gap` on the magnitude. Not `grounded`.** No measurement is claimed. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

Ideas about character and moral worth influence self-interpretation through four separable operations. The prediction-versus-behavior gap is indexed by self-slot rather than a global score. The ideas enter as an inherited prior mixed with private behavioral history by a learnable weighting, so they help generate the prediction. The vocabulary supplies bonds that turn an act into a route, plus a separate negation that can refuse the self-attribution while keeping the act in the record. The valence of any character idea is relative to the residual being closed, so no idea is intrinsically good or bad for interpretation. The framework does not name the construct and does not give the magnitude; both are declared gaps.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1: per-slot residual and learned prior (checkable in two ways)

- **Statement:** the interpretation of an act is predicted by a profile of slot-level deviations and by a learnable mixture of held character priors and the person's own behavioral history, not by a global self-worth score.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 1 :19 *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :22 *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."* Symbols table: `b` is *"What the system takes to be the case before it acts. Formed from its own history and from what it inherited, mixed by w."*
- **What to check:** in your own data, does a slot-level profile outperform a single global moral-self score in predicting how an act is interpreted? Is there a stable individual weighting of prior versus history that predicts the interpretation across acts?
- **Refuter:** a global score predicts as well as or better than the slot profile, or no learnable weighting of priors and history carries signal.

### C2: bonds and the fifth-bin refusal (checkable by a framing manipulation)

- **Statement:** act, self-term and worth spectrum can be bonded in different ways (sequence, comparison, subtraction) to yield different interpretations of the same act; and the self-attribution can be refused by a negation that is a different operation from re-description.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :111 *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* Fig 12 :112 *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."* Fig 5 :53 *"a term is owed that says so"*. Operators table :73-74 *"A vocabulary with − and without ¬ can say what is missing from an arrangement and cannot say that an arrangement is wrong, so it can only ever accumulate."*
- **What to check:** does changing the framing from sequence ("this is who I am becoming") to comparison ("this falls short of my standard") change the interpretation while the act and the self-theory are held constant? Does removing the refusal vocabulary (no "that was not me", no "everyone would have done it") change self-attribution and subsequent behavior?
- **Refuter:** interpretation is invariant under these framing and vocabulary changes, or the refusal is shown to be only a re-description with no negation operator.

### C3: valence is relative to the residual, so the licensing conflict is a union (checkable by interaction)

- **Statement:** the same character idea has different effects depending on what the person is trying to preserve; consequently licensing and consistency are two conditions rather than one effect, and a single lever cannot clear both.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 11 :49 *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* Fig 11 :51 *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."* Fig 9 :4-5 *"Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."*
- **What to check:** does the effect of a moral-self prime interact with framing condition (concrete versus abstract) and with domain (prescriptive versus proscriptive)? Is there a subset in which licensing holds and another in which consistency holds?
- **Refuter:** one condition accounts for both literatures with no interaction term and no surviving subset. Then this is an ordinary replication failure and C3 is wrong.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- No specific construct is asserted as the mechanism. Moral identity, self-verification, self-affirmation and moral disengagement are candidate bindings from the psychology frame, provenanced as such, none derived here.
- No claim about the sign of the effect: not "character beliefs always license bad behavior" and not "they always produce consistency".
- No magnitude, no effect size, no population estimate. The pooled `d = 0.31` in one meta-analysis is a budget filter, not this question's verdict.
- No extension of the six-term substrate. Character and worth are bindings onto `b`, `w`, spectrum, slot and bonds, not new primitives.

## 5. What would settle it

1. A per-slot design with a character_prime manipulation and a slot-level outcome measure (C1).
2. A repeated-act design estimating the prior versus history weighting (C1).
3. A crossed framing-by-refusal-vocabulary design (C2).
4. A crossed framing-by-domain design testing the licensing/consistency interaction (C3).
5. A source-discriminating instrument: the self-report reading must be shown to come from one source rather than several, as the spectrometer did for the green reading. Until then the source remains a projection.
