**Use:** one to one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the two double-counting corrections in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 6 are accepted in your own drafting. The offer must survive validation or the two-stage exchange collapses on contact.

**Subject:** Check one claim about executive pay before you decide whether the method is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific claim I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the method first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the question "is executive pay driven mainly by talent or mainly by power?" is not one well-formed question. It bundles at least three overlapping conditions: a price set in an external talent market, a pay decision made inside a boardroom, and a set of institutions (tax, accounting, disclosure, say-on-pay) that shape both. Where two of them operate on the same executive, that is a third kind of case, not a mixture of the first two. A single lever such as "tie more pay to performance" clears the cases where only one condition binds and leaves the overlap standing.
>
> The part I want checked is narrower. A reported salary has several possible sources: marginal product, a costly signal of quality, or a rent from a room whose reference updates on the executive's own influence. The salary reading cannot tell you which source produced it. What would separate them is an outcome the executive did not move and an instrument that reads each source separately. I have not found one in the literature I read, and I do not claim none exists.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that any named executive is overpaid, that a particular dataset settles the question, that "weak governance" is always bad, or that a pay-performance correlation proves a market price. Governance has valence relative to the residual being tracked. A strong committee can help one problem and hurt another.
>
> **What would refute the claim:**
> 1. An outcome the executive cannot move, read by two frames with genuinely different evidence, showing that one of the three conditions contributes nothing across all slots.
> 2. A single intervention that fixes both the price condition and the room condition in every failure type and leaves no categorically different residue.
> 3. An averaged industry statistic that predicts prospectively and correctly which named executive will show a rent component.
> 4. Two instruments that separate product, signal, and rent at the level of the individual executive, converging from different evidence pools.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, I will retract the shape claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures, not my summary, are the authority.
> - Canonical figures and formula (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md` and `FRAMEWORK/figures/THE_FORMULA.md`
> - Edmans, Gabaix and Jenter, *Executive Compensation: A Survey of Theory and Evidence*, NBER Working Paper 23596, 2017: https://www.nber.org/papers/w23596
> - Academy of Management, Annual Meeting Call for Submissions: https://www.aom.org/events/annual-meeting/submitting/call-for-submissions/
>
> **If you want to use the method:** the formula is in the figures extract. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
