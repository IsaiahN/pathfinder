# Are the two pay stories isomers? Structure, order, and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question offers two explanations as if they were ends of one dial. Two derivations are owed: (1) can the two stories be written over the same atom set with different bonds, that is, as isomers under Fig 12, and (2) where does the dependency chain for a pay decision actually break? A third derivation is needed because a variance share and an exogenous instrument are priced in different currencies.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms already held in the debate: the executive's contribution, the contract, the peer benchmark, the committee's decision, the independently read outcome, the imposed rule. Bonds held: `→` sequence (*"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, Fig 12 :72), `⋛` comparison (*"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, Fig 12 :87), `−` subtraction, and `¬` refusal. No new primitive is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are "productivity pay" and "power pay" isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- The productivity story over the held atoms reads: `contribution → contract → outcome`, with the benchmark bonded by comparison, `contract ⋛ peer`. The power story over the same atoms reads: `benchmark → committee decision → contract`, with the contributed outcome bonded by comparison only as a qualifier the committee chooses.
- Both use the same atom set. They differ in which bond carries the information and in the direction of the sequence. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, not points on a scale. A formula that records only the ingredient list (pay, performance, peers, board) cannot tell them apart, and nothing signals that anything was lost.
- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."** The standard "pay for performance versus rent" debate records ingredients and not the bond, which is why the same regression can be cited by both camps.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either story requires an atom the other does not (for example a genuine marginal contribution that has no counterpart in the power story, or a committee capture that has no counterpart in the productivity story), the isomer claim fails and the two are different molecules rather than different arrangements of one set. The union verdict in `09_watson_answer.md` does not depend on this claim; only the isomer reading in this file does.

### Q2 - Functional group versus isomer

- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Both camps share the substructure "pay tied to an outcome". That is the functional group; it says what kind of arrangement is under discussion. The bond type and the direction of the sequence say which member. As the two camps are usually written they share only this group, which is the weaker reading. Over the full atom set listed above they are the stronger isomer reading.
- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."** Both first-order stories, once settled against an outcome the executive did not move, become atoms for a second-order comparison at the firm or industry level. That is why a pay residual can itself be a operand in governance research. Whether a settled pay molecule counts as a quantity at that level is a type question requiring an instrument, not an argument. Marked `specified`, unmeasured.

### Q3 - Where does the dependency chain break?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Order for a pay decision: 1 perception (can the committee read the contribution?), 2 vocabulary (does the contract have terms for what it buys?), 3 objective (can it say what progress counts as?), 4 planning, 5 learn and carry.
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- The contract names revenue, earnings per share, and total shareholder return. It does not name the bond between executive action and long-run firm value. Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."** The middle link fails first. Reform that adds incentive intensity spends budget above the break (Fig 3 :19).
- The contract-theory corroboration from a different field: complex jobs *"will typically not be evaluated through explicit contracts"* (Wikipedia, principal-agent problem, quoting Prendergast 1999). Where explicit measures are weak, subjective evaluation takes over and ratings compress, which is a loss of resolution in the vocabulary rather than a failure of planning.
- **Falsifier:** a documented committee whose perception, vocabulary and objective links all measure as working, and whose pay residual is located at planning or execution. That breaks the "usually at the middle" claim.

### Q4 - Two currencies: a variance share and an instrument

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- A variance decomposition (X percent of pay explained by performance) is cheap to state in bits and may take a whole research budget to settle, because the same number is consistent with several sources. An exogenous rule change is expensive to describe and cheap to refute once it has happened. Neither figure predicts the other, so both must be read. Fig 9 :60: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."*
- **Falsifier:** a single unit in which description cost and action cost add without loss. If such a unit is exhibited and used successfully across both readings, Q4 is wrong.

### Q5 - Catalyst or spent part?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- Fig 11 :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*
- A compensation committee, a say-on-pay vote, or an independent consultant is a catalyst if it regenerates across uses and a spent part if it does not, and nothing in its job description records which. The say-on-pay finding that it can *"hold back sudden jumps, but it would not stop the general upward drift in pay rates"* is the spent-part reading: the part was used once and then became a ritual. Fig 10 :47: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one."*
- **Falsifier:** a governance part whose measured valence is invariant across a documented change of residual and across binding and advisory forms. If none can be found, the catalyst reading stays `specified` rather than becoming `general`.

## CONCLUSION

The two stories are **isomers** over the shared atom set: same atoms, different bond and direction, different substance, difference invisible in an ingredient-only formula. As usually written they share only the functional group "pay tied to an outcome" (Fig 12 :124). The dependency chain breaks at vocabulary and objective, where a contract can name outputs but not the route from action to long-run value. A variance share and an identifying instrument are priced in two currencies that do not add, and any governance part can flip from catalyst to spent part when the residual changes. All claims are `specified`; none is settled by a measurement here.
