# Are two diverse communities isomers? Structure, order and a falsifier

**Mode: `specified` on the ecological reading, `general` on the structural rules.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two communities can have the same species list and different stability. The figures name the bond as the missing half of the description. Two questions are owed: (1) are such communities **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, and (2) what is the dependency order for diagnosing which condition is actually binding?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (abridged; full census in `01`)

Atoms held: species as the cast, plus the typed interaction relations (predation, competition, mutualism, parasitism, dependency). Bonds held: the seven, including sequence, production, subtraction, comparison, and the cycle formed when an output re-enters as input. No new primitive is needed for the ecological reading; what changes is the arrangement. The anchor is a measured interaction matrix and a perturbation time series.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Same species, different web, different substance

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 13 :124: *"the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."*
- Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*

A species list records ingredients. Two webs over the same list are isomers: the difference is invisible in the list and decisive in behaviour. This is not an analogy imported from chemistry; the figure invokes isomerism directly (Fig 12 :97-98, the Liebig and Wohler and Berzelius precedent).

**Reading:** the question's "diverse ecosystem" names the ingredient set. The stability difference lives in the bond. This is the structural reason F1 in `01` refuses species count as an anchor.

### Q2: Functional group versus isomer

- Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

If two communities share only a motif (say, a predator-mediated loop) but not the full species set, they are not isomers; they are members of one functional group. The qualifier, the particular species filling the motif, decides which member. The weaker claim is still useful: the motif says what kind of dynamics to expect.

**Distinction kept:** isomer claim requires the full shared atom set. Functional-group claim requires only the shared substructure. Conflating them would overstate the result.

### Q3: The sign of the loop

- Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*
- Fig 13 :31: *"a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos, and the detail a crossing discards reappears above it as noise."*

A long chain is not an amplifying loop. A chain has no return edge by construction, so it cannot close. Amplification requires the loop and a sign such that the return adds to the perturbation. This is why "many interactions" is not the same claim as "interactions amplify fluctuations". The world literature corroborates the shape: non-reciprocal species-resource interactions generically undergo a transition to chaotic dynamics (Blumenthal, Rocks and Mehta 2024, read 2026-11-23), and positive feedback is defined in control theory as a perturbation whose effects increase the magnitude of the perturbation (read 2026-11-23). Both are secondary, not figure authority.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** a system with no return edge anywhere in its interaction web (a pure chain or a set of independent branches) that nevertheless shows exponential growth of a small perturbation with no external driver. That breaks the cycle requirement and leaves the functional-group reading standing.

### Q4: Dependency order for diagnosis

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

Diagnostic order, upstream first:

1. **Perception (D5):** can we see a single species slot amplifying, or does the aggregate hide it? Instrument: per-slot time series.
2. **Vocabulary (D2):** what typed relations are actually present? Instrument: interaction matrix or field estimate of who affects whom. This is the middle link most often assumed (Fig 3 :24-27).
3. **Objective (D3/D4):** what counts as progress here, in the system's own terms? A perturbation that decays is damping; one that grows is amplification. Correlated versus independent responses decide whether diversity supplies insurance.
4. **Planning and intervention:** introduction is available, exclusion is not (Fig 11 :61-62). A managed ecosystem is permanent work, not a solved state (Fig 11 :63-64).

Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not (Fig 3 :19). A study that improves the species inventory while the bond vocabulary is absent lands above the break.

## CONCLUSION

Two communities with the same species list and different interaction bonds are **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, with the bond carrying the stability difference that the ingredient list cannot show. If only a motif is shared, the correct claim is the weaker **functional group**. Amplification additionally requires a signed cycle, not merely many links, and diagnosis must run in dependency order with the bond vocabulary before the intervention. This rescues the defensible part of the diversity-stability question without inventing a threshold or a seventh primitive.
