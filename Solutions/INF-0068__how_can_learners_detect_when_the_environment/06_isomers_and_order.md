# Isomers and order: three causes behind one drift reading

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

A learner sees a residual on a held term that persists and does not trend down. Three different states of the world can produce that one reading: (1) the **habitat** changed, (2) the **channel** to the ground decayed, (3) the **term** is false. Which is it, in what order are the checks made, and what does a refusal actually remove?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the residual `R` indexed by slot, the bound terms in Γ, the anchor (ground), the habitat (actors, conditions, relations), the channel, and the operator ¬ (refusal), which is unary and not a bond. Bonds available: the seven, of which `→` sequence and `⋛` comparison are relevant to ordering, and ¬ for the fifth bin. No new primitive is available without an import. The three candidate states share the residual atom but are joined to different second atoms, so the question is a Fig 12 structure question, not a new-term question.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the three drift causes isomers under the residual reading?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :94-96: *"The arrangement is the substance. Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*

Map the three causes:

| cause | residual atom joined to | remedy (the behaviour that decides) |
|---|---|---|
| habitat change | the habitat, which updates (Fig 11 :73) | re-bind or mint, depending on whether a new mechanism is owed |
| channel decay | the channel to the ground (Fig 10 :24) | restore contact; output is *"you have lost touch"*, not a verdict on the world |
| term false | the held term, refused by ¬ | a refusal is owed; reach falls because something was added |

All three present the same residue: a flat, persistent residual. The notation (one scalar error curve) cannot tell them apart. The behaviour differs completely: re-bind, restore contact, or refuse. By Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**, and the isomer claim is strong here because the difference is *"decisive in the behaviour"*: applying the wrong remedy either discards a still-valid term, treats a dead instrument as a changed world, or keeps acting on a false belief.

The mechanism behind the shared reading is given by *The Hard Problem is About Source* (opened 2026-11-23): *"If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information."* The essay's green-light case is the same structure: one reading, several sources, and *"an instrument that can't distinguish these cases demonstrates its own inadequacy."* The remedy there was the spectrometer, not a better argument. Fig 6 :32 gives the figure-side version: **"An instrument is not built from a description. It is improved from a worse instrument already returning something."**

- **Verdict:** `specified`, triangulated for the structural claim (Fig 12 + the source essay, low overlap: chemistry-derived structure versus perception/instrument case). Single-source for the particular three-way mapping, which is this derivation's binding.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two of the three causes turned out to require the same remedy at the ground in every case, they would not be different substances and the isomer claim would fail, leaving only a functional-group claim (all three share the residual signature, nothing more).

### Q2 - What order are the checks made?

Fig 3 :4-15 gives the dependency chain: perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry. Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** Fig 3 :22-23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can."* A drift reading taken below a dead perception link is *"a reading of nothing."*

The order that follows, each step consuming what the previous one produces:

1. **Perception / contact check.** Is the channel still returning anything? Fig 10 :27-28 gives the output when it is not: *"you have lost touch"*. This first step is not optional, because the other two causes assume a working reading.
2. **Describe R in effect terms, with the slot kept.** Fig 9 :26: *"R's own predicates name the habitat that holds φ*"*; Fig 9 :27-28: **"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."** Fig 9 :45: *"Describing the residual is the expensive part. Leaving, looking up and recognising are one step each, and cheap only in that order."*
3. **Unbundle the union.** Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."** A case in two conditions is not a harder case of either.
4. **Route the residual into the five bins**, with the fifth bin last and not reachable from the first four (formula step 2). Fig 5 :12-14 for new, mis-attached and mechanism; Fig 5 :53 for refuted.
5. **Within the no-term case, ask SEARCHED first**, then INWARD, then OUTWARD (formula step 7). SEARCHED is cheapest and the other two assume its answer. INWARD and OUTWARD are opposed and must not be read against one another. Fig 6 :31: *"Neither answer tells you much about the other. Run them as two tracks, and never read one against the other."*
6. **Settle at the ground.** Fig 5 :70: *"A term is settled when the ground pays for it. A term is usable when something can compose over it."*

- **Verdict:** `general` for the dependency order (Fig 3 + formula step 7, two low-overlap routes), `specified` for the six-step instantiation.
- **Falsifier:** show a case where a refusal is correctly issued before the contact check, or where OUTWARD import succeeds while SEARCHED has not been asked, with no loss at the ground. If that holds, the order is preference, not dependency.

### Q3 - The two currencies of a detection decision

Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

Fig 12 :127: *"They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them and will not notice the shortfall. It proposes arrangements it cannot afford to test, and the budget is what decides how much of the space is reached at all."*

For this question:

- **Bits** price the detector's description: how many slots, how many bins, how fine the threshold, how long the window. A more discriminating detector is a longer description.
- **Actions** price finding out whether it holds: how many observations must be spent, how many false alarms must be paid for, how long the learner keeps acting on a false term before the alarm fires.

These do not add. A detector with a cheap description (one global scalar) can be expensive to refute when it is wrong, because the pooled number hides which slot failed. A detector with an expensive description (per-slot, five-bin, source-discriminating) can be cheap to refute, because a single counter-example on one slot tells you exactly where the model broke. Fig 12 :128-129: *"A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."*

**Falsifier:** exhibit a common unit in which description bits and detection actions add without loss. If one exists, Fig 12's two-currency rule does not govern this decision and the choice can be made with a single score.

### Q4 - A refusal is negation, not subtraction

Fig 12 amendment: *"¬ is A with B removed or absent? No. ¬ is the refusal of a claim."* (Operators table :57: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*.) Formula step 3: **"NOTHING IS EVER DELETED, and the reach falls because something was ADDED."**

This matters for what a learner loses when it correctly decides the environment invalidated its learning. The old term stays in the record. What falls is its standing to be composed over. Hence the refusal is safe to compute: anything built on a refused term follows from derivation, not from a stipulated erasure, and a refused term cannot cycle back without new growth. If the habitat later returns to its earlier state, the learner does not need to resurrect a deleted object; it needs new growth to re-establish reach. Fig 11 :67: **"Move the residual and you must reproduce everything its goal required, and you inherit the new habitat's actors, which you did not choose."** Isolation substitutes a habitat, it does not remove one, so the two silent failure modes (what you failed to reproduce, what you brought with you) both stay visible in the record.

- **Verdict:** `specified`, triangulated (Fig 12 amendment + formula step 3 + Fig 11 isolation law, three statements with different subjects).
- **Falsifier:** show a learner in which refusing a term deletes it from the record, so that never-held and held-and-given-up are indistinguishable. That breaks the safe fall and makes undoability uncomputable.

## CONCLUSION

The three drift causes are **isomers under the residual signature**: same reading, different bond to the residual, different remedy. The order of checks is dependency order, not preference order: contact before verdict, describe before look, unbundle before search, refuse before mint, SEARCHED before the two opposed instruments, ground last. The decision is priced in two currencies that do not add, so a cheap global detector is not a cheap detector. And a correct refusal adds a term rather than deleting one, which is what makes the decision undoable and auditable.

What remains `open`: the instrument that actually discriminates habitat change from channel decay is not given by the figures. It has to be built from one already returning something (Fig 6 :32), and its threshold must be written down before the run (Fig 5 :65). The figures narrow the space to that instrument; they do not name it.
