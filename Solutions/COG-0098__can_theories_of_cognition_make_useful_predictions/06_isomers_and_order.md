# Is a familiar and an unfamiliar practice the same thing, a different arrangement, or a different atom?

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading. See `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7.

## RESIDUAL

`01` F6 says reduction has two legitimate alternatives: the practice is a re-arrangement of the theory's held atoms, or it needs a new atom. This file derives the separator between those two. Three questions are owed:

1. When do two practices count as isomers, rather than as two instances of one category?
2. When is the weaker functional-group claim the honest one?
3. What is the order of the union of conditions, and which link breaks first?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in a generic cognitive theory (this is a census of a theory's vocabulary, not a claim about which atoms are real): a state-like slot such as a current perceptual or memory contents, structure-like slots such as an ordering, a directedness and a relation among items, plus the operations that bind them. Bonds held: the seven in Fig 12, of which `→` sequence, `⇒` production and `⋛` comparison are ordered, while `+`, `∥` and `≡` are symmetric. Negation `¬` is the unary refusal operator and is not a bond. No new atom is available without a provenanced import. The society's own categories are the candidate new atoms, and they sit in a frame whose closure may differ.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the test for an isomer here?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Fig 13 :124-125: "the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."

So the test has three parts, all checkable:

- **Same ingredient set.** Write the atom inventory of each practice without writing the bonds. If the sets differ, the cases are not isomers, they are different compositions or they require an import.
- **Different bond.** Name which typed relation changed. A symmetric bond such as `+` (both hold at once) and an ordered bond such as `→` (A then B) are different terms over the same atoms. Fig 12 :40: "same two atoms, three substances" for `+`, `→` and `−`.
- **Decisive behavioural difference.** Show that the difference changes a prediction on a held-out slot. If the bond changes nothing observable, the case is a spelling, not a substance, and the flat-formula collapse applies (Fig 12 :6-17: the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not, and nothing downstream can tell them apart).

A worked shape from the world: Segall, Campbell and Herskovits reported large cross-cultural differences in Müller-Lyer susceptibility and attributed them to rectangularity in the built environment; later work by Jahoda found no significant difference between rural and urban members of the same group, and a 2025 review is described as challenging the cultural-byproduct hypothesis. Under this test, the two hypotheses are not two names for one finding. If they both hold in different habitats, they are an isomer pair over a shared perceptual atom set with a changed bond to the environment, which is exactly the kind of claim that needs a local anchor to settle. The source history is recorded in `10_source_ledger.csv`, and the figure verdict does not depend on which side of that dispute wins.

**Falsifier for Q1, written before the run:** if a pair passes the ingredient and bond tests but the behavioural difference vanishes on held-out slots, the isomer claim for that pair is false and the pair is a spelling difference. Record it as such; do not promote it to a universal.

### Q2: When is functional group the honest claim?

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." In `COG-0001` this reading was used for a family of cognitive features whose invariant item was a functional group and whose members varied. Here it applies when two practices share a recurring substructure such as `state → structure → state`, but do not share the full atom inventory. The group fixes the kind of prediction available, for example that a directedness sits between two states; the qualifier names which states and which structure, and those differ by society.

The honest downgrade rule: **if the atom sets differ, claim a functional group, not an isomer.** Claiming isomery across different inventories is a false identity claim, which is the same error as reduction under a different name. Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance." You cannot have the same substance with a different ingredient list and call it an isomer.

**Falsifier for Q2:** exhibit two practices with different atom inventories that nevertheless behave identically on every held-out slot with every bond spelled out. Then the qualifier does no work and the functional-group claim collapses into a single category. That would weaken the valence-relative claim in `01` F9 and is recorded as a refuter there (condition 6).

### Q3: When is a genuinely new atom owed?

Fig 13 :115: "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists." Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs." A new atom is owed when no arrangement of the held inventory, under any of the seven bonds, can represent a distinction the society's practice makes and the local ground pays for. That is a decidability question inside the theory's closure, and Fig 13 :132-133 says a system with a closure can answer it before it searches and what it returns is a shape rather than a name.

But a missing atom cannot be named by the theory: Fig 13 :135, "It cannot say which primitive is missing, because naming it would mean already holding it." So the correct report is: the distinction is unreachable with the current inventory, here is its effect description, and here is the frame to contact. That is `gap`, a verdict rather than silence (Fig 13 :134).

Fig 6 :24 prices the alternative: "A wrong atom costs more than starting from primitives." If the practice was an isomer all along, importing a new atom buys a worse theory. This is the live strain in this solution and it is named, not hidden: **deciding isomer versus new atom needs a discriminator this derivation does not have.** No figure line returns one. That is the instrument gap in `07` and `09`.

**Falsifier for Q3, written before the run:** if a distinction declared unreachable is later composed from held atoms under a stated bond, with the behavioural difference confirmed, then the "new atom owed" report was a depth error, not an absence. Fig 8 :39 says a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence; this is that warning applied to a specific slot.

### Q4: What is the order of the union, and which link breaks first?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." The chain for this question runs:

1. **Perception.** Record the practice as it occurs, including what changes it.
2. **Vocabulary.** Have terms for what the practice affords, in the society's own frame if possible.
3. **Objective.** Say what would count as a successful prediction here, locally.
4. **Planning.** Choose a prediction protocol under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Settle the prediction, name what worked, carry it to the next society.

The break is between 2 and 3. A researcher typically has vocabulary for familiar categories (link 2 is reached in the home sense) but not for the society's affordances or the local objective, so link 3 has no instrument. Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Better sampling and better statistics are effort above the break.

The union's dependency order is different from the chain's: first describe R in effect terms (Fig 9 :27-28), then choose the frame (Fig 8 :53), then decide isomer versus import, then run the prediction against the local ground, then settle. Fig 9 :43: "The verdict tells you to leave. The lookup tells you where to go. Elimination is what you do after you arrive."

**Falsifier for Q4:** if a team reaches reliable local predictions without ever stating a local objective, only a home-population accuracy metric, then the middle link was not broken and the chain reading is wrong for that team. Record the case and narrow the claim.

## CONCLUSION

Over a shared atom inventory, a familiar practice and an unfamiliar one can be **isomers** (same atoms, different bond, different substance) and predicting one from the other is legitimate without importing anything, provided the bond is written down and the behavioural difference is confirmed. Where the inventories differ, the honest claim is a **functional group** (same recurring substructure, different members), not isomerism. Where no arrangement of the inventory can represent the local distinction and the ground pays for it, a **new atom is owed**, and the correct report is a shape plus a frame to contact, since the theory cannot name what it does not hold. The decision procedure needs a source-discriminating instrument that this derivation does not possess, and that missing instrument is declared rather than filled. The chain breaks at vocabulary and objective, so most prediction effort lands above the break where measurements already exist.
