# Successor as substitution: structure, order and the isomer test

**Mode: `general` on the structure, `specified` where a prediction is made. Not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The succession decision is described as "change the person". Two questions are owed: (1) what kind of change is a leadership handover under Fig 11, and (2) in what order must the checks run so a successor is not chosen before the residual is described? A third question follows: are two candidate leaders **isomers** under Fig 12, and what would make them different substances rather than different spellings of the same one?

Atoms held for this derivation: the founder frame, the habitat, the market anchor, the board seat, a candidate leader, live slot residuals, methods and recordings. Bonds held: substitution (from Fig 11, not one of the seven but the figure's own operator for moving a residual), sequence `→`, comparison `⋛`, refusal `¬`. ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What kind of change is a handover? CORRECTION TO MY OWN FIRST READING

- Fig 11 :66 "Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."
- Fig 11 :67 "Move the residual and you must reproduce everything its goal required, and you inherit the new habitat's actors, which you did not choose."
- Replacing the founder is **not** removal of a person from a fixed structure. It is moving the residual into a substituted habitat. The founder's tacit context is part of what the goal required; the successor's prior network and habits are actors the company did not choose.
- **Correction.** I first treated the handover as a subtraction, using `−` (A with B absent). Fig 11 makes that wrong: the habitat is not diminished, it is replaced. The correct operator for this derivation is substitution, which the figure states in words, not one of Fig 12's seven bonds. Recorded here so the correction is not silently lost.
- **Falsifier:** a handover in which the founder's slot is simply left empty, the goal still holds, and no new habitat is introduced. Then substitution is not the right operator.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order of the checks?

- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
- Fig 3 :29-30 "Before asking whether a system is good, ask which link it currently stops at, and whether the answer was measured or assumed."
- Ordered links, using Fig 3's chain: (1) perception: can the board see the live slot and tell it apart from last quarter; (2) vocabulary: does it have terms for what the next stage affords; (3) objective: can it say what would count as progress on that slot; (4) planning: the successor search; (5) learn and carry: methods handed over.
- Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." A search at link 4 cannot rescue a missing link 2.
- Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." The board that has never measured executive decision throughput does not have a working link 1; it has an unexamined one.
- **Triangulated** with Fig 9 :27-28 "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit" and Fig 8 :53 "Residual first, frame second." Three figures, low overlap, same order.
- **Falsifier:** a controlled set of transitions in which boards that start at the successor search, without a written residual, do as well as boards that follow the order above. Then the order is a preference, not a constraint.

### Q3 - Are two candidate leaders isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Take two candidates over the same atom set: market contact, operating cadence, network, capital access, decision tempo, identity story. If they hold the same atoms arranged differently, for example one connects market contact through network and the other through operating cadence, they are **isomers** under Fig 12. The résumé (the formula) lists the same ingredients; the behaviour differs.
- If the candidates share only a job title and a tenure band, they are not isomers. They are two different substances with a shared **functional group**, the Fig 12 :124 reading: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- This matters because the common succession question, "internal or external?", compares candidates by a single qualifier. The figure-grounded question is which bonds connect their atoms to the live slot.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two candidates with the same atom set but different arrangements show no behavioural difference on the live slot across the first year, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim survives.

### Q4 - What is the order of the union between founder and successor?

- Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."
- The founder's early success is a settled molecule at the startup level. At the scale level it becomes an atom for whatever composes over it, but only if it was settled against the ground, not merely celebrated.
- The handover is therefore a **sequence** `→`: the founder's settled contribution is the input, the substituted habitat is the medium, the successor's differing closure is the new composition. Fig 12 :71 "A then B; B needs A to have happened" ( Operators :30 "A then B; B needs A to have happened").
- It is **not** a production `⇒` in the figure's sense: the successor does not appear from nothing, and the founder is not absent before the successor fires. Both are present; the bond changes.
- It is **not** an identity `≡`. Fig 12 :67 "two names, one referent". A successor who is a renamed founder is Fig 8 :49 "one frame wearing two names", and adds no surplus.
- **Falsifier:** show that the handover is genuinely a production, that is the new leader's capability did not exist in any form before the founder's contribution was settled. Then `⇒` is the right bond and this Q4 is wrong.

### Q5 - Is the founder a catalyst?

- Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- A founder who is regenerated by each new stage can serve as an atom at the next level. A founder who is spent cannot. Nothing in the figure records which, so the derivation must not decide it.
- This is the open item carried into F13 of `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`. It needs an instrument, not an argument.
- **Falsifier:** a measure of decision quality across stages that shows the founder is regenerated rather than spent, on a slot named in advance. Then the open item closes.

## CONCLUSION

A leadership handover is a **substitution of habitat**, not a subtraction of a person (Q1). The checks run in **dependency order**: anchor, vocabulary, live slot, then successor search, then methods (Q2). Two candidates with the same atoms arranged differently are **isomers** whose behavioural difference is invisible on the résumé; candidates who share only a title are a functional group (Q3). The founder-to-successor move is a **sequence**, not a production and not an identity (Q4). Whether the founder is a **catalyst** is open and needs an instrument (Q5).

This rescues what is defensible in the plain "replace the founder" framing without adding a size threshold, a named candidate, or a claim that the transition itself changes contact.
