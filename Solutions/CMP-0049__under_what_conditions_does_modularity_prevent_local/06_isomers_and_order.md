## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the two architectures isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :94-96: "The arrangement is the substance." and "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

The two architectures share the same ingredient set: nodes, modules, intra-module links, inter-module links, a failure and a propagation channel. What differs is the bond pattern, that is which links are dense and which are sparse, and which class of propagation the boundary passes. By Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. A single scalar Q is the flat formula that cannot tell them apart, exactly the collapse Fig 12 names: "the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not... nothing downstream can tell them apart, and nothing signals that anything was lost."

**Status:** `triangulated` for the structural claim (Fig 12 definition plus Fig 13 network-as-structure, two figures with low overlap). The physics-facing check (are the two reported architectures genuinely the same atom set?) is `specified` and unmeasured for the specific datasets.

### Q2 — Are they a functional group as well?

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Both architectures contain the same functional group: a boundary between a failing region and the rest, with a direction. The qualifier is which class of propagation the boundary passes. So the honest two-level claim is:

- Kind: a boundary that filters (functional group, present in both).
- Members: the compartmented and the long-bridge architectures (isomers within the group).

This maps cleanly onto Fig 4 :21: "The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a filter with a direction." The rule is the kind; the direction and the passed class are the member.

### Q3 — What is the order of the two conditions?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

Fig 9 :54: "unbundle before you search again."

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."

The dependency order is:

1. **Perceive the failing slot** (Fig 1 :19). Without a per-slot residual there is nothing to propagate and nothing to contain.
2. **Enumerate the contact chain** outward until the cascade stops mattering (Fig 11 :44). This fixes which modules are really in contact, including links not drawn on the diagram.
3. **Name the propagation class** (overload, exposure, threshold, common cause). This is the dynamics condition.
4. **State which boundary bond passes or blocks that class** (Fig 4 :21). This is the structure condition, and it consumes step 3; you cannot say a boundary filters "failure" without saying what kind of failure.
5. **Only then** compare architectures and read a containment outcome.

Effort spent at step 5 before steps 3 and 4 is effort above the break: a global Q score arrives at a step that cannot use it. This is audit F6 stated as an order.

### Q4 — What is the union, and why can it not be averaged?

Fig 8 :45: "Two frames together reach what neither reaches alone."

Fig 9 :57: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."

Fig 1 :22: "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."

The union of structure and dynamics is not the average of two modularity scores. It is a specific compound with its own loop. Averaging would discard exactly the per-slot signal that says where the failure is, and it would erase the direction of the membrane. The two conditions are held apart, and each keeps its own verdict (F3 structure, F6 union), never pooled into one number.

### Q5 — What order is the analogy itself?

Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."

The correct order for importing the network-science results is: describe the containment-shape in effect terms first (a boundary that filters a named channel, independent modules, no hidden shared source), then find the frame whose closure predicts that shape (membrane, independence, common cause). The reverse order, picking "modularity" first and then looking for confirmations, is the inversion the figure forbids. This is why the outreach material withholds the framework until stage 2.

## CONCLUSION

The two reported architectures are **isomers over a shared functional group**, not two measurements of one lever. They differ in the bond pattern and in the propagation class their boundary passes, and a single Q value is the flat notation that cannot tell them apart. The order of the two conditions is fixed by dependency: slot, contact chain, propagation class, boundary filter, outcome. Geometry alone lands above the break. This framing rescues both world results without forcing either into a "modularity always helps" or "modularity always hurts" verdict.

## FALSIFIER, WRITTEN BEFORE THE RUN

If either architecture can be realised with the identical boundary rule, identical propagation class and identical measured overlap, yet still produces opposite containment outcomes, the isomer reading fails and the difference must be sought in an atom neither architecture was credited with. If a single global modularity value predicts containment across both architectures without any propagation-class term, the union reading fails and the two results really are one lever measured twice.
