**Mode: `specified` on the moral-psychology application, `general` on the structural claims.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## DERIVATION

### Q1a - Could the three be isomers over the six-term substrate? No, this is a category mistake

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- The six terms of Fig 13 are space, time, energy, spectrum, gradient, network. A moral disagreement is not composed from those as its atoms. Testing whether "values" and "facts" are isomers over the physical substrate would assign the wrong atoms to the bond and would strain Fig 13, so the re-census rule of the figures README forbids it.
- **Refuter held:** if someone derives a moral comparison term directly from the six physical terms by composition alone, the category reading is wrong and this sub-answer is withdrawn.

### Q1b - Is there a genuine isomer claim at the frame level? Yes, and it is the useful one

- Consider two disagreements with the **same atoms**: the same act, the same record of consequences, the same comparison terms available to both frames, the same two judges.
- Disagreement 1 is ranged against the **record** (Fig 2 :29); its live bond is a comparison ⋛ between the record reading and the claim.
- Disagreement 2 is ranged against **human judgment** as the legitimate anchor for a question about people (Fig 2 :37); its live bond is a different comparison, between the goal terms the frames hold.
- Same atoms, different bond, different substance. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: the difference is invisible in the ingredient list ("both are moral disagreements about harm") and decisive in behaviour (one shrinks against a record, the other does not).
- This is the structural reason why asking "is this a values or a facts disagreement" from the content alone cannot work: the content lists the atoms, and the bond is what decides. Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two disagreements with the same atoms and the stated different bonds show the same residual behaviour under effort (both shrink, or neither does), the isomer claim fails and "values versus facts" collapses back into one undifferentiated condition.

### Q1c - And "social interests"? A collapse, not an isomer

- Fig 8 :48-49: two frames that overlap almost completely are *"one frame wearing two names."* A shared evidence pool is not a third arrangement over the same atoms; it is the reduction of two frames to one.
- Fig 2 :13-15 and :18-21 are the two collapse panels. Neither is a bond over a fixed atom set in the sense of Fig 12; they are failures of the checking arrangement.
- So the answer to Q1 is: **two of the three are isomers at the frame level over a fixed atom set; the third is not an isomer at all.** The verdicts must be kept separate (Fig 1 :19).

### Q2 - What order is the union?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** The order is perception → vocabulary → objective → planning → learn and carry.
- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."** Unbundling precedes search; it is not a step inside it.
- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."** Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**
- Dependency order for this question:
  1. Describe R per slot (what act, which frame, which judgment).
  2. Test for an anchor: does something settle the contested point without moving when either argues (Fig 2 :29-30)?
  3. Test shrinkage: does R fall when both range against it (Fig 2 :45)? If yes, D1 governs; rebind (Fig 5 :13).
  4. If not, test subset structure: does each rule keep working well on a different subset (Fig 9 :4-5)? If yes, split; the differing objective is a comparison term (Operators :51), D2 governs.
  5. At every step test independence: do the frames draw on one evidence pool (Fig 2 :31, :40)? If so, buy independence before believing any verdict (Fig 2 :42).
  6. Only then plan an intervention. Effort above the break cannot rescue a link whose input never arrived (Fig 3 :19).
- A union is not a sequence of causes added together. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing (Fig 9 :55-57). The shared fraction cannot be assigned by inspection; that is the `gap` of F10, not a number to be estimated from a pooled correlation (Fig 9 :60).

### Q3 - What composes across the two kinds here, and what does not

- Fig 13 :74: **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."** At the frame level the two kinds are the *state* of the world (what happened, a record) and the *structure* of the comparison (which goal, which threshold). A moral judgment composes a state with a structure; it does not compose two records directly, nor two goals directly, without an intervening opposite-kind term.
- This is why the is/ought asymmetry holds (Greene 2003) and why F6 refuses to orient the attribution from a symmetric disagreement alone. A record constrains a goal; a goal does not constrain what happened.
- **Strain check:** Fig 13 governs the six physical primitives; the statement above governs frame-level states and structures by analogy. This is a mapping, not a derivation. Recorded as a strain and not hidden; it does not change the verdict because the verdict rests on Fig 2, Fig 9, Fig 5 and Operators :51, not on Fig 13.

## CONCLUSION

The three offered answers are not isomers over the physical substrate; that framing is a category mistake. At the frame level, two of them (a record-ranged disagreement and a human-judgment-ranged disagreement with the same atoms) **are** isomers under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with a stated falsifier. The third, "social interest," is a collapse of independence rather than an isomer. The order is a dependency chain, with unbundling before search (Fig 9 :54) and independence testing at every step. No figure line returns the shared fraction (F10).
