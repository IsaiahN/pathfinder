**Subject:** One checkable claim about the observer in computation, before any theory

> Hi [name],
>
> I am working on a narrow question in the foundations of computation: what role does the observer play in deciding which physical states count as computational states? I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If this output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the question is two conditions bundled into one. One condition is the mapping the observer declares (which group of microphysical states is a computational state, which counts as an input or an output). The other condition is whether the physical system actually supports that mapping. These come apart: different observers can declare different mappings, while the physical system does not change on their account.
>
> The claim is about the observer's office, not a state list. Under the framework I am using, the observer authors the convention and makes the gap statable, but it does not decide the outcome. A convention that decides an outcome has stopped being a convention. The ground, a measurement or controlled intervention that does not move when the observer changes, settles whether the system supports the mapping. So the observer's role is seat-like: declare, characterise, range against the ground, and refuse a failed mapping. It is not the anchor.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that a specific set of physical states in a named system is the computational state set. The framework returns a shape, not a name. I am also not claiming that the observer is irrelevant, that the mapping is read off the physics alone, or that agreement among observers makes the mapping true. And this is not a result: it is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a measurement.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. an observer's assignment shown to be a legitimate anchor, that is, it does not move when the observer is pushed on and settles the question without a measurement;
> 2. a frame certifying its own state assignment without a ground that cannot be moved by that frame;
> 3. a demonstration that the convention condition and the ground condition are one question, not two.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any holds, the claim is withdrawn. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the public sources. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Computation in Physical Systems": https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/computation-physicalsystems/
> - Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "The Computational Theory of Mind": https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/computational-mind/
> - Andre Curtis-Trudel, "Computation in Context", Erkenntnis (open access): https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-024-00851-2
> - Canonical figures extract with line numbers (local to the project): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
>  -  [your name]

**Audience note.** In philosophy of computation the most useful first readers are those who work on implementation, pancomputationalism or the observer-relativity debate and can point to a failed prediction. That is a ground-visible filter, not a receptiveness filter. Fig 2 :40 disqualifies a verdict drawn from people chosen because they are likely to agree.
