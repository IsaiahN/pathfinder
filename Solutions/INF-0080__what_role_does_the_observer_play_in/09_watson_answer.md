## QUESTION

Decide, in effect terms, the office of the observer in the assignment of computational states to physical states. Option A: the observer's declaration decides which states count (observer-relativity as a verdict). Option B: the observer authors the convention, makes the gap statable and ranges the frame against a ground, while the ground settles whether the system supports the mapping. What each leads to: A makes the convention a verdict and collapses into the claim that every system implements every computation; B gives a checkable shape, three refuters written in advance and an open state list until a source-discriminating instrument closes it.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain with actors, currency and anchor; partition or union; order by dependency.

- **R (the residual).** The gap between a physical state description and a computational state description of the same system, together with the observer proposing the mapping. Arity: a typed relation between two inventories, with a third term (the observer) in the world literature's three-place formulation. Symmetry: under permutation of the observer the assignment changes, which is the crux of the question. Scale: microphysical states to computational macro-states, a membrane crossing. Fig 9 names it a union compound: the convention condition and the physical support condition must be unbundled.
- **Domain 1, physical ground.** Actors: physical states, microphysical states, transitions, fields, energy. Currency: physical prediction, space, time and energy as states. Anchor: a measurement or controlled intervention that does not move when a frame pushes on it (Fig 2 :29-30).
- **Domain 2, computational description.** Actors: computational states, transition function, labelling scheme or coarse-graining. Currency: description length and the typed bond (Fig 12). Anchor: a correctness check of a run, legitimate but *"only an instrument"* for questions not constitutively about code correctness (Fig 2 :37-38).
- **Domain 3, observer / seat.** Actors: the describing agent, the frame that assigns syntax, the instrument builder. Currency: conventions, declared meanings, labels, the weighting `w`. Anchor: the ground it ranges against; it does not author the verdict (Fig 10 :43-44).
- **Order by dependency.** Perceive the physical states, then build the vocabulary for what states afford, then state the objective, then plan the mapping, then verify and carry (Fig 3 :4-15). Describe the residual before choosing the frame (Fig 8 :53). Refuse a failed mapping before minting a new one (Formula step 2).

## WORLD (secondary sources read)

World sources are corroboration only. Their overlap is declared rather than hidden. None overrules the figure verdict.

- **Philosophy of computation / foundations of computer science.** Gualtiero Piccinini and Matthew Anderson, "Computation in Physical Systems", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2010, revised 2025: *"John Searle (1992) explicitly argues that whether a physical system implements a computation depends on how an observer interprets the system; therefore, for any sufficiently complex object and for any computation, the object can be described as implementing the computation."* Predicts that observer-relativity is the live position and that a free mapping trivialises computation; corroborates Option A as the failure mode and F1. Overlap: high with the other SEP entry and the Putnam-Searle literature.
- **Philosophy of mind / cognitive science.** Michael Rescorla, "The Computational Theory of Mind", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2015, revised 2024: an object is a computer *"not in virtue of its intrinsic properties, but only in relation to an interpretation."* Predicts that the assignment is relational rather than intrinsic; corroborates the bond reading (F7). Overlap: high with the previous entry.
- **Philosophy of science / computation.** Andre Curtis-Trudel, "Computation in Context", Erkenntnis, 2024/2025: *"implementation is a three-place relation between a physical system, computational model, and a labelling scheme."* Also *"in engineering practice this decision is guided by a variety of considerations about how to best build devices that meet our needs."* Predicts that the observer supplies a scheme constrained by context and purpose, not by free choice; corroborates F1 and F6. Overlap: moderate, shares the Putnam-Searle starting point but offers a distinct positive account.
- **Philosophy of mind.** John R. - **Computer science / philosophy of computation.** Graham White, "Observer Relativity, Physical Properties and Computation", AISB50 symposium, 2014: examines Searle's argument and holds that the intensionality of explanatory language *"does not warrant the leap to observer-relativity."* Predicts that observer-relativity is not established by the triviality argument alone; **conflicts** with the strong Option A and therefore supports treating it as a failure mode. - **Computer science / models of computation.** Peter Leupold, "What is the Role of the Observer in a Computation?", AISB50 symposium, 2014: *"what is computed, is extremely relative to the observer in this case."* Predicts strong observer dependence under a Computing by Observing model; **conflicts** with a purely ground-fixed reading and corroborates the conventional component of the shape. Overlap: moderate, same symposium as White but a distinct technical model.
- **Cognitive science / philosophy of computation.** Ronald L. Overlap: moderate, shares the Searle target.

## VERDICT (from the figures)

The office of the observer is decided; the specific state list is not. The observer authors the convention mapping physical states to computational states, makes the residual statable, selects the frame after describing the residual and ranges that frame against a ground it cannot author. It does not decide which states count. If its declaration settles the outcome, the convention has become a verdict (Fig 10 :47) and the account collapses into the triviality the world literature documents. The mapping is a typed bond, so two labellings of one system are isomers in Fig 12's sense. Which specific states count, for any given system, is a `gap`: the framework returns a shape, not a name, and only a source-discriminating instrument can close it. This is a `specified`, ungrounded, `candidate` result.

- **cost if wrong:** bounded and locatable. The packet states its mode, three refuters and the union split before the conclusion, so an error is findable rather than invisible. The real cost is spending the independence of outside readers by sending a recording instead of a generator, which Fig 2 :42 prices: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."* That cost is avoidable by leading with the residual and its refuter.
- **undoable:** yes. The shape claim is removable without touching the derivation, and no physical atom has to be withdrawn. A sent claim can be superseded by a corrected packet. The specific-state-list gap remains a gap either way.
- **reversed by:** a ground reading, not agreement. An intervention that discriminates between two competing labellings of one system would settle the mapping; an observer assignment shown to be a legitimate anchor that does not move when the observer is pushed would break Fig 2 :29-30 and Fig 10 :49; a demonstration that the convention and physical-support conditions are one question would break Fig 9 :54-58; and a source-discriminating instrument of the kind described in *The Hard Problem is About Source* would begin to name the states. Until one of these fires, the verdict stands as `candidate`.
