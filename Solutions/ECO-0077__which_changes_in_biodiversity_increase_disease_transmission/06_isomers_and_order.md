# Isomers and order: why the same community can give opposite transmission signs

**Mode: `specified` for the ecological reading, `general` for the figure structure.** Simulated
derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The same species list, or even the same direction of species-richness change, is reported as
protective in one study and harmful in another. Two questions are owed:

1. are two communities with identical species lists but different interaction structures **isomers**
   under Fig 12's definition; and
2. what is the **order** of the derivation that determines the sign, given that contact cascades run
   outward and effects reverse with scale?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available here are not the six substrate terms but the enumerated habitat actors of Fig 11
(hosts, vectors, competitors, predators, conditions, relations). Bonds available are the seven of
Fig 12 plus negation. No new primitive is introduced. The anchor is measured infection in a defined
host population. Overlap with `01` is deliberate; this file answers the structure and order question
that the residual leaves open.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Same species list, different interaction structure: isomer?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible
in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
Fig 12 :46-47: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not
interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands. A recipe that names only
its ingredients has recorded half of itself."*

A species richness count is the flat formula: it records ingredients and not arrangement. Two
communities with the same richness can differ in which species is a competent reservoir, which is a
dilution host, which predator suppresses which host, and whether the vector feeds randomly. Those are
differences of bond, not of atoms. Under Fig 12 they are different substances, and nothing about the
ingredient list can tell them apart.

So: **yes, two communities with the same species list can be isomers for transmission.** The same
holds for the same *direction* of richness change: adding a non-competent host and adding a competent
reservoir are the same atom count with different bonds. This is `specified`, not `grounded`: whether
a given pair of real communities is an isomer pair requires measuring the relations.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two communities with the same species list but a proven
difference in interaction structure showed identical transmission for the same pathogen at the same
scale, the isomer reading would fail and richness would be doing the work after all.

### Q2: Dilution and amplification: isomers or a functional group?

Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what
kind; the qualifier says which member."*

Dilution and amplification are not different isomers of a single community. They are net outcomes
of a shared substructure: a community in which host competence varies and contact is structured. The
qualifier (which host is dominant, which vector feeds where, whether transmission is density or
frequency dependent) decides which member of the group you get. That matches Luis et al. 2018 (PNAS,
read 2026-11-23), who find a component amplification effect and a net dilution effect in the same
host-pathogen system: one substructure, two net labels depending on which component is stronger.

So the correct figure category is **functional group**, not isomer, when comparing dilution and
amplification as outcomes; and **isomer** when comparing two communities with the same ingredients
arranged differently. Both readings are used, and the distinction is kept.

**Falsifier:** if dilution and amplification required disjoint sets of component mechanisms with no
shared substructure, the functional-group reading would fail and they would be two unrelated kinds.

### Q3: What is the order of the derivation?

Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one
above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
Fig 11 :52-53: *"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the
eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps
out."*
Fig 9 :27-28: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."*

The dependency order for this question is:

1. **Name the residual** (pathogen, target host, place, scale, transmission mode). Without this there
   is no slot to index and no valence to read.
2. **Enumerate direct contact** (actors, conditions, relations). Fig 11 :43-48.
3. **Follow the cascade outward** until it stops mattering, including second- and third-order
   effects. Fig 11 :52-53. A predator loss can raise a reservoir two links out; a fragmentation can
   change a tick host three links out.
4. **Decompose the changed link** into host density, contact rate, transmissibility and competence.
   This is where sign is read.
5. **Measure the net effect on infection** in the target host. The ground decides. Fig 2 :29.
6. **Only then** state management advice. A lever is defined against an enumerated contact, and
   introduction is available while exclusion is not (Fig 11 :61-62).

An answer that jumps from step 1 to step 6 is Fig 3's failure: effort spent above the break arriving
at a step that cannot use it. Common policy statements that "protecting biodiversity reduces disease
risk" are exactly this jump.

**Falsifier:** if a first-order enumeration (step 2 only) predicted the measured sign as well as the
full cascade enumeration, the order claim would be unnecessary and step 3 could be dropped.

### Q4: Does a measured component compose at the next level?

Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a
fact about a level and never about the world."*

Once a component effect (for example, predator presence lowering reservoir density) is settled
against the ground in a system, it can serve as an atom in a larger composition (a landscape-level
management plan). It is not a new primitive and it does not move the wall (Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes
inside the closure and can never add an atom."*). Where the effect has not been settled in that
system, composing over it is dead reckoning.

**Falsifier:** show that a component effect settled in one habitat cannot serve as an atom in a
composition at a larger scale without being re-measured; then Fig 12 :104's recursion does not apply
here and each scale needs its own ground. This is open, not decided.

## CONCLUSION

The same species list can be an isomer pair for transmission: identical ingredients, different bond,
different behaviour (Fig 12 :117, :46-47). Dilution and amplification are better classified as a
functional group of component mechanisms, with the qualifier deciding the net sign (Fig 12 :124).
The derivation is ordered: residual, direct contact, cascade outward, component decomposition,
measured net effect, then management. A first-order-only reading is the failure mode the DDT example
names. All of this is `specified` structure; no sign is asserted for any real place.
