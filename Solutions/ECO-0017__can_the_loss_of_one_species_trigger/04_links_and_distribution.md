## Wider literature that bears on the question

These are contextual readings, not extra pillars of the verdict. They show the same tension the figure audit names: strong structural prediction and weak empirical documentation sitting side by side.

- **Dunn et al. 2009** review the discrepancy directly: models suggest coextinction is the most common form of species loss, yet few events have been recorded. That is the dependency-chain break from `01` F5, a disagreement about the objective and its instrument rather than a contradiction inside the figures.
- **Kehoe et al. 2021** distinguish coextinction from network-transmitted extinction and identify modularity and functional redundancy as the structural features that keep a disturbance local. This corroborates Fig 12 :123 (functional group) and Fig 11 :44 (enumeration outward) from an ecological evidence pool.
- **Sanders et al. 2018** supply the ground-facing experiment: harvesting one parasitoid caused secondary losses of indirectly linked species, and the effect was weaker in more redundant food webs. This is the closest world source to Fig 11 :52-53's indirect-effect claim.
- **Valdez et al. 2020** and **Haldane and May 2011** come from network physics and finance, not ecology. Their agreement with the shape is low-overlap corroboration. Their numerical thresholds are not ours and are not imported.
- **McDonald-Madden et al. 2016** is the practical check on the "identify the important species" move: no single common index reliably guides management across food webs. It corroborates the residual-relative role claim (Fig 11 :49-51).

No world source conflicts with the figure verdict. Had one conflicted, the rule is to return to the domain census, not to flip the verdict. Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."

## Distribution: offer, do not push

Figure-grounded reasons:

- **Fig 4 :22** — "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down." Sending the entire architecture before anyone has described a residual sends a recording, not a generator. Lead with the enumeration rule and its refuter; offer the links after.
- **Fig 10 :42** — "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it." A reader who has absorbed the framework before being asked for a verdict has joined the frame being checked.
- **Fig 9 :27-28** — "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit." Ask what they would expect to see if the shape claim were true before giving them the article.
- **Fig 2 :40** — "disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool." Never select recipients for likely agreement, and never pool later agreement as corroboration.

**Recommended order in a reply:** (1) the shape claim and its three refuters; (2) ask what they would count as a failure; (3) record that verdict; (4) only then the sources, with the note that the figures, not the summary, are the authority.

## The two-stage offer

1. **Stage 1, before anything else.** Send the residual in effect terms, the shape claim and the pre-registered refuter. Fig 10 :52: "Bring a characterised residual, never the source." The recipient checks the output without holding the framework's interior.
2. **Stage 2, opt-in only.** If they are curious, offer the three source links. Their verdict on stage 1 must already be recorded; later agreement is never pooled as corroboration.
3. **Stage 3, method reuse.** The enumeration rule is a generator, so giving it away does not give away an answer (Fig 4 :22). Each user must state their own ground, write their own refuter before running it, and label the mode of what they get. `THE_FORMULA.md` "Direction" warns that a frame cannot check the direction of its own question.

**Hard precondition:** stages 1 to 3 work only if the stage-1 output survives. `08_expert_verification_packet.md` is that output. If a sub-claim fails the verbatim figure check, repair it before sending.
