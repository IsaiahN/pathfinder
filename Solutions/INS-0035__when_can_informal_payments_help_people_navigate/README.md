## The question restated as an effect

What is being decided is not whether informal payments are "good" or "bad" in themselves. In effect terms: a person faces an institution whose formal channel returns something different from what the person is entitled to (delay, denial, quality shortfall). The residual R is the gap per person and per transaction, not an average (Fig 1 :19, Fig 1 :22). An informal payment is an action in a side channel that carries a different currency (cash, gift, favour) and settles something the formal channel did not. The question is when that side action reduces R by restoring contact with the thing the person is entitled to, and when it increases R for others and for the system by becoming a standing second gate.

- Option A, "payments are grease that helps people navigate dysfunction": predicts the side payment reduces R for the payer at the transaction level and leaves the formal channel and other people unchanged.
- Option B, "payments deepen exclusion and inefficiency": predicts the side payment reallocates access to those who can pay and erodes the formal channel through second-order effects.
- What an answer would look like, written down and bound as an ordinary term before the run (Formula, "Direction"): a **shape** naming the conditions under which a side payment restores contact with the ground without becoming a self-reinforcing gate, plus an instrument (a patient outcome, a counted delay, a measured access rate) that would discriminate the two options. Until it is bound it predicts nothing and no check in the loop can see it.

The verdict recorded in `09_watson_answer.md` is that neither Option A nor Option B holds universally. Valence is relative to the residual (Fig 11 :49-51), so the verdict is given per domain and never averaged.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree.
- No bulk-send plan.
- No claim that this derivation is `grounded`, no claim that it settles the corruption question, and no claim that the framework is the only true account of informal payments.

Three figure-grounded reasons, plus one practical one:

1. **A room is not an anchor.** *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes."* (Fig 7 :35) and *"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."* (Fig 2 :35). A list of people selected to agree that "corruption is bad" manufactures exactly the correlation that disqualifies a verdict.
2. **Correlation has valence.** *"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"* (Fig 2 :40). The grease/sand literature is largely one economics pool and the health informal-payments literature is largely one health pool; their internal agreement is one witness each, not many.
3. **A verifier must be outside the interior.** *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* (Fig 10 :42). The first contact therefore carries the residual and its refuter, not the framework.
4. **Legally**, unsolicited bulk email to scraped addresses breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules in many jurisdictions and gets the sending domain blacklisted. Public opt-in routes avoid that failure as well as the structural one.
