## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the two claims isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As usually written: decoherence is `system + unobserved field` under a tracing-out operation; collapse is `system + stochastic nonlinear term` under a modification of the dynamics. They share almost no atoms. By Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**; they are two members of a broader functional group, "a dynamics that loses coherence", with different qualifiers.
- The stronger version: if both are written over the same reduced-state formalism, `state → coupling → reduced state`, they share the atoms `{state, coupling, reduced state}` and differ in what the coupling does (trace out versus select). Then they **are** Fig 12 isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it makes the difference structural and observable rather than verbal.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if collapse cannot be written as a reduced-state dynamics with a coupling operator, the isomer claim fails and the weaker functional-group reading stands. Corroboration (secondary): Matsui 2026 reports that *"decoherence alone cannot certify that gravity is quantized"*, which is what the isomer distinction predicts.

### Q2 - Does the order matter?

- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Formula step 2 routes REFUTED before mint: *"a term held is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation."*
- Operators table :73: **"Subtraction is the nearest bond and is not it. − is A with B removed or absent, which is the absence of an operand. Negation is the refusal of a claim."**
- The claim "gravity causes collapse" is often reached by taking the decoherence term and reading a collapse into it. That is an addition followed by a misread, not a refusal. The figure order is: (1) refuse the conflation of channel and ground (¬), (2) settle the channel claim against an instrument, (3) only then ask whether a new atom is owed for the ground claim.
- Reversing the order produces exactly the Fig 3 failure: effort spent on a collapse mechanism arrives at a step whose input (a settled channel claim) never arrived.
- **Falsifier:** if a protocol shows that a collapse term can be settled before any channel measurement, the dependency order is wrong and must be re-censused.

## CONCLUSION

The two claims are **not isomers as the source question writes them** (little shared atom set), but they **are a testable isomer claim** when both are written over the same reduced-state atoms with different coupling bonds. That is the structural result worth sending, because it is checkable by an expert and it does not require naming a collapse model. The obligation order is fixed: refuse the conflation, settle the channel, then ask for a new atom for the ground. The substrate rule governs the primitives only and does not price laboratory rates. One strain is named: the D2/D3 boundary, since collapse models are expressed as master equations (a channel vocabulary) while claiming a physical ground change. That is exactly the isomer difference the figure tests, so it is recorded as a strain to be resolved by an instrument, not smoothed over.
