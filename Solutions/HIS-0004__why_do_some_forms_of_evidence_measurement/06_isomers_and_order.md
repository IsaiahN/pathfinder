# Union, not isomer: the structure and order of the four evidence forms

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Why are measurement, testimony, experiment and tradition not interchangeable in context? Are they four species of one kind (isomers under Fig 12), or four different contact relations that happen to share a name in ordinary language? A secondary question: what decides the order in which a context deploys them?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (restated from `01` §1)

Atoms held in the historian's frame: a question Q, a form E, a context C, a ground G, frames F1..Fn, a channel, and an anchor. Bonds held: sequence (→), comparison (⋛), production (⇒), subtraction (−), conjunction (+), identity (≡), disjunction (∥), and negation (¬). No new primitive is needed.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are the four forms isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

An isomer claim requires the same atom set arranged differently. Test it:

- Measurement uses `{instrument, standard, reading, quantity}`.
- Experiment uses `{hypothesis, intervention, prediction, outcome}`.
- Testimony uses `{speaker, hearer, assertion, separate contact}`.
- Tradition uses `{lineage, apprentice, inherited method, correction}`.

These are not the same atoms under different bonds. They share the *relation to ground* but not the operands. So under Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**. They are analogous in the weaker sense of Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." The shared functional group is `form → ground → settlement`, with different operands in each slot.

**Correction recorded from `05` §3:** "shape, not a name" governs a missing primitive, not the ordinary nouns already in the historian's vocabulary. So calling these four "forms" is fine; ranking them is not.

### Q2. Why the union reading governs

Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again." Fig 9 :55-56: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."

A court is a compound: it uses testimony (who saw what) and measurement (what the test returned) simultaneously. A laboratory is also a compound: it uses measurement (the reading) and tradition (the trained technique). Because the four overlap, treating the question as one league table is the "single lever" error: clearing the region where only measurement holds leaves the intersections standing, because testimony still binds there (Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either").

**Falsifier Q2, written before the conclusion:** if the four forms can be ordered on a single scale such that the order predicts authority in every compound context, the union reading fails and a partition reading stands. A counter-instance would be any context where a lower-ranked form decides the outcome while a higher-ranked form is present and applicable.

### Q3. What order do they come in?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." :4-15 give the five links: perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry. Fig 3 :10: the objective link is "it can say what would count as progress here, in its own terms". Fig 3 :25-26: the link between perception and planning "has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."

Ordering the four forms by dependency:

1. **Tradition** supplies vocabulary and a first head start (Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down"). It is upstream.
2. **Perception/instrument** supplies readings, but only if the objective link exists. Fig 13 :16 "it names what is being measured"; :26 "it decides what the measurement would mean".
3. **Experiment** can refuse a held term (Fig 5 :14 "mechanism broken: a new term is owed"), which is the only way to settle the objective link when it is disputed.
4. **Testimony** is the right anchor when the question is constitutively about persons (Fig 2 :37) and must be checked for independence (Fig 2 :31-32).

So the dependency order is not a prestige order. It is the order in which the chain can actually run. A context whose objective link is missing cannot reach step 2 or 3 and will borrow from step 1. That is an effect-level explanation of why tradition appears authoritative in contexts where measurement is available but unused.

**Falsifier Q3:** show a context where experiment settles a question before the objective link exists, with the objective still unspecified at settlement. That breaks Fig 3 :20 and Fig 13 :26.

### Q4. What is the analogue that makes the four comparable?

The analogue is the **anchor** in Fig 2, not the bond in Fig 12. Fig 2 :29: "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks." Read each form as a candidate anchor:

| form | can it fail to update under pressure? | condition |
|---|---|---|
| measurement | yes, if traceable and the channel holds | Fig 2 :29; VIM3 2.41 |
| experiment | yes, the outcome can refuse a held term | Fig 5 :14; Fig 9 :42 |
| testimony | yes, if the witness has separate contact and the question is about persons | Fig 2 :31-32, :37 |
| tradition | no, not of itself; authority borrowed from earlier contact | Fig 4 :11; Fig 10 :47 |

The analogue is structural, not material. Fig 8 :50: "Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence." Two independent witnesses and two independent instruments are the same *structure* at different substrates. That is why WATSON can search low-overlap fields (metrology, animal behaviour, social epistemology) for corroboration of the shape.

### Q5. Are there additional forms the census missed?

Fig 12 :90: "A relation that fails all six is not a bond you are missing. It is a claim that has not been made yet." Applied here: a candidate form that cannot be described as a contact relation to a ground is not a missing form; it is an unstated claim. Fig 8 :39: "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence." The census is open. If a historian names a fifth form (for example, simulation, or a market price), it must be re-censused as actors / currency / anchor before being admitted. A market is already named as an anchor in Fig 2 :29; a simulation would need a ground it cannot range against from inside.

**Falsifier Q5:** name a fifth form with a clear non-updating anchor that is not a composition of the four, and the census is incomplete.

## CONCLUSION

The four forms are not isomers. They are a **union of contact relations** sharing a functional group (`form → ground → settlement`), with different operands and different conditions for anchor legitimacy. They are ordered by dependency, not by rank: tradition feeds vocabulary, instruments feed readings, experiment refuses terms, testimony supplies the right anchor for questions about persons. The analogue that makes them comparable is the anchor, not the bond. One strain remains live and is recorded here rather than smoothed: the Fig 3 dependency order is single-source within the framework, and the world sources (Tal 2020 on standardization, Lamon et al. 2017 on transmission) corroborate only parts of it.
