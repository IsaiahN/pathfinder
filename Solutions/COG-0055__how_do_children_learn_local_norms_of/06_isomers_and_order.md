## DERIVATION

### Q1 Sharing: which bond?

Fehr et al. (W1) found the driver is not simply increasing the partner's payoff but removing inequality: at 7 to 8 children prefer (1,1) in both the prosocial and the envy game, and in the envy game (1,2) would have increased the partner's payoff. So sharing is a **threshold relation between two quantities**, the Operators table's ⋛ comparison: "a goal is a comparison." It is ordered, not symmetric: swapping self and partner share changes the comparison. Mode `specified`; the comparison reading is corroborated by W1 at the ground, but the bond assignment is a binding from the developmental frame.

### Q2 Fairness: same bond, different operands

Fairness in the third-party case compares two other people's allocations. Same bond ⋛, different operand slots. Under Fig 12 :117 "Same atoms, different bond, different substance" this alone would not make sharing and fairness different substances, because the bond is the same. What differs is the **scope of the operands**: self-involved comparison versus third-party comparison. So sharing and fairness are not isomers of each other on the figure's definition; they are the same bond over different operand selections, which the figure treats as different terms because "which state it is bonded to is part of what it is" (Fig 13 gradient-over-space passage, applied as an analogue). Record this as a **strain-for-now**: the figure distinguishes operands by the state they bond to, and self versus third party is a social, not a substrate, distinction. Need an instrument, not an argument.

### Q3 Reciprocity: a cycle, not a new bond

Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input." Reciprocity is A gives to B, B gives to A; the output re-enters as input. The realization is a sequence bond → whose second act needs the first to have happened ("A then B; B needs A to have happened", Operators table). The sign of the loop decides whether it closes. Wilkinson's vampire bats (W6) corroborate at the ground: sharing depends on "an index of opportunity for reciprocation" and on recognition of partners, and food sharing between pairs is "balanced only over extended periods rather than via short-term matching." So reciprocity needs repeated contact and a memory, which is exactly a habitat condition (Fig 11), not a separate mental module. Mode `specified`.

### Q4 Obligation: status designation then sequence

Rakoczy et al. (W2) give the status-function formula: "an X counts as a Y in context C." That is the Operators table's identity ≡ (two names, one referent) *within a declared context*. Once the designation is in place, correct action follows as a sequence → ("A then B; B needs A to have happened"), and progress toward fulfilling it is a subtraction − ("progress is a subtraction", Operators table). Fig 12 :111-113 states the same grammar: a plan is a sequence, a goal is a comparison, progress is a subtraction. Obligation is therefore a compound: ≡ designation in C, then → performance, with − for how far along it is. It is **not** a primitive "duty" atom.

### Q5 Are the four isomers?

Over the full shared atom set {self, partner, resource, quantity/threshold, context, status designation}, the four differ in bond and in which atoms occupy which slots:

| norm | bond structure | closing/scope |
|---|---|---|
| sharing | ⋛ over self-share and partner-share | self-involved |
| fairness | ⋛ over two other-party shares | third party |
| reciprocity | → whose output re-enters (cycle structure) | repeated contact with recognition |
| obligation | ≡ in context C composed with → and − | status designation before performance |

- **Strong form:** all four over the shared atom set, arranged differently. Then by Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. This is the checkable structural claim.
- **Weak form:** as the field usually writes them they do not all share every atom (reciprocity needs repeated contact; obligation needs a status designation), so they are better described by Fig 12 :124 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome." The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member.
- The ingroup/conventional case (W3, W4) is a **compound**, not a harder instance of fairness: Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again." It carries its own loop.

### Q6 What order, and what recurses?

Acquisition order is the dependency chain of Fig 3, not a list of importances: perception of who gave what -> vocabulary for what an act counts as (the status designation) -> objective (what would count as doing it right) -> planning -> learn and carry. Fig 3 :24 "Why the middle link is where chains usually break." W2 puts the objective link in place by about three years. Recursion is Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." Once a first-order norm is settled locally, it becomes an operand for the next order: a child who has settled "this is how we play this game" can treat that settled molecule as an atom and compare it with another group's practice, which is the start of norm relativity. No new primitive is needed.

## CONCLUSION

The four norms are checkably written as differently bonded molecules over one enumerated atom set, so the strong form is a real isomer claim (Fig 12 :117); as normally described they are a functional group with a compound case (Fig 12 :124). Reciprocity is a cycle structure realised by a sequence bond, not a new operator. Obligation is a status designation composed with sequence and subtraction. Acquisition follows Fig 3's dependency order, and settled norms recurse as operands by Fig 12 :104. The one live strain is Q2: whether self-involved and third-party comparisons count as different bonded terms under the figure's operand rule needs an instrument. The bond assignments remain `specified`, provenance recorded, unmeasured.

## FALSIFIER (written before the run)

1. If any one of the four can be written without an atom another requires, the strong isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.
2. If all four collapse to the same bond over the same operand slots, they are one substance, not isomers, and F3's union claim in `01` fails.
3. If the ingroup/conventional compound cannot be separated from the universal moral case by an instrument, the compound claim fails.
4. If a child can run the objective link (say what counts as doing it right) without first acquiring terms for what an act counts as, Fig 3's dependency order is wrong for this domain.
