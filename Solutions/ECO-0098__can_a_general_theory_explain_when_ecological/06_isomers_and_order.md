## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is the union a partition in disguise?

- Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*
- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*

The two conditions are C1 (a maintained channel to a non-updating ground, so recovery is readable per slot) and C2 (renewed reach through contact with a differing closure, so the network has terms for a changed residual). They overlap: a monitoring programme is itself part of the habitat and can change reach, and a new interaction can change what the ground pays for.

Therefore "general theory versus context" is not two answers to one question. It is one union compound with a shared fraction that cannot be assigned from the question alone. That is why the answer in `07` gives conditions rather than a percentage.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a documented system shows C1 and C2 independent with zero overlap, or one intervention clears both with no categorically different residue, the union claim fails and the partition reading stands.

### Q2 - Are two networks with the same species set isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- Fig 12 :96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*

Over a **fixed species set**, two networks that differ only in which interaction bond connects which pair are isomers in the figure's strict sense: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. Their species lists are identical, so an inventory that records only species cannot tell them apart, and the difference is decisive in the recovery behaviour. This matches the empirical observation that the same species pool can assemble into networks with different stability properties.

**The fixed-atom condition is load-bearing and is a live strain.** A real habitat change often adds or removes actors, so the atom set is not fixed across the comparison. When the set changes, the relation is composition plus import or subtraction, not isomerism. This is named rather than hidden; Fig 8 :50-52 warns that convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents and only the record separates them. The record must say whether the species set was held.

**Falsifier:** if two networks with identical species and different bond arrangements show identical slot-level recovery across two different perturbations, the isomer claim fails and the species list is sufficient for this domain.

### Q3 - What if the species sets differ but the motif is the same?

- Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

Two networks with different species sets but the same motif (for example, a keystone predator with a redundant prey guild) are members of a functional group, not isomers. The motif says what kind of arrangement is present; the species identities say which member. This is the weaker relation and is the one that survives habitat substitution, because the member changes while the kind can persist.

The distinction matters for outreach: a claim of the form "this motif confers resilience" is a functional-group claim, and its qualifier must be stated. A claim of the form "these two networks are the same network differently wired" is an isomer claim and requires a fixed species set.

### Q4 - What order governs the cascade?

- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
- Fig 11 :52: *"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."*
- Fig 4 :27: *"The transform between scales loses information, and that is the point."*
- Fig 4 :30: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."*

The dependency order is: perceive R per slot → have terms for what the perturbation affords and what recovery would count as → state the objective → choose a route → name what worked and carry it. A study that measures recovery before saying recovery of what has put effort above the break.

The cascade order is separate and must not be read against it. Fig 11 enumerates outward from the residual until the cascade stops mattering. A one-ring habitat census can miss a third-order effect entirely. Fig 4 adds the cross-scale rule: a result carried from the slot scale to the landscape scale is lossy, and the round-trip gap R_T must be measured rather than assumed zero.

**Falsifier:** if a first-order habitat census predicts the residual as well as a third-order census across two perturbation types, the cascade scope rule is not load-bearing here.

### Q5 - Two currencies, and why one number cannot decide

- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."*
- Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"* and :127 *"and will not notice the shortfall."*

A network description can be cheap in bits (a short adjacency rule) and expensive in actions (a long field campaign to check whether the interaction is actually there). Or it can be expensive in bits and cheap to refute. Fig 12 :129: *"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."* A resilience theory that reports only a complexity score is counting one currency and will not notice that the other budget is exhausted.

This is the structural reason the question's "too strongly on context" complaint is partly a budget statement. If most of the action cost sits in the habitat, a theory of the graph looks general only because it has priced the wrong currency. Fig 9 :60: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."*

**Falsifier:** show a case where description bits and settlement actions are convertible at a fixed rate across two networks, with both measured. Then the two-currency rule fails for this domain.

## CONCLUSION

The two networks are **isomers over a fixed species set**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. They are **functional-group members** when the species sets differ but the motif is preserved. The union of C1 and C2 is unbundled before search, not averaged into a context percentage. The governing order is dependency (Fig 3), with a separate outward cascade order (Fig 11) and a lossy cross-scale transform (Fig 4). Two currencies are held apart (Fig 12).

What survives the derivation is a checkable structural vocabulary, not a resilience score. The live strain is the fixed-atom condition on the isomer claim: real habitat changes move the species set. It is recorded in `09` VERDICT and re-checked in `11_audit.md`.
