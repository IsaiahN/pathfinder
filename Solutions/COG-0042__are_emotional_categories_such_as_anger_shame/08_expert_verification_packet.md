# Expert verification packet: the Tether on cross-cultural emotion categories

**What this is:** a claim in a form a researcher in emotion, language or cross-cultural psychology can check. Mode, anchor, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `specified` on the domain split and per-domain verdicts; `general` on the union structure. Not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a result. It is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

The question "are anger, shame and grief shared or variable?" is not one binary. It is a union compound with three strata. A shared affective capacity (pleasure, displeasure, arousal) is universal. The categories that carve that capacity are locally bonded arrangements, so anger, shame and grief are isomers over shared atoms in the strong form, or members of one functional group in the weaker form. Displays preserve a context mapping across regions while their meaning and frequency vary with local norms. Agreement on a translated label is a convention about shared meaning, not evidence of a shared inner state. Because the question is about what people experience, the anchor is structured first-person report, with behavioural and neural instruments ranged against it rather than substituted for it.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1 Framework-internal, checkable by reading Figure 9 and Figure 1

- **Statement:** the binary must be unbundled into a union, and the residual must be indexed per society and per category slot rather than averaged into one cross-cultural number.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 9 :4-5 *"Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."* Fig 9 :54-55 *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 1 :22 *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*
- **Refuter:** show that one mechanism governs capacity, category and expression at one level, with a residual that trends down as effort rises, or that the between-society residual on every slot is zero after method correction.

### C2 Cross-domain, checkable by an emotion researcher against the cited literature

- **Statement:** shared atoms, different bonds. Categories are locally bonded arrangements over a shared affective capacity, and a translated label is an identity claim that fails its own substitution test where concepts differ.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :117 *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* Fig 12 :124 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Operators table :24-27, identity is *"two names, one referent"* with the test *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."*
- **What to check:** Kirchner et al. 2018 (Cognition and Emotion) report that shame predicted anger for US participants but not for Japanese participants, and label the phenomenon *"a culture-specific phenomenon."* Gendron et al. 2014 (Emotion) report that Himba free-sorts did not reproduce the presumed universal pattern while US sorts did. Jackson et al. 2019 (Science) report both significant cross-language variation and universal structure along valence and activation in 2,474 languages. Do those three findings jointly support a shared-capacity, locally-bonded reading better than either a pure-universal or a pure-relativist reading?
- **Refuter:** show that category differences vanish with valence and arousal held fixed, or that a translated term substitutes without loss in both directions in actual use.

### C3 Anchor and instrument, checkable by a methodologist

- **Statement:** first-person report is the anchor because the question is constitutively about experience; a recognition rate or neural map is an instrument that can be wrong on the axis at issue. Cross-society agreement on a label is a convention, not corroboration.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :36 *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."* Fig 2 :37-38 *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people. It is only an instrument for is this code correct, and instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure."* Fig 2 :41 *"efficient for a convention: agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it."*
- **What to check:** does your own method measure the felt quality of anger or shame, or does it measure a label choice, a facial configuration or a BOLD pattern? If the latter, what bridges it to the report?
- **Refuter:** exhibit a society where first-person report is constitutively the wrong anchor for emotion, or two communities with separate evidence pools whose category boundaries and reports converge without contact. The first breaks the anchor claim, the second upgrades agreement to corroboration and changes C2.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not a universal lexicon of anger, shame and grief. The framework returns a shape, not a name (Fig 9 :32 *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*).
- Not that emotion is entirely cultural. A shared capacity is asserted.
- Not that a face, a word count or a scanner reads off subjective feeling.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement was taken in this run and no row is settled.
- Not a claim about which affective primitives exist. The six-term substrate is used only for its independence consequence, and the mapping of bond typology onto affect carries a declared round trip gap.

## 5. What would settle it

1. A slot test on per-society, per-category first-person reports with method correction.
2. A synthesis test holding valence and arousal fixed while varying situation and audience.
3. An anchor test comparing unconstrained sorting, forced choice and report in one sample.
4. A convention test using communities with no contact and no shared words.
5. A display test holding context fixed across societies.
