# Isomers, order and analogues: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Given a shared affective capacity, are anger, shame and grief the same atoms under different arrangements, or different atoms? And what order must the domains be settled in? Two questions are owed: (1) does Fig 12's isomer relation apply to categories built from valence and arousal, and (2) does the dependency order D1 to D2 to D3 hold, or can a category be settled before the capacity it attaches to?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms in the world frame: valence (an ordering, pleasant to unpleasant) and arousal (a level of activation). Bonds available: relation to a situation, relation to a cause, relation to a self, relation to a social other. No new affective primitive is available without import and a source gate. The figure set supplies the bond typology, not the affective atom list.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 Isomer or functional group?

- Fig 12 :46-47: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."*
- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

If two societies both have valence and arousal, and one bonds them to a blaming situation with an other as cause while the other bonds them to a self-exposing situation with a group as audience, the two categories are **isomers over the shared atom set**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. This is the strong form.

As the evidence is usually reported, however, the two categories are not even stated over the same atom set. One study reports "anger" ratings and another reports "shame" ratings, and the atom inventory differs by instrument. In that weaker form they are a **functional group**: the group says what kind (a negatively valenced, self- or other-related state), the qualifier says which member. The stronger isomer claim is the one worth testing, because it is falsifiable.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if categories are fully specified by their valence and arousal values once the situation is held fixed, so that bond information adds nothing, the isomer claim fails and only the shared-capacity verdict stands. Equally, if two apparent categories have no shared atoms at all, they are not isomers and the functional-group reading also fails.

### Q2 What order do the domains settle in?

- Fig 3 :19-20: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* and *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

The order D1 (capacity) to D2 (category) to D3 (expression) is a dependency order, not a prestige order. A category is a bond over affect, so it cannot be assessed before the affect it binds. An expression is a category made public under a norm, so it cannot be assessed before the category. Most existing cross-cultural work starts at D3 (recognition of faces) and reasons backward to D2 and D1. Fig 3 :29-30 says effort lands above the break because that is where the measurements already exist. Here the break is between D1 and D2: capacity is well instrumented, the bonding rule is not.

- **Falsifier:** exhibit a society where category boundaries are stable and measurable while the underlying affective capacity is unmeasurable or absent. That reverses the dependency and breaks the order.

### Q3 Why a translation is an identity bond, not a discovery

- Operators table :24-27: identity, *"two names, one referent"*, with the test *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."*
- Fig 12 :92 *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."*

A translated emotion word is offered as an identity claim: English *shame* equals Japanese *haji* or *hazukashii*. The identity bond is symmetric, so six kinds of evidence would be one referent. But the literature reports that the referents differ: Boiger and colleagues note that Japanese shame *"commonly revolves around the exposure of inappropriate behaviours"* while US shame cuts to the core of the person (Kirchner et al. 2018, Cognition and Emotion, quoting Boiger et al. 2013 and Lebra 1983). If substituting one term for the other loses information, the identity bond fails its own test. What remains is an overlap, not an identity.

- **Falsifier:** demonstrate a complete substitution test in which no information is lost in either direction between two translated terms in actual use. That would license the identity bond and weaken the variation verdict for that pair.

### Q4 Why negation is needed and Fig 12's bonds are not enough

- Fig 12 :134-140: the seven are bonds and bind two operands; negation is unary and *"A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."*
- Fig 5 :50-56 introduces the refuted bin: *"refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so"*

A universal-lexicon claim is a term held in the shared vocabulary of introductory psychology. Kirchner 2018 does not merely add a new culture to the list; it refuses the standing of "shame necessarily turns into anger" as a universal term. That refusal is a negation, which the bonds cannot express. So the honest record keeps the universal claim and its refusal and lets the reach fall, rather than deleting the claim and pretending it was never held (Fig 6 :41-46).

## CONCLUSION

The two category cores are **isomers over a shared affective atom set in the strong form**, and a **functional group** in the weaker form that the current literature usually reports. The order is capacity, then category, then expression, and it is a dependency order. A translated label is an identity claim that fails its own substitution test where the reported concepts differ, so the record must carry a refusal alongside the universal claim. All of this is `specified`, ungrounded, and carries a round trip gap where Fig 12's bond typology is mapped onto affective phenomena.
