**Mode: `specified` on the board application, `general` on the structural claims.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The improve/ratify question is a union of conditions, not a list of alternatives. Three questions are owed: (1) what is the order of the conditions, (2) are two boards with the same formal atoms different substances under Fig 12's definition, and (3) which analogues are genuine low-overlap witnesses rather than borrowed words.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the order? Dependency, not importance

The four conditions are ordered, not a checklist to be ticked in any sequence.

> Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
> Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

Mapped order:

1. **Perception (D3):** can the board see the ground at all? Without a channel there is no input to grade.
2. **Vocabulary (D2):** does it have terms for what a management report affords, that is, which claims need an outside reading?
3. **Objective (D1a):** can it state what would count as a failure here? This is the comparison ⋛, not a policy statement.
4. **Planning (D1a):** the oversight action, including refusal.
5. **Learn and carry (D4):** refresh the room so independence is renewed by separate experience rather than fixed at origin.

Most board reform effort lands at steps 4 and 5 (more committees, more training), because those are where instruments already exist. A board with a dead channel is unchanged by them.

> Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

### Q2: Are two boards with the same formal atoms different substances?

> Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Two boards can share the same formal ingredients, the same number of independent directors, the same committees and the same charter, and differ in which bond holds. Consider:

- Board A: anchor + channel + differing closure + refusal, where channel is bonded to the ground and refusal is bonded to material recommendations.
- Board B: the same ingredients, where the channel is bonded to management's report and refusal is bonded only to non-material items.

Same atoms, different bonds, different behaviour. Under Fig 12 they are **isomers**, and the ingredient list cannot tell them apart. This is the exact reason a governance score (an ingredient list) fails to predict oversight.

The weaker reading is a **functional group**: both boards share the structure "frame ranges against an anchor", and the qualifier says which member.

> Fig 12 :124 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two boards with identical formal bonds (same channel source, same refusal scope, same anchor) differ systematically in ground-verified oversight with no further atom, the isomer claim fails and the difference must be located in an actor rather than an arrangement, which would send the derivation back to the habitat census.

### Q3: Does refusal survive its own use?

> Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use."

A refusal office is a catalyst if it stays effective across uses. A contrarian director who is appointed once and then socialised into collegial consensus stops being a catalyst and becomes a spent part. The seat map's shelf-life law gives the mechanism: every correction raises correlation between corrector and corrected, so a checking frame calibrated against for too long stops checking.

> Fig 10 :80 "Frames whose independence is renewed by separate experience can be maintained; frames whose correlation is fixed at origin can only be rotated,"

The Cambridge proposal addresses this by preventing shopping for an amenable contrarian and by keeping the role outside the normal director career path. That is a rotation mechanism, not a personality requirement.

### Q4: Analogues: which are low overlap, which are borrowed words

| analogue | field | what it predicts here | overlap with governance pool | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Premortem (Klein 2007) | human cognition and risk assessment | A team asked to imagine the plan has failed generates reasons the plan is wrong; refusal is a procedure, not a disposition | low | corroborates C4 (written refusal before a decision) |
| Groupthink mindguards (Janis, via Simply Psychology 2026) | social psychology | Members screen out disagreement before it reaches the group; a board can ratify without any bad faith | low | corroborates F3 (channel blocked by the frame being checked) |
| Contrarian Director (Sweeney 2015) | corporate governance | A named office writes the case against each material recommendation | high, same field | corroborates C4 but is one witness, not an independent one |
| Rubber stamp as a testable condition | human oversight and systems design | An approval step can be compliant and still change nothing; presence and contribution are different | low, where the source can be opened | corroborates the seat map's "built, plumbed, never called" genus |

The governance sources (Black and Bhagat, Hermalin and Weisbach, Weisbach, Sweeney) largely draw on the same evidence pool of listed-company data. They are one witness for the mechanism shape, not four. The cognition sources are lower overlap and carry the corroboration.

### Q5: Which seat is the board?

This was the live strain in `01` F7. The board appoints the CEO and advises, which puts it inside the mandate loop; it also monitors, which requires it to be outside the frame it grades. Re-census: these are two loops with different anchors. The monitoring loop (D1a) is the subject of this question. The mandate loop (D1b) is not refused, it is simply out of scope. Two different objects, so no figure contradiction (canonical rule: a reading that makes figures contradict one another has misidentified its domain).

## CONCLUSION

The four conditions are a dependency-ordered product. Two boards with the same formal atoms but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12, which is why an ingredient-list score cannot predict oversight. Refusal is catalytic only if it survives use, so rotation is a structural requirement. Two analogues from cognition corroborate the shape at low overlap; the governance literature is one witness. The D1 partition dissolves the manager-versus-checker strain without softening either rule.
