# Union, order, and two currencies: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified` on the business application, `general` on the structural claims.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The company cannot decide whether to commit resources to an innovation because market, customers and business model are all uncertain. Three questions are owed: (1) is the residual one question or several, (2) in what order must the parts be settled, and (3) what does it cost to find out, as distinct from what it costs to describe?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the three conditions (market, customers, business model), the company's Γ, a contact instrument, a pinned anchor, an imported analogue frame. Bonds held: → sequence, ⋛ comparison, − subtraction, ¬ negation. No new primitive is available without import. The habitat is enumerated, not composed (Fig 11 :43).

## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  One question or three? Union, not partition

- Fig 9 :4-5: *"First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."*
- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- A partition would let three independent teams settle the three uncertainties separately and add the results. A union does not: the conditions overlap (customers appear in market and in business model), so a lever that clears the region where only one condition holds leaves the intersections standing.
- ⇒ The residual is a **union compound**. Each condition gets its own loop, its own instrument and its own verdict. Verdicts are never averaged into one score (Fig 1 :19).
- **Falsifier:** if a single lever clears all three conditions at once with no residue, the union claim fails and the partition reading stands. Written before the run.

### Q2  -  What order must the parts be settled in?

- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- Fig 3 :10: *"it can say what would count as progress here, in its own terms"*
- Dependency order: perception (can the company register what the innovation affords) → vocabulary (terms for what customers do) → objective (what would count as progress, the missing comparison term) → planning (a route under incomplete information) → learn and carry (name what worked, verify, carry).
- The chain stops at objective when the business model is uncertain. No amount of pitch polish (perception/vocabulary work) rescues it. Fig 3 :22-23: a reading taken below the break is *"a reading of nothing"*.
- ⇒ The business model is settled **before** the larger commitment, not after. Operationally: bind the comparison term (what would count as a win) before running the contact experiment, not once the data are in.
- **Falsifier:** if an objective can be supplied after the fact without changing which contact experiment is run, the dependency order claim fails.

### Q3  -  Are the three conditions isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- As stated, the three conditions share no atoms in the same slots: market (payment anchor), customers (behavioural instrument), business model (comparison term). They are **not** isomers; they are three members of a functional group, "uncertain condition requiring its own ground", with different atoms.
- The stronger form: if all three are written over a shared atom set (Γ, contact instrument, pinned anchor, comparison term, habitat, refusal criterion), differing only in which bond connects them, then they **are** isomers, and a lever that works on one arrangement may fail on another despite identical ingredients. That is the claim worth testing.
- **Falsifier:** if any condition can be settled without an atom another condition requires, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4  -  Two currencies, and why a plan is not a test

- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."*
- Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."*
- Fig 12 :127: *"It proposes arrangements it cannot afford to test, and the budget is what decides how much of the space is reached at all."*
- Operators table :52: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*
- A business plan is mainly a bits object. A contact experiment is an actions object. Holding only one accounting, the company proposes arrangements it cannot afford to test and does not notice the shortfall. The missing business model is a missing comparison term, not a missing spreadsheet.
- Fig 9 :60: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."* A score is a filter; a payment is a witness.
- **Falsifier:** if description cost predicts action cost on ground-verified cases, the two-currency claim fails and a single accounting suffices.

### Q5  -  What the analogues add, and what they do not

- **Animal behaviour, marginal value theorem (Charnov 1976):** *"The predator should leave the patch it is presently in when the marginal capture rate in the patch drops to the average capture rate for the habitat."* This is a low-overlap witness for the **leave** rule: stop refining a contact line when its residual stops trending down relative to alternatives. It is an analogue for the decision to leave, not a business measurement. It corroborates formula step 2's persistent-and-flat residual test.
- **Business practice, discovery-driven planning (McGrath and MacMillan):** *"The key issue is to know from the start that the plan is wrong."* Corroborates pre-committed refusal and cheap checkpoints, but shares the business evidence pool with the other business sources, so it is one witness.
- **Business practice, MVP (Ries):** *"the minimum viable product is that version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort."* Corroborates the proto-instrument rule at the business layer. It is an instance, not a figure-derived tactic.
- **Human cognition, premortem (Klein):** *"By making it safe for dissenters who are knowledgeable about the undertaking and worried about its weaknesses to speak up, you can improve a project's chances of success."* Corroborates arranging for refusal, with moderate overlap (cognition applied to business).
- **Conflict rule:** no source conflicts with the figure verdict. If one had, it would send the derivation back to the domain census, never flip the verdict.

## CONCLUSION

The three uncertainties are a **union compound**, not a partition and not one hard question. Their dependency order runs through the missing comparison term (the business model), so the objective must be bound before the contact experiment. They are a **functional group** as stated, a real isomer claim over a shared atom set, and they cost in **two incommensurate currencies**. The analogues (foraging leave rule, discovery-driven checkpoints, MVP, premortem) corroborate the mechanism shape at low to moderate overlap but supply no business verdict. What survives is the structure: split, order, refuse, hold the currencies apart. No name of a winning market is derived.
