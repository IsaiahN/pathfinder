# Are labour-law regimes isomers? Bonds, order, and the refusal of a state act

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two legal regimes are often said to have "the same law on the books": a statutory right to a wage floor, a right to organise, a procedure for complaint. Yet their per-slot outcomes differ. Two questions are owed: (1) are the regimes **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, that is same atoms, different bond, different substance; and (2) what is the **dependency order** of the chain by which a rule on the books becomes an outcome on the ground? A third question emerges from the bond set itself: can the seven bonds express a court's refusal of the state's own act?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in each regime: the state, employers, workers, citizens, plus the instruments (courts, inspectorates, unions, contracts, procedures). Bonds held: the seven (`+`, `∥`, `≡`, `→`, `⇒`, `−`, `⋛`) and the unary operator `¬`. No new atom is available without import. Currency A is bits (the description). Currency B is actions (finding out whether it holds). These do not add (Fig 12 :125-130).

## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  Are two regimes with the same statutory text isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- If both regimes are written over the full shared atom set `{state, employers, workers, citizens, court, inspectorate, union}` and differ only in which typed relation connects which pair, they are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different per-slot outcomes. That is the strong claim.
- If one regime lacks an atom the other requires, for example no inspectorate or no recognised union, they are **not** isomers. They are two members of a functional group sharing a shape such as `state → rule → citizen`, with different occupants in the slots. The cautious claim is that they share a functional group.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two regimes can be shown to have identical atoms and identical typed bonds and still produce different per-slot outcomes that survive a ground reading, the isomer claim fails and the arrangement is not the whole of the substance. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 :117, :123); single-source for the institutional application.

### Q2  -  What bond is a right, a duty, a procedure, and a comparison?

- Fig 12 :55-89 defines the seven bonds. A statutory right that two parties hold at once is `+` conjunction: *"both hold at once"*, symmetric (Fig 12 :56-59). A duty imposed on one party toward another is asymmetric and is not `+`.
- A legal rule that creates a legal capacity that did not previously exist is `⇒` production: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Fig 12 :75-79). A complaint procedure that must precede a remedy is `→` sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :70-74).
- The word "distribution" in the question is `⋛` comparison: *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* (Fig 12 :87). It is not a subtraction of one party's gain from another's loss. Treating it as `−` is the flat-formula error: *"A with B removed or absent"* (Fig 12 :81) would make power a conserved quantity, which the figures do not license.
- A repeal is `−` subtraction, the absence of an operand. A court's refusal of the state's own act is **not** `−`; it is `¬` negation, *"this does not hold"* (Operators table :54-58), and negation is unary, not a bond (Operators table :70-78; Fig 12 :134-136: *"The seven are bonds, and a bond is a relation between two things. Negation is not one"*).
- **Open strain, named not hidden:** the bond set can write the creation of a right (`⇒`) and the refusal of a claim (`¬`), but a vocabulary with `¬` still needs a seat able to wield it against an act that comes from the state itself. Fig 10 :42: *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* Whether an institutional checker counts as outside the case at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. This is the same second-order type question carried in `01` R4 and `08` C3.

### Q3  -  What is the dependency order of a rule reaching the ground?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- Order: 1 perception (can the worker read the posted standard, can the inspector read the workplace?) to 2 vocabulary (does Γ contain a term for "informal employment", "dependent contractor", "collective bargaining"?) to 3 objective (can a party say what would count as progress, a goal is a comparison, Fig 12 :112) to 4 planning (organise, draft, litigate, inspect) to 5 learn and carry (precedent, carried method).
- The middle link has no instrument in most labour-law systems. Fig 12 :111-113: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one. A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A statute that names a right without giving the worker a term for the situation's affordances stops at link 2, and effort spent on links 4 and 5 cannot rescue it.
- The labour-law record supplies the measured case: Benin's code is enforced *"poorly and only in the so-called formal sector"*, and Angola's standards are *"poorly enforced"*. Both are failures below the middle link, not failures of the statute's wording.
- **Falsifier:** a regime that improves per-slot outcomes across two independent disputes while its vocabulary link is measurably unchanged, with no new term, instrument or actor introduced. Under Fig 3 :19-27 that should be impossible; if observed, Q3 is false.

### Q4  -  Can the two currencies be combined into one "power" figure?

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- Fig 12 :128-129: the two readings come apart in both directions. A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle.
- Fig 12 :130: **"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."**
- The labour-law page gives the empirical version: a country can have many laws regulating working conditions and still have *"workers' rights ... routinely violated with impunity"*. The count and the violation are two readings. Mechanism design corroborates from a different field by defining institutions as rules that produce outcomes under a predefined metric, which makes the choice of metric visible rather than neutral (low-overlap corroborator, not a figure line).
- **Falsifier:** a single currency in which bits and actions can be validly added and which predicts per-slot outcomes as well as the two readings held apart. If produced, Q4 is false.

### Q5  -  What order is the union of the four regimes?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- Each first-order relation (state to citizen, employer to worker, union to employer) is settled when the ground pays for it, for example by a recorded ruling or a collected wage. Once settled, it becomes an atom at the next level.
- The "distribution of power" is therefore a **second-order** comparison over settled first-order relations, not a property of any single relation. It is legal only because the first-order relations settled. This is the recursion Fig 12 names, not an import.
- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."** The combined question stays a union: the description-only case, the enforcement-only case and the habitat case have different grounds.
- **Falsifier:** a case in which a second-order comparison moves while none of the first-order relations beneath it settled or changed, across a ground reading. Under Fig 12 :104 that should not compose; if observed, Q5 is false.

## CONCLUSION

Two legal regimes with the same four parties and the same typed bonds are isomers in the strong reading. Where one lacks an atom the other requires, the honest weaker reading is a shared functional group. The chain from text to outcome is ordered by dependency, and it breaks at the uninstrumented vocabulary/objective link. The two currencies of rights and enforcement are held apart, and "distribution" is a comparison, not a conserved subtraction. The comparison is second-order over settled first-order relations. The refusal of a state's own act needs the unary operator `¬` and an outside seat, which is the live strain. `metric = Gradient` style reductions are not attempted here: Fig 13's physical substrate does not govern labour law (`01` R2).
