**Subject:** Check one output on legal institutions and power before you decide whether the frame is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know from labour law and power research. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** a legal right and the enforcement of that right are two readings in different units and they do not add. The right is a description, cheap to state. Enforcement is actions, paid for by finding out whether the right actually holds. A jurisdiction can have the description complete and the actions unpaid, and calling that "partial power" is the error. The framework puts it plainly: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."* So the question "how do legal institutions shape the distribution of power between the state, employers, workers and citizens" is not answered by counting rights or statutes. It is answered by tracing, per party and per relation, which instrument carries a rule from the text to an outcome, and what is lost on the trip.
>
> A second output follows from the first: law is a grammar, not a verdict. It makes a dispute statable; it does not settle who is right. *"A convention makes no claim about the world; a verdict and an atom both do."* Where a legal convention is allowed to decide an outcome by declaration, it has stopped being a convention. I am not claiming a rank order of the four parties, and I am not claiming a single scalar can measure "power". The framework returns a shape, not a name.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that a named office, inspectorate, ombudsperson or court is the answer for a given residual. The framework returns a kind of thing, not a name, and I have left that slot open. I am also not claiming that saying a right exists changes anyone's contact with the ground; the claim is the opposite, that a change which does not change contact changes nothing.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. a single scalar per jurisdiction predicting, without loss, the per-relation outcomes of two legal changes that move different relations in opposite directions;
> 2. a legal change that alters realized outcomes while every actor's contact with the ground, actor set, conditions, relations and instruments stay the same;
> 3. a valid common currency in which rights on paper and enforcement in the world can be summed and still predict the ground per slot as well as the two readings held apart;
> 4. a legal frame expanding its reach by internal composition alone, with no contact with a differing closure;
> 5. a line of precedent yielding ground-verified new outcomes across two handoffs with no change of closure or instrument in between;
> 6. a stable ranking of state, employers, workers and citizens that predicts per-slot outcomes across regimes and dispute types.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, the corresponding claim is withdrawn. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Canonical usage rule (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`
> - Six-term derivation and the shape limit: https://adventuresinml.substack.com/p/how-intelligence-actually-works
> - The source-discrimination argument: https://adventuresinml.substack.com/p/the-hard-problem-is-about-source
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a labour-law or power problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. The framework's own rule is that a loop cannot check the direction of its own question, so a reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
