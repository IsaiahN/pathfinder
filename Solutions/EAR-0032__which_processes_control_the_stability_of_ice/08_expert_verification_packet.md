# Expert verification packet: the Tether on ice shelf stability

**What this is:** a claim in a form a glaciologist, ice-ocean modeller or cryosphere observer can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion.

**Mode: `general` on framework-internal shape claims, `specified` on the applied shape, `gap` on the process ranking and all thresholds. Not `grounded`.** No measurement was taken by this derivation. It is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

The question "which processes control ice shelf stability" is a union of overlapping conditions, not one question with one answer. The residual must be indexed per shelf, per tributary glacier, per grounding-line sector and per season; a global stability scalar can look fine while one slot is live. The condition set, ocean heat and basal melt, surface meltwater and hydrofracture, calving and rifting, geometry and pinning, sea-ice loss and wave flexure, must be unbundled before search, because a lever that clears a shelf where only one condition holds leaves the intersections standing. Contact must be enumerated outward, since the controlling term may be second or third order. Measurement is the only anchor. Several processes produce the same observed thinning, so a source-discriminating instrument is owed before any process is named as the control. The framework returns that shape and refuses the name.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1: framework-internal; checkable by reading Figures 1 and 9

- **Statement:** the residual is indexed per slot and the condition set is a union compound, so a single stability number and a single process ranking are notation failures, not findings.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." Fig 1 :22 "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :57 "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either".
- **What to check:** do the figures actually say this, and does the world record fit? Wekerle et al. 2026 report basal-melt dominance at one Greenland tongue and calving dominance at another in the same model, which is the per-slot result.
- **Refuter:** produce one scalar with a stated instrument that predicts every shelf's response under both ocean and surface forcing without hiding a live slot; or show two condition families never co-occur and never change each other's effect.

### C2: observation-facing; checkable against published shelf records

- **Statement:** the anchor is measurement, not model or expert agreement, and contact must be enumerated outward beyond the shelf itself.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :29 "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks." Fig 2 :35 "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." Fig 11 :44 "List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering." Fig 11 :52 "And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."
- **What to check:** is there a published case where second- or third-order contact changed the shelf outcome? The sea-ice and swell pathway into Larsen and Wilkins breakups is the candidate record (NSIDC science page, 2018 study linked there).
- **Refuter:** exhibit a model-only metric that every independent field observation fails to move; or show that sea ice and swell never change the shelf residual once melt and meltwater are held fixed.

### C3: instrument-facing; checkable by someone who designs shelf observations

- **Statement:** observed thinning is a projection with lossy information, so a source-discriminating instrument is owed; until it exists, naming a process is a metamerism error, not a finding.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 6 :32 "An instrument is not built from a description. It is improved from a worse instrument already returning something." Fig 9 :30 "your words are borrowed too, so describe the effect rather than the cause". The supporting essay, opened this session, states: "If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information."
- **What to check:** can one instrument at one shelf in one season return basal melt rate, crevasse propagation and flexural strain together, and thereby apportion a thinning signal? If yes, the `gap` narrows.
- **Refuter:** exhibit such an instrument already apportioning one thinning uniquely among the co-producers. The `gap` then closes and this sub-claim is withdrawn.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not that any named process is the universal control. That is a `gap` from the framework and a per-shelf empirical finding from the ground.
- Not one global stability index or one ranked process list.
- Not that marine ice cliff instability is established. Its support is contested in the world record; that disagreement is for the ground, not for a vote.
- Not that the six-term substrate contains a melt or heat primitive. The substrate bounds composition form only.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement was taken in this derivation.
- Not that agreement among glaciologists, however distinguished, settles any of this. Fig 2 :40 "disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool".

## 5. What would settle it

A ground reading, not agreement. In order of leverage:

1. Coincident basal melt rate, crevasse propagation and flexural strain at one shelf in one season.
2. A per-shelf threshold determination with the instrument declared in advance.
3. A direct observation or refutation of the cliff-failure link at a named grounding line.
4. A partition proof for two condition families at a named shelf.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 1 :19, :22 and Fig 9 :55, :57. Four short passages. Do they say what C1 claims?
2. Check C2 against one shelf you know: was there a third-order contact (sea ice, swell, pinning) that changed the outcome? Name the observation.
3. Name one existing instrument that could return all three measurements in C3 at one site, or say why none could.
4. Try the falsifier: can you write one stability scalar that survives Wekerle et al.'s two glaciers?
5. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
