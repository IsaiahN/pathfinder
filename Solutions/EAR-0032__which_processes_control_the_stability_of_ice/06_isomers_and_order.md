# Isomers and order: two response modes over one shared atom set

**Mode: `specified` on the applied derivation, `general` on the figure rules.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The earth-science frame distinguishes two broad ways a shelf loses its restraint:

- **Mode G (gradual):** warm water melts the underside, the shelf thins, back stress falls, the grounding line retreats, glacier flow accelerates over years to decades.
- **Mode R (rapid):** surface meltwater fills crevasses and wedges them through (hydrofracture), or swell flexes an already fractured shelf, and large areas disintegrate in days to weeks.

Both end in reduced restraint and faster glacier flow. The question for this supporting derivation is structural: are these two different substances, or two spellings of one, and what is the shared substructure that actually does the work on the glacier?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are Mode G and Mode R isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Written over the **full shared atom set** the two modes qualify as isomers in the figure's sense:

```
Mode G: ocean heat → (melting over time) → thinning → (less) back reaction → grounding-line retreat → velocity
Mode R: meltwater AND wave energy → (wedging / flexure) → fracture → loss of coherence → back reaction gone → velocity
```

- Same atoms: ice, water in one phase or another, stress, back reaction, velocity.
- Different bond: G is an ordered sequence (`→`, "A then B"), R is a conjunction of co-present drivers (`+`, "both hold at once") followed by a threshold crossing.
- Different substance: years of gradual thinning versus daisy-chain disintegration in weeks.

Fig 12 :94 "The arrangement is the substance." The two modes are not one process with a different rate; they are different molecules. This is why a single scalar "stability index" loses the thing that matters.

**Isomer claim, stated narrowly:** over the shared atom set the two response modes are isomers under Fig 12's definition. Status: `specified`, single-source for the figure definition, with the physics-facing check still unmeasured. It is **not** a claim that the framework derived the atoms; they are imports (see `01` F6).

### Q2: What is the shared functional group?

Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use."

The substructure both modes share is:

```
back reaction on the glacier  ->  reduction of back reaction  ->  acceleration
```

The *kind* is "loss of restraint." The *qualifier* is which route removed it: basal melt, hydrofracture, wave flexure, calving, unpinning. This is the load-bearing insight for the question as asked. The processes people argue about are qualifiers; the functional group is what actually changes glacier speed. The world sources make the same point in plain language: when the shelf collapses, "the brakes on glacier flow are released" (NSIDC, "Why Ice Shelves Matter").

Pinning points and ice rises are the nearest analogue to Fig 12's catalyst: a part that survives its own use and continues to supply back reaction until it is lost. Unlike a chemical catalyst, a pinning point can be removed, so the analogy is structural only and is labelled as such.

### Q3: What order is the union?

The modes are not simply alternatives; G and R compose:

- G prepares R: basal thinning reduces the shelf's ability to tolerate fracture, so hydrofracture succeeds where it would previously have arrested.
- R completes G: once disintegration removes the shelf, the grounding-line response of G runs much faster.
- The sea-ice term is a third-order modifier: it changes the wave energy available to R without changing ocean heat directly (Fig 11 :52 "And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out.").

So the union is ordered G → R in many cases, but not universally. Where a cold shelf with no surface melt loses its pinning purely mechanically, R can precede G. An ordering claim that holds in one region and not another is exactly the kind of disagreement Fig 9 :4 says to split rather than search: "Split it rather than search."

### Q4: Does a cycle close the loop?

The basal-melt chain is potentially self-reinforcing: melt thins the shelf, thinning moves the grounding line, retreat changes cavity geometry, the new geometry admits more warm water, and melt increases. The world sources describe this as a self-reinforcing mechanism for marine ice sheet instability. In figure terms the output re-enters as input, so the sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction. The sign is a ground question, not a figure question: it is measured by whether observed melt rates rise as the grounding line retreats at a named shelf. At this budget it remains `specified`, instrument owed.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if at a named shelf the grounding line retreats while basal melt rates stay flat or fall, the positive loop does not close there and the cycle claim is withdrawn for that shelf. A second falsifier: if a shelf loses back reaction with neither meltwater nor wave energy nor basal melt present (pure unpinning alone), the functional-group claim stands but the isomer claim fails, because one mode then uses atoms the other does not.

### Q5: Is a settled shelf an atom at the next level?

Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." Once a shelf's response is settled against observation, it can serve as an operand for the glacier-scale and ice-sheet-scale composition. This is how the local shelf residual becomes a sea-level residual. The transform is lossy (Fig 4 :30 "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."), so the round-trip gap between a shelf-scale explanation and an ice-sheet-scale projection must be measured, not assumed zero.

## CONCLUSION

The two response modes are **isomers over a shared atom set** under Fig 12's definition: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour. What they share is a functional group, loss of back reaction, and that group, not the qualifier, is what changes glacier speed. The union is ordered in some regions and not others, so the order claim must be indexed per shelf. A self-reinforcing basal-melt cycle is a real structural possibility whose sign is still a ground question. No new primitive was minted, and every concrete atom came from an imported, provenanced world source.
