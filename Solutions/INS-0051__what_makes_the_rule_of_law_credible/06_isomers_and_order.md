# Union, order, and source discrimination: supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The headline question asks for one thing ("what makes the rule of law credible") but the audit found four conditions. Two further questions are owed: (1) is this a union or a partition, and what follows for the search; (2) what is the dependency order among the conditions; and (3) what analogue identifies the error of reading a source off a verdict?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: a ruling, evidence, an interest, a selection pool, a record, a ground, a frame, a seat. Relations held: a ruling ranges against evidence; a seat is corrected by a ground it cannot move; a pool is authored or unauthored; a settled ruling becomes an atom for a later ruling. No new primitive is available without import. D3 (the political frame) has no anchor and is treated as a frame acting on the others, not as a domain with its own ground.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
- The four conditions (non-updating anchor, blind checker, declared interests, unauthored pool) plus the measurement condition are jointly necessary. A system with an unauthored pool but a moving anchor still fails. A system with a non-updating anchor but a checker handed the wanted result still fails. That is a union, not a list.
- **Consequence:** no single lever is an answer. A lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing (Fig 9 :55-58). The residue after one lever is "smaller and also categorically different", a specific compound, not a harder version of the same problem.
- **Falsifier:** if one condition can be shown to entail the other four, the union reading collapses to a partition and the unbundling instruction was unnecessary.

### Q2: What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
- Ordered chain: **anchor (D1) -> blind seat (D2) -> unauthored pool (D2) -> per-case record (D4)**. If the anchor moves, nothing downstream matters. If the checker can reconstruct the wanted verdict, independence is spent before the pool is even chosen. If there is no per-case instrument, credibility cannot be observed and the loop stops at the middle link.
- Fig 3's own warning applies: "Where a given chain stops is a reading, never a property of the chain." So this order is a claim about *this* residual, not a universal ranking of legal reforms.
- **Falsifier:** a system whose pool is unauthored and whose checker is blind, but whose anchor demonstrably moves with pressure, yet which is still credited as credible. That would break the order.

### Q3: The source-discrimination analogue

- *The Hard Problem is About Source*: "If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information." The colour instrument reads green and "cannot comment on the source". It was a spectrometer, not an argument, that settled it.
- Fig 8 :50: "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them".
- Applied here: a ruling is a reading. It can be produced by law (the ground) or by political pressure (a different source). The verdict text alone does not discriminate. What discriminates is a source-sensitive instrument: per-case reasons, the record of which evidence was admitted, the record of who selected the decision-maker, and an independent frame ranging against the same evidence.
- Fig 10 :62: "A ruling carries provenance like an atom." Written reasons are part of the term, not documentation attached to it.
- Fig 10 :65: "Provenance shows a ruling was reached legitimately. It never shows it was right: the ground does that, and a wrong ruling stays wrong however clean its record." So provenance discriminates the *source*, not the *correctness*. Both are needed.
- **Falsifier:** an instrument that reads the verdict alone and correctly identifies whether law or pressure produced it, with no access to reasons, selection records, or independent comparisons. That would refute the metamerism analogue.

### Q4: How credibility recurses

- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."
- Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."
- A ruling settled against the ground becomes an atom for later rulings. Precedent is therefore the recursion, not a pile of recordings. A line of rulings that are each settled and each composable is a reach that grows; a line of verdicts copied without the reasons is a library that grows while the reach does not (Fig 5 :72: "a library that grows is not a reach that grows").
- The practical consequence: the unit of credibility transmission is the *reasoned method*, not the outcome. Fig 4 :25: "A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion."
- **Falsifier:** show a legal system whose settled rulings cannot be composed over, or whose unreasoned outcomes function as reusable methods.

## CONCLUSION

The question is a union of conditions, not a single lever (Q1). The conditions have a dependency order that begins at the anchor and ends at the instrument (Q2). The central confusion, reading a source off a verdict, is a metamerism error that requires a source-discriminating instrument rather than a better argument (Q3). Credibility recurses through reasoned rulings, not through copies of outcomes (Q4). The four claims together are the supporting derivation for the shape in `07_the_tether_answer.md`.
