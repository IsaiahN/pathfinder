## Independent domain census

I redid the census before reading the primary's conclusions, using the WATSON order: R by arity, symmetry and scale; one line per domain with actors / currency / anchor; partition or union; order by dependency.

- **R (arity / symmetry / scale).** The gap between the law's claim to settle by evidence and what happens when enforcers are pressured. Arity: two or more political frames, one legal anchor, one checking seat, so a multi-frame arrangement. Symmetry: the anchor is pinned and asymmetric with the frames; correlation has valence, so agreement can be disqualifying (Fig 2 :40). Scale: one ruling, one court, one polity over time. Fig 9 supplies the union/partition split.
- **Domain 1, case-level adjudication.** Actors: judge or jury, parties, evidence. Currency: the ruling. Anchor: facts and law, which neither side can move. Question type: triangulation.
- **Domain 2, institutional checking arrangement.** Actors: courts, appointing bodies, executive, legislature. Currency: tenure, budget, assignment, reasons. Anchor: the same ground, kept reachable by a seat that declares its stake. Question type: seat design.
- **Domain 3, political pressure frame.** Actors: officeholders, factions, opinion. Currency: influence. Anchor: none. It is a frame, not a domain with a ground, so no verdict.
- **Domain 4, legitimacy and measurement.** Actors: citizens, litigants, observers. Currency: trust. Anchor: observed per-case behaviour of courts, not belief. Question type: instrument design.

Union, not partition: four conditions must hold together, and no single lever clears the intersections. Order by dependency: anchor, then blind seat, then unauthored pool, then per-case instrument.

My census matches the primary's three verdict-bearing domains and its union reading, but I reached them from different starting figures. The primary derived D1 from Fig 2 and D2 from Fig 10; I derived D1 from the seat map's anchor definition and D2 from Fig 9's selection-between-rooms rule plus Fig 7 :35. Different routes inside the same framework, so the agreement is weak but not circular in derivation. It is still one evidence pool, so it carries no independent weight.

## Round trip gap R_T

The Symbols table defines R_T as the gap between what was sent up and what came back down, `gap(x, (T_E ∘ T_A)(x))`, measuring what the coarser description cannot hold. Fig 4 :30 requires it to be measured rather than assumed. I ran the round trip on the condensation the folder actually uses.

1. **Sent up (x):** the verbatim figure set, chiefly Fig 2 :29, :33, :40, :42, Fig 10 :42, :59, :73, :76, :93, Fig 1 :19 and Fig 9 :54.
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** the shorthand in `01` section 0 and `07` section 3: non-updating anchor, blind checker, declared interests, unauthored pool, per-case measurement.
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** a concrete institutional claim, for example "randomly selected, well-trained judges with disclosed interests and protected tenure produce credible rulings".

**R_T is nonzero.** Three things the shorthand holds do not survive the trip back down:

- the **valence of agreement**: the shorthand says "measurement", but a concrete implementation can quietly substitute an approval index, which Fig 2 :40 disqualifies;
- the **provenance-not-correctness distinction** (Fig 10 :65): a concrete scheme can treat clean procedure as a substitute for checking the facts;
- the **per-slot rule** (Fig 1 :19): a concrete implementation can replace per-case records with a national average and still appear to be measuring.

Conversely, one thing survives cleanly: a non-updating anchor is required, and the checker must be blind to the wanted result. That is the residue worth sending. R_T was **not** measured numerically, because no ground reading exists; it is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints. Treating the four-condition shorthand as identical to the figure set would set R_T to zero and would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.
