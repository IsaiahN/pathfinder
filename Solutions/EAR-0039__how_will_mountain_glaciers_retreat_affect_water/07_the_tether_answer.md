# The Tether's answer on glacier retreat and water supply

**Mode: `specified` - not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a forecast for any named basin. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

Water systems plan against a historical hydrograph. Glacier mass loss changes the magnitude, the seasonal timing and the interannual variability of the water a basin actually delivers. The gap between the planned yield and the delivered water grows in glacier-dependent slots while remaining small elsewhere. R = |Γ(b,a) - o′| is indexed per basin and per season, never aggregated (Fig 1 :19, Fig 1 :22). The question is a **union compound**, not one hard problem: a basin before peak water and a basin after it are not different degrees of one condition (Fig 9 :57). It has its own loop and its own ground.

## 2. The shape

1. **There is no single global water outcome.** The residual is per basin (Fig 1 :19). A claim that glacier retreat means less water everywhere is already contradicted by the public record: roughly half of the large glacierized basins studied have not yet reached peak water (Huss and Hock 2018). Mode `general` for the indexing rule, `specified` for its application here.
2. **The response is a sequence, not a trend.** Accumulation → increased melt → peak water → decline (Fig 12 :72, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*). Where a basin sits in that sequence determines the sign of the magnitude effect. The framework does not name the year.
3. **The question is a three-condition union.** C1 magnitude and phase, C2 seasonal timing, C3 variability and hazard. They are isomers over a shared atom set (Fig 12 :117, *"same atoms, different bond, different substance"*), with different instruments. Two basins with identical ice mass and identical annual runoff can have opposite agricultural outcomes because timing and threshold are arranged differently.
4. **Adequacy is a threshold, not a stock.** Supply meets demand as a comparison bond (Fig 12 :87, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*). The same annual total can fail or pass depending on when it arrives relative to the growing season. UNESCO WWDR 2025 reports the earlier spring melt peak and the resulting difficulty in timing irrigation intake.
5. **The same meltwater changes sign.** It buffers drought where the dry season coincides with the growing season, yet it contributes to flood and GLOF hazard elsewhere. Valence is relative to the residual (Fig 11 :49, *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries"*). Nothing about the glacier changed to flip that sign.
6. **Old plans fail from a closed frame and a degraded channel, not from a changing ground.** A stationarity-based planning closure cannot derive glacier change from inside (Fig 6 :23, *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs"*; Milly et al. 2008, *"stationarity is dead"*). And the ground does not decay while the channel does (Fig 10 :24), so stale gauges and pooled readings matter as much as the ice loss.
7. **The downstream consequence is several steps out.** glacier mass loss → streamflow timing → reservoir rules → irrigation and crop choice → income, food price, migration, conflict (Fig 11 :52). It is enumerated, not assumed. The IPCC AR6 reports about 2 billion people dependent on mountain water and two thirds of irrigated agriculture dependent on mountain runoff.
8. **The framework returns a shape, not a name.** It declines to say which basin peaks when and which crop fails where (Fig 9 :32). The peak-water concept is itself a provenanced import from glaciology, constrained by the UNESCO Box 2.2 caution: the idealized trend weakens with basin size and downstream reliance is often mischaracterised.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not a forecast for any named basin, region, year or crop.
- Not "less water everywhere". Roughly half the studied basins are still before peak water, and for about two thirds of the world's large rivers the ice is too small to affect continental runoff materially.
- Not that peak water is a policy date. The UN water report explicitly warns against that application.
- Not that glacier retreat is the sole cause of low summer flow. Rainfall, snowmelt, groundwater and evaporation also contribute; the reading does not determine the source (*The Hard Problem is About Source*).
- Not that more storage is the general fix. A single lever clears the region where only one of the three conditions holds and leaves the intersections standing (Fig 9 :55).
- Not grounded. No per-basin ground reading here settles `left(R,φ)`.

## 4. What would settle it - the ground

A maintained per-basin record, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: an instrument that reads a result *"cannot comment on the source"*; the spectrometer resolved the analogous case. Here:

- **Anchor:** measured glacier mass balance and gauged streamflow, archived by public services (WGMS, GTN-G, national gauges), read per basin and per season.
- **Sequence test:** does the basin's annual runoff show the accumulation → peak → decline order, and where is it in that order now?
- **Timing test:** has the seasonal centre of melt shifted earlier, and does the supply-demand threshold now fail in the growing season at constant annual total?
- **Source-discrimination test:** after a low-flow reading, can the contributions of glacier melt, snowmelt, rain, groundwater and diversion be separated? If not, the instrument is inadequate, not the question answered.
- **Standing falsifiers from `01`:** supply unchanged after the glacier contribution is removed; a stationarity model predicting peak water with no imported atom; mass balance and streamflow stable while supply changes; a single lever restoring all conditions; monotone decline with no intervening increase.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** The figure-derived shape is checkable now. The basin-level numbers are not held here and require the public measurement record. *"what a missing thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)"* governs the stop. Indiscernibility is not identity: a basin that has not been measured is not a basin without an answer. The lineage is recorded so the stop is checkable.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not give a single answer to "how will glacier retreat affect water supply", because the figures forbid aggregating the residual across basins. What it gives is a shape: the response is a three-condition union, indexed per basin. First, magnitude and phase, because glacier runoff follows a sequence of accumulation, peak water and decline, and basins sit at different points in it; roughly half have not yet peaked. Second, seasonal timing, because the same annual total helps or hurts irrigation depending on when it arrives. Third, variability and hazard, because the same meltwater that buffers drought can also drive floods. Adequacy is a threshold against demand, not a stock, and the old planning frame fails because stationarity cannot derive glacier change from inside its own closure while the monitoring channel quietly degrades. The framework names no basin, no peak year and no crop. It offers the shape, the instruments that would close it, and refuters written in advance. The center stays open until the per-basin ground settles it.
