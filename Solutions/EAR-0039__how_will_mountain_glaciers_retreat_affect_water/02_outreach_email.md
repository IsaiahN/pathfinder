**Subject:** Check one shape claim about glacier-fed water supply before you decide whether the method is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know about glacier-fed water systems. I am not asking you to read the method first, and I am not asking you to agree with me. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the framework's rule is that the residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated. Applied here, the slot is the basin, not "global water supply". From that and three other figure lines it follows that glacier retreat does not deliver one water outcome. It delivers a union of three basin-specific conditions:
> 1. **magnitude and phase**, where a basin sits in the sequence accumulation, peak water, decline;
> 2. **seasonal timing**, where the same annual total can help or hurt irrigation depending on when it arrives;
> 3. **variability and hazard**, where a glacier that buffers drought in one frame becomes a flood or GLOF source in another.
>
> The claim is about the shape these conditions take and the instruments that would tell them apart. It is not a claim that any particular basin has or has not passed peak water, and it is explicitly not a claim that glacier retreat means less water everywhere. The public record already contradicts that: roughly half of the large glacierized basins studied are still before peak water.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that this method settles the question, that it names which basin peaks when, that it predicts a crop failure in a named district, or that the "peak water" concept can be applied as a policy date. The framework returns a shape, not a name. A published UN water report warns that the idealized peak water trend weakens with basin size and that reliance on glaciers is often mischaracterised. I take that warning as a constraint, not as noise. Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents and only the record separates them, so any binding of a figure term to a hydrological quantity is provenance owed, not evidence.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. a glacier-fed basin whose supply reliability is unchanged after the glacier contribution is demonstrably removed (breaks the need for an imported atom);
> 2. a planning model that predicts the timing and magnitude of peak water for a named basin from its own historical streamflow alone, with no glacier-physics input, and passes the ground test prospectively;
> 3. measured mass balance and streamflow both stable while delivered supply changes for reasons internal to the model's own terms;
> 4. a single intervention that restores magnitude, timing and variability conditions in all failure types with no categorically different residue left over.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, the shape is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the public sources are below. The figures, not my summary of them, are the authority.
> - Global-scale hydrological response to future glacier mass loss, Huss and Hock, Nature Climate Change 2018: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-017-0049-x
> - Cross-Chapter Paper 5, Mountains, IPCC AR6 WGII 2022: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/ccp5
> - Cryospheric change and its impact on water resources, UN World Water Development Report 2025: https://www.unesco.org/reports/wwdr/en/2025/cryospheric-change
>
> **If you want to use the method:** the formula is public in the figures repository. Run it on a basin you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> - [name]
