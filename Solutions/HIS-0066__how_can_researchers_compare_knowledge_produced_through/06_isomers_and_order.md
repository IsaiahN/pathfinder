# Are experimental and field knowledge isomers? Structure, order, and the union

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The comparison claim in `07_the_tether_answer.md` says the two bodies of knowledge are held apart and then compared, and that the comparison is second-order. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. Are experimental knowledge and observation/practice knowledge **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or only members of a shared functional group?
2. What is the **order** of the union, and what licenses composing it?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available for this derivation: a phenomenon P, a claim C about P, the outcome O that both frames try to predict, and the record R of what happened. Bonds available: the seven (three symmetric `+ ∥ ≡`, four ordered `→ ⇒ − ⋛`) plus negation `¬`. No new primitive is introduced. The question is how the two frames bond the same atoms, not what new atoms they contain.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What counts as the shared atom set?

Fig 12 :117 defines the isomer: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* Fig 13 :121 adds *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*

The tempting answer is that the atoms are the methods (randomisation, control, field site, long series). That makes the two frames share nothing and the isomer question trivial. The better answer: the atoms are the **phenomenon and the claim about it**. Both frames are trying to say something about the same P and to predict the same O. That is what makes comparison possible at all; without a shared atom there is no union, only two unrelated libraries.

Fig 8 :45 says *"Two frames together reach what neither reaches alone."* That surplus requires a shared object with differing closures, which is exactly the isomer setup.

### Q2: Which bonds does each frame hold?

Writing the two bodies in the bond vocabulary:

- **Experimental frame (E):** `randomise → treat ⇒ outcome`, with the control as `−` (the second operand's absence is the point, per the Operators table test *"is the second operand's absence the point? Yes."*), and the treatment effect as `⋛` (a threshold relation between treatment and control quantities). E is strong on `−`, `⇒` and `⋛`.
- **Observation / practice frame (O):** `season → season → outcome` (temporal succession), `condition + condition` (co-occurrence in the unmanipulated field), and a skilled functional group: noticing which changes matter. O is strong on `→`, `+`, and the functional group (Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*).

Fig 12 :111 says *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* The two bodies name the same state (P, O) but different routes to it. That is the bond difference.

### Q3: Are they isomers?

**Strong form: yes.** If both are written over the full shared atom set `{P, C, O, R}` and differ only in which bond mediates which pair, then they satisfy Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different substance. Their predictions can diverge even though their ingredients are the same, which is the decisive behavioural difference the figure names.

**Weak form: functional group.** As normally reported, E and O use different outcome measures and different records, so they do not share the full atom set in practice. They share only the phenomenon P. They are then two members of the same functional group, *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member"* (Fig 12 :124), not isomers.

The honest verdict is therefore: **isomers under a stated normalisation, functional group as actually practised**. The normalisation must be declared, not assumed, or the claim smuggles in an import. Fig 8 :50 says *"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them"*; the record here must say which atom set was used.

### Q4: What is the order of the union?

Each body is settled against its own ground before comparison: experiments against re-runs and instruments (SEP *Experiment in Physics*, 2026-11-23), practice against long unmanipulated records. Fig 12 :104 says *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"*. So each first-order body becomes an atom at the second level, and the comparison composes over those atoms. No new primitive is required.

Fig 12 :133 says the union surplus can be *"a bond the other frame holds and you do not."* E can write subtraction; O cannot. O can write long temporal succession; E's substrate is a substituted habitat in which long succession was removed. Each can import the other's bond only with a source gate and an independence debit (Fig 6 :23-24). That is the concrete content of the union surplus for this question.

**Strain, still live.** The Operators table defines `⋛` as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*. At second order the operands are settled bodies now serving as atoms. Whether an atom counts as a quantity at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. Recorded as a strain, not resolved by a softer reading.

## CONCLUSION

The two bodies are first-order structures over a shared phenomenon. Over a declared common atom set they are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117); as normally practised they are a **functional group** (Fig 12 :124). Their union is **second-order** and is licensed by Fig 12 :104, not by any new kind of evidence. The surplus is a bond the other frame holds, which is why a comparison that records only the conclusion and not the bond has recorded half of itself.

## FALSIFIER, written before the run

1. If either frame requires an atom the other does not (for example a counterfactual control with no counterpart in the observational record), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands alone.
2. If a single bond set writes both bodies with no loss of predictive content, the union surplus is zero and Fig 8 :45 does not apply to this pair.
3. If a settled first-order body cannot serve as an atom for a second-order comparison, the order claim fails and the comparison is a new first-order act, not a composition.
