The question asks whether consistency and discretion can coexist. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. Are "rule" and "discretion" isomers, that is, the same atoms arranged differently, or are they different substances?
2. In what order must the repair be made, and what falsifier would show the order is wrong?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are rules and discretion isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

- Written as ingredients, a rule and an exception can look like the same set: a condition, an official, a case, an outcome. If that were all, they would be spelling variants of one term.
- They are not. The rule uses a sequence or a comparison (→, ⋛): "if condition, then outcome". The exception uses negation (¬): "this rule does not hold here". Negation is not a bond; it does not join two operands (Operators :70-72). So the difference is not a different bond between the same atoms. It is a different operator arity in the vocabulary.
- Therefore they are **not isomers** under Fig 12's definition. Isomerism requires the same atoms and a different *bond*. Here one side has an operator the other side lacks. This is a vocabulary difference, which is a stronger and more structural claim.

World corroboration: SEP *Non-monotonic Logic* distinguishes monotonic consequence from defeasible consequence that "retract[s] ... upon the acquisition of further information". That is a difference in the consequence relation, not a different arrangement of the same facts, which matches the figure's verdict that this is a vocabulary difference. It also notes the legal pair *lex superior* and *lex posterior*, which are conflict-resolution rules, that is, structure, not new atoms.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if an exception can be written using only the seven bonds, with no unary operator, and still be inherited by a later case, then rules and exceptions are isomers and Q1 fails. The figure's own operator test (Operators :68: "Seven tests separate the eight named here") decides this.

### Q2 - Rule versus standard is a bond difference, not a negation difference

SEP *The Rule of Law* distinguishes "a rule is like a numerical speed limit, whereas a standard is like a norm that requires people to drive at a 'reasonable' speed" and says standards "allow for less certainty in the law". This is a world distinction, not a figure line.

Re-census against the figures: a standard is a comparison bond (⋛) with a graded threshold; a rule is a sequence (→). Both are among the seven bonds. The unusual case still needs ¬ on top of either one, because neither → nor ⋛ can say that a held norm is false in this case. So a standard does not solve discretion; it moves the threshold question one level. The world source corroborates the difficulty ("difficult ... to predict how their judgement will be viewed by an official or by a court"), and the figure explains why: a threshold without a refusal operator still cannot carry the correction.

**Falsifier:** a standard shown to carry an inheritable refusal of itself without any negation or retraction operator kills this reading.

### Q3 - What order is the repair?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

Dependency order from `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 1:

1. channel to the case facts reachable (Fig 10 :29);
2. rule kept as a convention that does not decide the outcome (Fig 2 :43);
3. negation available so the rule can refuse itself (Operators :57);
4. ruling record with provenance (Fig 10 :62);
5. unauthored and rotated reviewer (Fig 10 :73, :81);
6. residuals read per case (Fig 1 :19).

Link 3 is the usual break. Links 1 and 6 have obvious instruments (a case file, a per-case count). Link 3 has neither, because a vocabulary that lacks ¬ cannot report its own lack: Fig 12 :140: "A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate." A rule set can have perfect compliance statistics and no way to write "not here". This is Fig 3 :24-27 applied to institutions: the middle link attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments.

**Falsifier:** an institution shown to write an inheritable, checked refusal while lacking any negation or retraction operation in its rule vocabulary breaks the dependency order and Q3 fails.

### Q4 - What is the round trip gap R_T here?

Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."

- **Sent up (x):** the four-part shape in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 0.
- **Abstraction (T_A):** the shorthand "be consistent but allow discretion", which is how the question is usually asked.
- **Concretisation (T_E):** a concrete institutional design: a non-updating rule, a written refusal, a dated record, an unauthored reviewer.

The shorthand holds things the concrete design does not. "Be consistent" is read as uniformity; "allow discretion" is read as an unwritten favour. Both readings are refused by the figures (F1, F3). R_T is nonzero and qualitative here: it is the set of dropped constraints (anchor, negation, provenance, unauthored pool, per-case residual). `11_audit.md` records it in detail. Treating the shorthand as identical to the shape would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

## CONCLUSION

Rules and discretion are **not isomers**: one side has a unary operator the other lacks, so this is a vocabulary difference rather than an arrangement difference. Rule versus standard is a bond-level difference (sequence versus comparison) and does not remove the need for ¬. The repair order runs from channel to anchor to negation to provenance to unauthored reviewer to per-case residual, with negation the likely silent break. R_T from the everyday phrasing to the four-part shape is nonzero and must be recorded rather than assumed away.

What survives is a checkable shape, not a named institution. Its falsifiers are written above, before the run.
