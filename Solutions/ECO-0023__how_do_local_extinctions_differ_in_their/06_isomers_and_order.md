# Union, subtraction and negation: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6), not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The audit left one structural question open. Local and global extinction have been called a union compound (Fig 9 :54) and a categorical break in source availability (Fig 6 :25-26, Fig 8 :32). What is the *operator-level* difference between the two removals, and what order do the loops run in?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: a habitat as a set of actors, conditions and relations (Fig 11 :45-47); a species actor; the claim that the species exists somewhere. Operators held: the seven bonds plus negation. No new primitive is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Is the difference just a larger subtraction?

- Fig 12 :39 names subtraction: **"A without B"**. Fig 12 :84 gives its test: **"is the second operand's absence the point? Yes."**
- Local extinction is exactly that: habitat H1 with species S removed or absent. The other operands of H1 (conditions, relations) remain bonded. It is a subtraction, a binary operation with a typed relation, and the absence of the second operand is the point.
- Global extinction cannot be *only* that. If it were, it would be the conjunction of every habitat-minus-S, an ever-growing accumulation.
- Operators table :58: **"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."**
- Fig 12 :140: **"A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."**
- So a vocabulary holding only `−` can state local extinction cleanly and can only approximate global extinction by piling up subtractions. The figure gives the reason: negation is not a bond.

### Q2 — Why negation is the right operator for global

- Fig 12 :135: **"Negation is not one: it takes a term and yields a term, and joins nothing."**
- Operators table :55: *"this does not hold."* Negation refuses a claim; subtraction removes an operand.
- Global extinction refuses the claim "S exists somewhere in the world." It does not merely remove S from one arrangement. That is why the repairs differ (audit F5): reintroduction after L re-adds an operand to an existing arrangement, while restoration after G would have to re-ground a refused claim, which is habitat substitution (Fig 11 :66-69), not the inverse of a subtraction.
- **Verdict:** L is a subtraction; G is a negation of standing. They are not the same operator at two scales. Triangulated for the operator definitions (Fig 12 + Operators table, low overlap in presentation); `specified` for the ecological reading.

### Q3 — Is there a closed enumeration that makes them the same?

This is the honest pressure point. If the set of habitats were finite and closed, then "every habitat minus S" would be a finite composition and the difference between subtraction and negation would be practical rather than categorical.

- Fig 9 :44: **"Only a sealed room can be searched to the end, and no frame is a sealed room. So searching and finding nothing proves nothing."**
- The world is not a sealed room, so the enumeration of habitats is never finished. The global claim therefore remains a refusal, not a completed conjunction.
- The world source "Local extinction" agrees at the ecological level: a local loss is *"from a specified geographic area"* while the species *"continues to survive in some other part of the world"*; global means it *"no longer lives anywhere worldwide."* The "anywhere" is exactly the unclosed enumeration.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** produce a closed, finite enumeration of all habitats with a complete inventory of the species' presence. If that exists, global extinction reduces to a finite accumulation of subtractions and the categorical operator claim fails. Until then the negation reading stands.

### Q4 — What order do the loops run in?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- The order for this question is: enumerate the habitat (Fig 11 :44), then determine source availability (Fig 6, Fig 8), then index consequences per slot (Fig 1 :19), then verify against the ground (Fig 2 :45).
- A consequence study that skips step 2 and measures only the cascade in H1 is a reading below the break. It will report that L and G look similar, because it cannot see the missing source. Fig 3 :22-23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can."*
- Fig 9 :27-28: **"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."** The residual is described in effect terms before any ecological frame is chosen.

### Q5 — Are the two cases isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Written as bare ingredient sets, L and G share the same atoms (habitat, species, relations, conditions). On the ingredient list alone they look like variants of one thing.
- They are **not** isomers under Fig 12's definition, because the operator differs, not the bond between the same operands. L is a subtraction bond; G is a unary refusal. Fig 12 :49: *"Negation is the eighth operator and is not a bond; see the amendment below."*
- The correct structural term is **different operator, different substance**. The difference is invisible in an ingredient-only formula and decisive in the behaviour (recoverable versus not recoverable), which is the phenomenon Fig 12 names even though the specific bond claim does not apply. Recorded as an analogue, not an identity.

## CONCLUSION

Local extinction is a subtraction (`habitat minus species`); global extinction is a negation (refusal of the claim that the species exists anywhere). The two are not one operator at two scales. Because no enumeration of habitats is closed, the global case cannot be written as a finished accumulation of local cases, and the categorical break stands. The order of derivation is habitat enumeration, then source availability, then per-slot consequence, then ground verification; a study that skips source availability reads below the dependency break. The isomer framing fails on the strict definition (negation is not a bond), but the underlying phenomenon, same ingredients, decisive behavioural difference invisible in the formula, is exactly what Fig 12 describes.
