# Outreach draft — check the output first, then decide about the theory

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the two senses of "threshold" in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` (F1 to F5) are kept separate in the text. A note that conflates them collapses on contact.

---

**Subject:** Check one distinction before you decide whether the claim is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I am working on a narrow question in tipping-point research: can a threshold move as the climate, ecosystem or human pressures around a system change? I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the word "threshold" is doing two jobs that must be kept separate.
>
> 1. As a **property of a changing system**, a threshold is a comparison between two quantities: is one larger than the other. It is not a stored constant. When the quantities change, the comparison changes, so the threshold can move. That is the sense in which the answer to the question is yes, and it is what the rate-induced-tipping literature reports: the position of the boundary shifts with the forcing rate, the history and the background state.
> 2. As an **anchor for a decision**, a threshold must not move. If the number a decision leans on moves with the pressures it is meant to judge, the decision has no fixed reference and drifts coherently, which is why nobody notices the target has slid. In that sense a moving threshold is not a threshold you can rely on as ground.
>
> So the output is not "yes" or "no". It is: a threshold can move, and when it does it is not a ground. What must stay fixed is the measurement, not the threshold. The operative decision rule is a cost bargain that is re-struck against that measurement, not a cut held forever. This is a shape and a route, not a new number for any tipping element.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that any particular threshold has a particular value; that all thresholds move at the same rate or for the same reason; that early-warning signals reliably announce them; or that this settles whether any named tipping element is close to one. The framework returns a shape, not a name.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. a showing that "threshold" as a system property and "threshold" as a decision ground are the same quantity in the same frame at the same scale, so that one lever clears both;
> 2. a measurement of a system threshold invariant under changes of forcing rate, noise, history and background state;
> 3. a decision criterion that moves with pressures and still settles verdicts without coherent drift;
> 4. a figure line defining a threshold as a fixed constant rather than an ordered relation between two quantities.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, the shape is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the figure text and the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract, branch material): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`, especially Fig 12 :87-89, Fig 2 :29-34, Fig 5 :42 and Fig 11 :73.
> - Rate-induced tipping in natural and human systems, Ritchie et al. 2023, Earth System Dynamics: https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/14/669/2023/
> - Failure to track a stable AMOC state under rapid climate change, van Westen et al. 2026, Nature Climate Change: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02730-w
> - Global Tipping Points Report 2023, Key concepts: https://report-2023.global-tipping-points.org/introduction/key-concepts
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
