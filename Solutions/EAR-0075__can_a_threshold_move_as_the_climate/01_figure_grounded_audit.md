## 0. The residual, in effect terms

The question is "Can a threshold move as the climate, ecosystem, or human pressures around a system change?" Stated as an effect:

> A decision, model or warning system uses a threshold value. The pressures around the system change. Does the threshold stay put or move with them, and what follows for the decision that leans on it?

The residual is not the numerical value of any threshold, nor which temperature tips the AMOC. It is the identity of the object: is a threshold an anchor (fixed, settles a verdict, does not update) or a relation among changing quantities (a property of a habitat that updates)? The two answers lead to different operating rules:

- **Anchor reading:** a number can be fixed once and used as ground. If the system actually moves the number, decisions keyed to it drift coherently and nobody notices (Fig 2 :34).
- **Relation reading:** the threshold moves with its quantities; it cannot be ground; the decision must be re-struck against a measurement that does not update; the movement itself is a residual to re-measure.

What an answer would look like is bound now, before the run, as an ordinary term (formula step "Direction"): *a threshold is a comparison relation between two quantities; it moves when the quantities move; while it moves it cannot serve as the anchor that settles a verdict.* Then it can accrue a residual and be refused by the same test as anything else.

## 1. Domain census, before any figure line is chosen

### 1.1 R by arity, symmetry, scale

- **Arity:** a threshold relates at least two quantities (a system variable and a comparison level; in the world literature a state variable and a forcing, or a value and a rate). The figures define the relevant bond as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* (Fig 12 :87; Operators :49). Arity two.
- **Symmetry:** that bond is **ordered**, not symmetric. Fig 12 :89 gives the identifying test: *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."* Swapping the operands changes the verdict; this is not a `+` conjunction.
- **Scale:** a threshold operates within a scale (the state space of one subsystem) but can be re-cast at another (a warming level versus a warming rate). That cross-scale step is a lossy transform with a round-trip gap `R_T` (Fig 4 :30), not a relabelling.

### 1.2 One line per domain: actors, currency, anchor

**Domain 1, the system-dynamics domain (the thing that tips).**
Actors: state variables (temperature, salinity, biomass, ice extent), forcings (climate, freshwater, land use, emissions), feedbacks, noise. Currency: the comparison ⋛ between a state and a level, plus the cost of measuring it. Anchor: observation and measurement of the system, the ground for this domain. Nameable, so this is a domain. The threshold here is a property of the habitat.

**Domain 2, the decision and assessment domain (the thing that uses the number).**
Actors: risk assessors, managers, policy targets, "safe operating spaces". Currency: verdicts, agreements, budgets. Anchor: to settle a verdict this domain needs a reference that does not change because of what the assessors think (Fig 2 :29). If the number it uses moves with the pressures, its anchor updates and it collapses into mutual update. Nameable, so this is a second domain.

**Domain 3, the verification domain (the people checking this derivation).**
Actors: outside expert readers and their instruments. Currency: residuals and agreement. Anchor: the ground, never agreement. In this simulated run all three domains draw on one evidence pool (the same figure text, the same essays, the same opened world pages), so this domain's independence is zero. Its agreement is internal consistency, not a witness.

### 1.3 Partition or union, and dependency order

Domains 1 and 2 form a **union compound with an overlap**, not a partition. The same number is used both as a description of the system (where would it tip?) and as a ground for a decision (what target do we hold?). Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."* The overlap is where the harm lives: a threshold that moves while being treated as a fixed ground. Unbundle before searching (Fig 9 :54).

**Order by dependency (Fig 3):**
1. Describe the residual in effect terms before choosing a frame (Fig 9 :27-28): *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."*
2. Settle the anchor question before the dynamics question. If a threshold is ground, it cannot move by definition; if it is a habitat relation, it can. This decides which figure governs.
3. Decide the decision rule (bargain versus fixed cut, Fig 5 :42) after the anchor question.
4. Verify against measurement, last. One lever will not clear a union (Fig 9 :54-57).

### 1.4 Strain test applied before concluding

Fig 2 requires the anchor not to move. The world literature says thresholds move. These only strain if "threshold" names the same object in both. Re-census shows two objects: a threshold *used as ground* (Fig 2 governs) and a threshold *as a relation in a changing habitat* (Fig 11 governs). Different quantities, so no strain after re-census. This re-census is the spine of the derivation, not an afterthought.

## 3. Findings F1 to F9

### F1 — A threshold is an ordered comparison relation, not a constant (decisive for object identity)

Fig 12 :87 names the bond:

> **"a threshold relation between two quantities"**

Fig 12 :89 gives the test:

> **"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."**

Operators :49 and :51 repeat both lines. So in the figures a threshold is a binary, **ordered** relation holding between two quantities. It is not a stored number and not a symmetric co-presence. A threshold "moves" precisely when one of the quantities it compares changes, or when the comparison is re-expressed in a different quantity (a level versus a rate).

**Refuter:** a figure line defining ⋛ as a fixed constant, or as a symmetric bond, would break this. None exists; the Operators table marks sym = no.

### F2 — A threshold used as ground cannot move (decisive for the decision domain)

Fig 2 :29-30:

> **"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."**
> **"If it moves when you push on it, it is not an anchor."**

Fig 2 :33-34:

> **"The anchor must not update."**
> **"If it does, panel 3 turns into panel 1: the anchor becomes another frame, everything drifts together, and the drift is coherent, which is why nobody notices."**

Fig 10 :24:

> **"the ground does not decay, the channel does"**

So if a decision treats a threshold as the anchor that settles a verdict, that threshold must not move. If it does move, the decision has no anchor: it has become panel 1, mutual update, and the drift is coherent, which is exactly why stakeholders do not notice their target has slid. This is a structural consequence, not a metaphor.

**Refuter:** a figure line that lets an anchor legitimately update and still count as an anchor; or a measurement showing a decision criterion that moves with pressures and still settles verdicts without drift. Neither is available in the figure set.

### F3 — A system threshold belongs to the habitat, and the habitat updates (triangulated with F2)

Fig 11 :73:

> **"The habitat is not the ground. The habitat is where actors come from and it updates; the ground is what settles, and it does not."**

Fig 11 :63:

> **"Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."**

The state, forcing and feedback conditions around a tipping element are enumerated as habitat (Fig 11 :46 *"conditions: light, water, temperature, season, terrain"*). A threshold defined over those conditions is a habitat property. The habitat updates, so the threshold can move. This does not conflict with F2: F2 governs the *ground use* of a number; F3 governs the *system property*. Two objects, low overlap between routes (Fig 2 anchor rule versus Fig 11 habitat rule), so the pair is triangulated.

**Refuter:** a figure line making the habitat non-updating, or a measurement showing a system threshold invariant under all changes of forcing, rate, noise and history.

### F4 — The operative decision rule is a bargain, not a threshold (single-source)

Fig 5 :42:

> **"The acceptance test is a bargain, not a threshold."**

Fig 2 :42:

> **"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."**

On the decision side the figures do not license a fixed threshold as the rule that settles a choice. They license a cost bargain (state a candidate, price it, compare with the residual it removes) and a budget for independence set by the cost of being wrong. So even where people *use* a threshold, the figure-grounded decision procedure is to re-strike a bargain against a ground that does not update, not to hold the cut.

**Refuter:** a figure line licensing a fixed threshold as the mint acceptance rule. Fig 5 :42 states the opposite. Triangulation status: the bargain line is single-source (Fig 5 :42); the cost-of-being-wrong line is a second figure but governs the budget, not the acceptance test, so it does not upgrade this to triangulated.

### F5 — This is a union compound, not yes/no (framework-internal)

Fig 9 :57:

> **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

Fig 9 :54:

> **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**

The same number does two jobs: describing where a system would tip, and fixing a decision target. Asking "can it move?" without saying which job is meant is a bundled question. A single lever (fix the number, or let it float) clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersection standing. The verdicts must be given per domain and never averaged into a yes or a no.

**Refuter:** a demonstration that the two uses are the same quantity in the same frame at the same scale, so that one lever clears both.

### F6 — Thresholds are indexed per slot, not global (framework-internal)

Fig 1 :19:

> **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."**

Fig 1 :22:

> **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."**

Each tipping element has its own threshold and its own mechanism. A single global "the threshold" is an aggregation that hides the live slot. In the world literature this is why a shortlist of distinct tipping elements, each with its own critical control value, is the useful object (Lenton et al. 2008), not one planetary number.

**Refuter:** a figure line or measurement showing that one threshold governs all subsystems after slots are separated.

### F7 — A threshold used to allocate effort is a filter, not a witness (framework-internal)

Fig 9 :60:

> **"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."**

Ranking subsystems by how close their thresholds are is a budget filter: it decides where to look first. It does not decide whether a system will tip. A threshold estimate must never be promoted to a verdict by virtue of being the number the policy uses.

**Refuter:** none in the figure set; this is a rule the framework imposes on itself.

### F8 — Re-expressing a threshold at another scale is a lossy round trip

Fig 4 :30:

> **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

The world literature proposes converting an AMOC warming threshold into a warming-*rate* threshold (van Westen et al. 2026). Under Fig 4 that is a change of predicate across a scale membrane, not a translation. The gap `R_T` between the level description and the rate description is the measurement of what the coarser account cannot hold, and it must be reported rather than set to zero. `11_audit.md` records it qualitatively.

**Refuter:** a demonstration that the level and rate descriptions are losslessly intertranslatable for a given system, so `R_T = 0`.

### F9 — The world sources corroborate F3; they do not decide it

The world sources opened for this solution are listed in `10_source_ledger.csv` and synthesized in `09_watson_answer.md` §WORLD. In brief:

- Ritchie et al. 2023 (Earth System Dynamics, mathematics): changing forcing *"instead shifts its position"* and *"If the threshold moves past the initial position of the base state"*.
- van Westen, Börner and Dijkstra 2026 (Nature Climate Change, climate modelling): *"such a threshold may not be meaningful because AMOC stability depends on the rate of radiative forcing change, not a set temperature"*.
- Feudel 2023 (Nonlinear Processes in Geophysics, nonlinear dynamics): *"the boundaries of the basins of attraction ... start moving under time-dependent variation of intrinsic parameters or external forcing"*.
- Global Tipping Points Report 2023, Key concepts (Lenton et al., synthesis): *"A tipping point is a type of threshold."* and a hysteresis passage in which a reversed forcing reaches *"a different tipping point"*.
- Lenton et al. 2008 (PNAS, Earth-system foundations): a tipping point is *"a critical threshold at which a tiny perturbation can qualitatively alter the state or development of a system."*
- Dakos et al. 2015 (Phil Trans R Soc B, ecology): *"Critical slowing down indicators are not forecasting tools per se"* and, for fast approach, *"there may not be time for CSD indicators to be detected"*.
- Scheffer et al. 2009 (Nature, ecology): early-warning signals *"may indicate for a wide class of systems if a critical threshold is approaching"*.
- Kéfi et al. 2013 (Oikos, ecology) and Boettiger, Ross and Hastings 2013 (Theoretical Ecology, ecological theory): slowing down is not specific to catastrophic transitions and warning signals generalize only in certain contexts.

These predict that system thresholds move with rate, history, noise and background state, and that observing an approaching threshold is limited. They corroborate F3 and F8. They do not decide the verdict: that comes from the figures (F1 to F4). The rate-induced sources share the tipping-dynamics literature and partly cite one another, and the early-warning sources share the Scheffer school, so these are evidence pools, not independent witnesses. No world source conflicts with the figure verdict; the conflict rule was not triggered.

**Refuter:** a world measurement showing a threshold invariant under rate, history, noise and background-state changes. It would be returned to the census, not used to flip the figure verdict arbitrarily.

## 4. Pre-registered refuter (written before the conclusion)

"A falsifier written before the run is a design instrument." The shape claim — *a threshold is a movable relation; a moving threshold cannot be ground; the decision is a bargain re-struck against a non-updating measurement* — is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. A figure line defines ⋛ as a fixed constant or a symmetric bond (breaks F1).
2. A figure line lets an anchor legitimately update and still count as an anchor (breaks F2).
3. A measurement shows a threshold used as a decision ground also moving as a habitat property of the same system at the same scale, with no scale or predicate change to separate them (breaks the union separation F5 and the F2/F3 re-census).
4. A figure line licenses a fixed threshold as the acceptance rule for a mint (breaks F4).
5. A measurement shows a system threshold invariant under all changes of forcing rate, noise, history and background state (breaks F3 and F9).

If any fires, the shape is withdrawn or re-censused. Until one is run, nothing here is `grounded`.

## 5. Recommendation

1. State which sense of "threshold" is in play before answering: system property or decision ground.
2. Do not anchor a verdict or a policy target to a threshold that moves with the pressures it is meant to judge (F2). If the number must move, move it as an explicitly re-struck bargain against a measurement that does not update (F4).
3. Index thresholds per tipping element, not as one global number (F6).
4. Treat threshold estimates as filters for where to look first, never as witnesses for what will happen (F7).
5. When re-expressing a threshold (level to rate), report the round-trip gap `R_T` rather than assuming a lossless translation (F8).
6. Send the generator: the shape claim, the two senses, the refuter and the provenance, in that order (Fig 10 :52 *"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."*).

## 7. Updated verdict

The shape survives after re-census. A threshold in the figures is an ordered comparison relation (F1), so it can and does move when its quantities move. But a threshold used as the anchor that settles a verdict must not move; if it does, it stops being an anchor and the decision drifts coherently (F2). A system threshold is a habitat property, and the habitat updates while the ground does not (F3), which is exactly what the world literature reports for rate-induced tipping, moving basin boundaries and hysteresis. On the decision side the figures license a bargain re-struck against a ground that does not update (F4), not a fixed cut. The question is a union of two uses (F5), thresholds are per slot (F6), estimates are filters not witnesses (F7), and re-expressing a threshold across scales carries a measurable gap `R_T` (F8).

Strains after re-census: one live (S3, which quantity the threshold lives in), none hidden. The verdict is `specified`, ungrounded, candidate. It states what any answer must look like and what would settle it; it does not settle it.
