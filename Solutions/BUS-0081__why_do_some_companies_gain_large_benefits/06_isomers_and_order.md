# Same tools, different benefit: isomers, bonds and order

**Mode: `specified` on the firm-level reading, `general` on the notation rule, with one live strain on the fixed-atom condition. This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).**

## RESIDUAL

The phrase "despite buying similar tools" asserts a shared atom set and a divergent outcome. Two questions are owed:

1. Under Fig 12's definition, are two firms with the same tool atom **isomers**?
2. Does the **order** of the bonds matter, and if so, which bond types are involved?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: the tool `T`, data `D`, skills `K`, routines `R`, decision rights `Q` and the market reading `M`. Bonds held: the seven of Fig 12, including sequence (`->`), conjunction (`+`), production (`=>`) and comparison (`⋛`). No new primitive is available without a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 8 :50-52). The six physical terms of Fig 13 are **not** used here; the strain that required renaming a tool as energy was retracted (see `01` section 6, S1).

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Is "same tools" the same atom set?

- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands. A recipe that names only its ingredients has recorded half of itself."**
- The purchase order records `T`. It does not record which bond holds between `T` and `D`, `K`, `R`, `Q` or `M`. So the thing the two firms are said to share is an ingredient, not a molecule.
- **Correction to my own first pass.** I initially wrote that two buyers hold the same atom set and are therefore isomers. That overstates: real buyers hold different data, skills and routines, so the sets are never literally identical. The strong isomer claim is a **controlled comparison over a declared shared set**. The always-available weak claim is that both configurations are members of the same functional group (Fig 12 :124): *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Both forms are now recorded. The strong form carries a live strain, S2.

### Q2: Are they isomers under the figure's definition?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Under the controlled shared set `{T, D, K, R, Q, M}`, firm A might bond `T` to `D` by production (`T => D'`), `D'` to `K` by sequence (`->`), and `K` to `Q` by conjunction (`+`), while firm B bonds `T` to `K` by sequence, leaves `D` conjoined but unread, and measures `M` with a saturated metric. Same atoms, different bonds, different substance. The purchase order cannot tell them apart, which is exactly the figure's definition.
- Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** Under a symmetric bond such as `+`, k operands have k! spellings and one term, so counting every reordering of a tool stack as a new capability overcharges by log2(k!) (Fig 12 :92-93). Under an ordered bond such as `->`, different orders are different terms and charging for the sequence is correct (Operators :59-62). So order matters only where the bond is ordered, and a "tool stack" that is really a conjunction has no order to discover.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two firms with the declared shared atom set and demonstrably identical typed bonds, measured per slot, still show different realized market benefits, the isomer claim fails and the difference must be located in the habitat or the channel (D2/D4) instead. If two firms with different bonds show indistinguishable benefits after the ground test, the bond carries nothing and F1 collapses.

### Q3: What order does the chain have?

- Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**
- Fig 12 :112: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."**
- The ordered part of a technology adoption is a plan: perceive the market response, acquire terms for what the tool affords, say what progress would count as, deploy, then learn and carry (Fig 3 :4-17). This is a `->` chain. If any link's input never arrives, the link below the break is not a weak reading; it is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23).
- "Large benefit" is `⋛`, a comparison between a realized return and a baseline or competitor (Fig 12 :87). It is not the same bond as the plan. A benefit figure is the output of a comparison applied to an outcome, not the outcome itself, and it cannot be used to score the frame that produced it (Fig 1 :27).
- The tool itself may be a `=>` production bond when it brings a new representation into being: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Fig 12 :77). Whether a given tool is production or merely conjunction is an empirical question about contact, not a naming choice. Fig 6 :26: *"instruments extend what can be represented at all."*

### Q4: What is the relation between pilot and production?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."** A pilot that has settled against a ground becomes an atom at the next level, so scaling is composition, not a replay.
- Fig 4 :23: **"upward: a method that can be reapplied. Never the recording of a particular success."** What crosses from pilot to production must be a method. The vendor's playbook is usually a recording.
- Fig 11 :66: **"Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."** So pilot and production are not one arrangement at two sizes; they are two arrangements in two habitats. They are **not** isomers under the strict definition, because the atom sets differ: the production habitat contains actors and conditions the pilot did not have. The correct relation is composition plus habitat substitution with two silent failures (Fig 11 :67-69).
- **Falsifier:** if a pilot's method can be reapplied after a habitat substitution with no loss and with no new actor inherited, the isolation law of Fig 11 :66 is false.

### Q5: What do the two currencies price?

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- A tool's licence cost and integration cost price the description and the actions of building the arrangement. They do not price the act of finding out whether the arrangement pays in this slot. Fig 12 :128: **"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."** A business case that sums purchase price, training hours and a single expected benefit figure has summed two incommensurable currencies and declared the result a verdict. That is the naming error Fig 12 :125 exists to prevent.

## CONCLUSION

"Same tools, different benefit" is an **isomer question over a declared shared atom set**, with a weaker and always-available functional-group reading when the sets differ. The bond is the variable, not the ingredient, and order matters only where the bond is ordered. A pilot and its production rollout are not isomers; they are a method crossing a habitat boundary, with silent failures on both sides. The two currencies of arrangement (description cost and action cost) do not add to a benefit figure. These are structural results from Fig 12, Fig 4 and Fig 11, not measurements of any firm. The strong isomer claim remains `specified` with a live fixed-atom strain, S2.
