# The Tether's answer: weak-state legitimacy and capable-state delegitimation

**Mode: `specified` on the mechanisms, `general` on the structural shape, not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a result. It is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §7), not a ground reading. No country was scored.

## 1. The residual it answers

Weak states sometimes retain legitimacy and capable states sometimes lose it. Read as an effect: why does the per-slot gap between a state's claim to rightful rule and what the governed actually do stay small in some low-capacity cases and grow in some high-capacity cases? The question is a **union of two conditions**, not one capacity axis with legitimacy as its dependent variable (Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*). The gap is indexed per citizen or group slot, never averaged (Fig 1 :19, :22).

## 2. The shape

1. **Two anchors, not one stock.** Capacity is an action quantity; legitimacy is a ground-contact quantity whose anchor is the governed. They have different anchors and they do not add (Fig 2 :29; Fig 12 :125-126). The idea of a legitimacy stock that capacity fills is a category error in the figure's terms.

2. **The governed are the anchor for this question.** Fig 2 :36: *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."* Legitimacy is constitutively about the governed, so their live response, not the state's own metric and not elite acclaim, settles it. The state and the governed are corrected separately by a ground neither can move (Fig 2 :46).

3. **Weak states maintain legitimacy through contact and introduction.** Capability is a property of state-and-habitat, never of the state alone (Fig 11 :39). An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing (Fig 11 :40). The available lever is introduction, not exclusion (Fig 11 :61); this is permanent work, not a solved state (Fig 11 :63). It is why low capacity does not mechanically produce illegitimacy, a prediction corroborated from social psychology (system justification) at low overlap.

4. **Capable states lose it through two separate loops.**
   - **Stake / standing:** standing survives the outcome it was earned on (Fig 10 :61), so the capable state's officers come to benefit from the value the ground returns. The anchor becomes another frame and everything drifts together coherently, which is why nobody notices (Fig 2 :34). A ground with a reputation to protect has a disqualified keeper (Fig 10 :93).
   - **Channel / metrics:** capacity buys statistics, and statistics pool the live signal away (Fig 1 :22; Fig 10 :25-28). The state receives an output that is not a verdict but the signal that it has lost touch. Scott's legibility thesis corroborates this from political ecology at low overlap.

5. **Legitimacy is a convention, and cashed conventions end.** Agreement about a shared meaning is the product for a convention, not a corruption (Fig 2 :41). But *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"* (Fig 2 :43; Fig 10 :47). When a capable state uses its legitimacy as a credential to settle a contested outcome, it has stopped having a convention; a room's agreement is not a verdict (Fig 7 :35).

6. **Weak and capable are not opposite kinds.** They are values of one atom whose valence flips with the residual (Fig 11 :49). Over a shared atom set, the four cases are isomers: same atoms, different bonds, different substance (Fig 12 :117). The two loss mechanisms are a union of separate loops, never added into one score (see `06_isomers_and_order.md`).

7. **No reform is named.** The figures return a shape, not a name (Fig 9 :32; Fig 13 :134). Which specific service, decentralisation or accountability body restores legitimacy in a given place is F8's `gap`, unreachable at this budget. What is owed is a per-slot, source-discriminating instrument.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that legitimacy is a national score, a reservoir, or a stock that capacity fills.
- Not that all weak states are legitimate or all capable states illegitimate. Valence is residual-relative; counterexamples are expected.
- Not that capacity is bad. Capacity spent on the channel is the legitimate use; capacity spent on standing is the corroding one.
- Not that coercion and interests play no role. They are real; this derivation only refuses to score them with frame-internal credibility.
- Not a country verdict, not a reform recommendation, not a league table.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement settles `left(R,φ)` here, and this package is a simulation, not a ground reading.
- Not a claim that this framework is the only one that could produce the shape. That would be a uniqueness claim over a census nobody ran (Fig 9 :44: *"Only a sealed room can be searched to the end, and no frame is a sealed room."*).

## 4. What would settle it : the ground

An instrument, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: a reading that cannot discriminate its sources demonstrates its own inadequacy, and it was the spectrometer, not an argument, that resolved the green-reading case. Here the corresponding instruments are:

- **Per-slot longitudinal contact measure:** does the same citizen or group slot stay in contact with the state across a change of government? A national average cannot answer this (Fig 1 :22).
- **Stake discriminator:** can a state officer's reading of legitimacy be shown to move when their own standing is at issue, independently of what the ground returns? If yes, Fig 10 :93 fires.
- **Metric resolution test:** does the legitimacy instrument retain per-slot resolution after aggregation, or does the average hide a live slot (Fig 1 :13, :20-21)?
- **Convention-outcome test:** can the state use its legitimacy credential to decide a contested outcome and still show per-slot legitimacy against a non-updating ground (Fig 2 :43)?
- **Standing falsifiers from `01`:** a monotone capacity effect at fixed contact; an updating anchor; an average that beats per-slot measurement; equal loss rates with and without a stake.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** The instrument that would name the specific reform is characterized in effect terms but not held (Fig 6 :34-40: you cannot specify a sensor from a description of Mars, you improve one already returning something). The figure set stops at F8's gap and records the lineage. *"You get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."* Indiscernibility is not identity; the record of the route is what keeps the stop checkable. Agreement inside this folder is one evidence pool talking, not corroboration (Fig 2 :40).

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not say that weak states are good at legitimacy or that capable states are secretly brittle. It says the question bundles two different quantities with different anchors: capacity is what a state can do, legitimacy is the governed treating it as a rightful anchor that does not move when they push. A weak state keeps legitimacy while its per-slot channel to the ground stays reachable and its lever stays introduction rather than exclusion; a capable state loses it when standing gives its officers a stake in their own reading and its statistics pool away the live signal, and when it cashes its legitimacy as a credential to settle an outcome, at which point it has stopped being a convention. Weak and capable are not opposite kinds but isomers over a shared set of atoms, so the question's own grammar misleads. What the framework offers is that shape, five refuters written in advance, and the kind of instrument that would name the missing reform. It does not name the reform, and it is not a measurement.
