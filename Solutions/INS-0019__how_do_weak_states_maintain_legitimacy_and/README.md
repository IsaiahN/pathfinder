# Solution INS-0019 : Tether outreach package

**Question:** How do weak states maintain legitimacy, and how do capable states lose it?

**Source:** `Questions/institutional questions.txt` line 24

**Section:** 2. State capacity and public administration

**Mode:** `specified` on the substantive claims, `general` on the structural shape. No measurement settles the binding here, so nothing is `grounded` and nothing is `accepted`. Every substantive claim carries its mode where it appears.

**Status:** done

**Registry status:** pending

**Verdict in one line:** Legitimacy and capacity are different quantities with different anchors, coupled by a channel to a non-updating ground; weak states keep legitimacy by keeping that channel reachable and by introduction rather than exclusion, while capable states lose it when standing gives them a stake in the reading and pooled metrics erase the per-slot signal. This is a `specified`, ungrounded shape, not a measurement.

---

## What the question decides, in effect terms

Option A: legitimacy is a stock that capacity accumulates and protects. A weak state should therefore lack legitimacy, and a capable state should have it. Building capacity first is the remedy, and legitimacy is read off outputs.

Option B: legitimacy is a relation of contact between a state and a ground that does not update on what the state thinks. Capacity is a contact-changing lever whose valence is relative to the residual. It can be spent on the channel, or it can be spent on the state's own standing, in which case it corrodes the relation it was meant to serve.

What each leads to: A predicts a monotone capacity/legitimacy curve and recommends output metrics. B predicts a union of two conditions (contact maintained, reach renewed) with different anchors, requires the residual to be indexed per citizen slot rather than averaged into a national trust score, and requires an outside seat that can refuse the state's own verdict. The derivation below finds B figure-licensed and A refused as written.

The answer bound as an ordinary term before the run: *legitimacy is the governed treating the state as an anchor that ranges against their ground and does not update on its own reputation.* Binding it makes the preference refusable by the same test as everything else (formula step "Direction").

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to believe the claim.
- No bulk-send plan.
- No country score, no league table of "legitimate states", and no claim that the figures name a specific reform.

Three reasons, in the framework's own words:

1. A room does not upgrade the label. Fig 7 :13: *"agreement compounds, evidence does not"*. A list selected for people already sympathetic to the claim produces a convention, not corroboration.
2. Correlation disqualifies a verdict. Fig 2 :40: *"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"*. Selecting recipients for receptiveness manufactures the correlation that makes their agreement worthless.
3. Asking for trust before checking spends independence. Fig 10 :42: *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* Seat 4 must not hold the interiors.

Legally: unsolicited bulk email to scraped addresses breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules in many jurisdictions. That is a practical failure on top of the structural one. Public opt-in and institutional submission routes avoid both.
