# Redundancy, functional groups and isomers: the supporting derivation

## RESIDUAL

The question's ordinary word "biodiversity" treats species as interchangeable units in a list. Fig 12 :5 says the opposite: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Two questions are owed: (1) what is the structural relation between two species that perform the same function, and (2) does the order in which loss happens change the outcome?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is two species that do the same job?

Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance." Fig 13 :123: "Two chains built from different links may compose functionally identical terms". Fig 13 :124: "the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."

Two species that compose the same function are **not** duplicates in the figure's sense. They are two different routes to one substance, and Fig 13's warning runs both ways: the same set of species under a different arrangement of relations can be a different ecological substance. A pollinator and a decomposer are not interchangeable; a guild of four pollinators with different phenologies can compose one pollination substance.

Fig 12 :117 gives the discriminating definition: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Two communities with the same species list and different interaction networks are isomers, not versions of one thing. A richness count records the formula and misses the substance.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two communities with identical species lists but different interaction arrangements are shown to have the same function under the same perturbation, the isomer reading fails and the flat formula reading stands instead for that function.

### Q2: Is functional redundancy a functional group?

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." The group fixes kind; the qualifier fixes which member.

Redundancy is not "extra species". It is a functional group with more than one member that can occupy the same substructure. Moffett et al. 2026 report that hazard regulation depends on "a few functionally unique foundational species whose persistence nonetheless is interdependent on the wider community". That is exactly the functional-group form: a small substructure fixes the outcome, and its members depend on the wider network for persistence. Remove the group and the function goes even if the species count stays high. Keep the group and remove ten unrelated species and the function may persist.

Fig 12 :119 adds the catalyst case: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is." A perennial canopy, a soil mycorrhizal network or a seed bank is a part that survives its own use. A harvest that removes the catalyst is not equivalent to a harvest that removes the same mass of a spent part.

**Falsifier:** if a function is shown to be fixed by total biomass rather than by the presence of an identifiable group, the functional-group reading is reduced to a stock reading for that function.

### Q3: Does order of loss matter?

Fig 12 :103: "Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible." Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." Fig 13 :115: "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."

Order matters when the thing lost becomes an atom for what composes over it. Losing a foundational group first collapses the level on which the remaining species compose; losing a peripheral species first leaves that level intact and only reduces the count. So the order of loss is an ordered bond (Operators table :33: "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."), not a symmetric one. A pooled total of species lost over a decade is a symmetric reading and throws the order away.

Fig 12 :121 is the dynamic form: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Ecosystem recovery is a cycle: the output of one season's growth re-enters as next season's input. A disturbance that breaks the cycle is not the same as one that reduces its rate. Fig 13 :31-32 says that a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos; the detail a crossing discards reappears above it as noise. So small losses of a catalyst can have effects far larger than their share of the species list, which is the contact cascade of Fig 11 :52.

**Falsifier:** if the order of loss is shown to be irrelevant for a function once total functional-group count is held constant, the ordered-bond reading is refused for that function and the symmetric count reading stands.

### Q4: What is the union of the three outcomes?

Over the shared atom set {species, response types, functional groups, relations, stocks, disturbances}, the three outcomes are not isomers of one another. Resilience reads as selection between rooms (Fig 7 :33), carbon as a conserved stock composed across kinds (Fig 13 :14, :74), recovery as habitat substitution plus an ordered trajectory (Fig 11 :66, Fig 13 :12). They share atoms but fix different outcomes, which is Fig 12 :123's functional group reading at a higher level: the group says what kind, the qualifier says which member.

Moffett et al. 2026 give the world check: blue carbon is "exceptionally sensitive" while hazard regulation is "relatively insensitive to biodiversity, instead depending on a few functionally unique foundational species". Two outcomes, two verdicts, different substructures. They must not be averaged into one "biodiversity matters" score.

**Falsifier:** if one intervention is shown to move all three outcomes through a single shared mechanism with no residual in any branch, the union collapses to a partition and Fig 9 :55 is refused for this question.

### Q5: Two currencies, one trap

Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." Fig 12 :126: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds." Fig 12 :128: "A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."

A species list is cheap to state and expensive to settle (which species actually fixes which function). A single stock measurement is expensive to state in full detail and may be one action to refute with an inventory. Mixing the two prices into one "biodiversity value" index is the error Fig 12 names. The ledger's costs and the figure's costs are different currencies; even the two figure currencies do not add, so they certainly do not add to a policy score.

## CONCLUSION

Redundancy is a functional group with multiple members, not extra names. Two communities with the same species list and different relations are isomers (Fig 12 :117), and carbon, resilience and recovery are different functionals over one shared atom set, not versions of one quantity. Order of loss matters wherever the lost term becomes an atom for a higher composition (Fig 12 :104), and cycles make small losses of a catalyst larger than their share. Three falsifiers are stated above; none was fired by this derivation. The result is `specified`, ungrounded, and cannot substitute for a measured function, inventory or trajectory.
