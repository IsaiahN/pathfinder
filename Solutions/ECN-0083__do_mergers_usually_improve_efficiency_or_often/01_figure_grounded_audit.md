## 0. The claim under audit

> Do mergers usually improve efficiency, or often reduce competition and bargaining power?

The sentence is an either/or between two outcomes of one operation. The operation is a composition of two firms under common control. Option A says the composition pays: lower average cost, economies of scale and scope, elimination of duplicated fixed costs, better coordination of complementary assets. Option B says it costs: higher prices, fewer choices, weaker wages, weaker supplier terms, less entry, less innovation.

Stated as a shape before any theory is applied: an answer would have to (1) name the slot being measured, (2) name an anchor that does not move when the merged firm pushes on it, (3) state what observation would count as a failure, and (4) keep that verdict separate from the verdict on every other slot. This is bound as an ordinary term now, so it can accrue a residual like anything else (formula step "Direction"; Fig 5 :65-66 on making the sought-for shape an ordinary term).

The question as written supplies none of those four things. That is the finding, not a preface to it.

## 1. The residual in effect terms

**R as error signal.** What failed to resolve is not "mergers" in the abstract. It is a set of before-and-after comparisons in which two frames give incompatible readings of the same event: the merging parties project cost savings, while rival firms, workers, consumers and agencies report price, wage, variety and innovation changes. Fig 1 :23 "The ground enters as the only metric." The residual is the gap between the projected saving and the registered outcome, per slot.

**Arity.** At least four slots, not two.

| slot | quantity before and after | who registers it |
|---|---|---|
| S1 productive efficiency | average cost, output, investment | the merged firm's cost data, a counterfactual |
| S2 allocative effect | price, quantity, variety, quality | consumers, market data |
| S3 dynamic competition | entry, R and D, innovation | rivals, patents, product introductions |
| S4 input-side bargaining | wages, hiring, supplier terms | workers, suppliers, labour market data |

The question collapses these into two words, "efficiency" and "competition". That is an aggregation across slots. Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." Fig 1 :22 "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." Symbols table :43 "R is measured per slot, never as a single global number."

**Symmetry.** The two conjuncts are not mirror images and do not join under a symmetric bond. "Efficiency" is a claim about one firm's cost function against a counterfactual. "Competition reduction" is a claim about the strategic dependence of the remaining firms and about the terms offered to trading partners. Different operand types, different anchors. Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands." The operators table :68 "A relation that fails all seven is not an operator you are missing; it is a claim that has not been made yet." No figure supplies a bond joining S1 to S2 through S4. The either/or is not a comparison in the figure's sense.

**Scale.** Three scales are in play: the firm (cost, organization), the market (price, wages, entry) and the welfare evaluation (a convention chosen by an agency or court). Fig 4 :27 "The transform between scales loses information, and that is the point." A cost saving registered at the firm does not compose by addition into a welfare verdict at the market; the round trip has to be measured, not assumed (Fig 4 :30).

**Dependency order (Fig 3).** Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." The links here are: (1) is there an anchor that can register cost independent of the merged firm's influence, (2) do we have terms for what a merger affords in each slot, (3) can we say what would count as progress in each slot, (4) can we design a before-and-after instrument with a control, (5) can we name what worked and carry it to the next case. Most published merger analysis effort lands at links 4 and 5 (simulation, retrospective), while link 1, the anchor, is often assumed. Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

## 2. Domain census, done before choosing figure lines

One line per domain: actors, currency, anchor. A domain without a nameable anchor is not yet a domain.

| domain | actors | currency | anchor | partition or union | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 the firm as composition | the two merging firms, then the merged firm; managers, plants, assets | cost, output, investment, projected synergy | a cost counterfactual independent of the merging parties' influence | one slot of a union | anchor is model-dependent, so weak until a retrospective measures it |
| D2 the market as measurement device | remaining firms, consumers, workers, suppliers | price, wage, quantity, variety, quality | competition itself, named as a market anchor in Fig 2 :29 | overlaps D1 and D4, union not partition | nameable anchor, strong |
| D3 the evaluation frame | agencies, courts, economists, expert witnesses | evidence, burden of proof, decisions | consumer welfare or total welfare, a declared convention | separate loop, consumes D1 and D2 | anchor is declared, not grounded |
| D4 the question-posing frame | the asker of "usually", the aggregation itself | frequency, an average over cases | a distribution of per-slot results over a defined population of mergers | union of overlapping conditions | no single anchor for an unqualified "usually" |

**Order by dependency (Fig 3).** Settle D2's anchor before admitting D1's claim (the cost reading is only interpretable against a market that still discriminates). Settle D1 and D2 per slot before D3 chooses a welfare standard. Unbundle D4 before any of the three, because a union asked as one question sends every later step to the wrong place (Fig 9 :54).

**Partition or union.** This is a union, not a partition. A merger that raises price in a concentrated product market and lowers cost through plant consolidation is a case in two conditions, not a harder case of either. Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :57 "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."

**Strain test applied before concluding.** An early reading tried to apply Fig 13's six-term substrate directly to firms, treating a merger as a forbidden within-kind bond. That strains: firms are frames, not states or structures of the substrate. Re-census: Fig 13 governs what composes at the substrate level; a merger changes the actor set of a market and belongs under D1 and D2. The reading is withdrawn, not smoothed over, and kept in section 8 and in `06_isomers_and_order.md` Q1.

## 4. Findings

### F1: The question is a union asked as if it were one question (decisive for the form)

Fig 9 :4-5: "First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."
Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."

The empirical literature behaves exactly as Fig 9 predicts. Bhattacharya, Illanes and coauthors (NBER Working Paper 31123, 2025) find a small average price effect of completed consumer-packaged-goods mergers, between minus 0.6 and plus 1.0 percent, but a standard deviation of 4.0 to 7.5 percentage points and quartiles from minus 2.1 to plus 3.7 percent. A single "usually" is precisely the average that discards the signal. The FTC Bureau of Economics retrospective program likewise reports that retrospectives concentrate on short-run price changes, with limited evidence on innovation, productivity and non-price attributes, and that the mergers studied are on the enforcement margin, not a random sample. That is a selected subset, so it cannot license a frequency over all mergers.

**Mode:** `general` for the structural refusal, `specified` for the claim that these four slots are the operative ones. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 9 and Fig 1, with the empirical heterogeneity as secondary corroboration only. **Refuter:** show that the four slots reduce to one quantity with one anchor under a transformation that preserves the per-slot verdicts, or produce a census-defined population of mergers for which one scalar predictor predicts every slot.

### F2: Efficiency claimed by the merging parties is self-scoring

Fig 1 :27: "They are frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."
Fig 1 :23: "The ground enters as the only metric."
Fig 5 :48: "Its own output is never a measure of success. A system can be enthusiastically curious and produce nothing."

The 2023 Merger Guidelines require that cognizable efficiencies be "verifiable, and have been verified, using reliable methodology and evidence not dependent on the subjective predictions of the merging parties or their agents", and note that "efficiencies projected by the merging firms often are not realized." That is an operational statement of Fig 1 :27 in the language of the field. It is also why "the merging parties say they will save money" cannot close R: the quantity is produced by the frame that scores it.

**Mode:** `specified`. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 1, Fig 5 and an independent field document (DOJ and FTC, 2023), with low overlap between a metatheory about frames and an administrative evidentiary rule. **Refuter:** a case in which the projecting frame cannot in principle influence the measurement, so its projection is not self-scoring; or a figure line that permits a frame to score itself under a declared code.

### F3: Competition is the anchor, and market power moves the anchor (decisive for D2)

Fig 2 :29: "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks. A measurement, an experiment, a counter, a market, a patient outcome. If it moves when"
Fig 2 :30: "you push on it, it is not an anchor."
Fig 2 :33: "The anchor must not update."
Fig 2 :34: "If it does, panel 3 turns into panel 1: the anchor becomes another frame, everything drifts together, and the drift is coherent, which is why nobody notices."
Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."

The question "is this merged firm efficient?" needs a ground that reveals cost. In a competitive market, price and wage readings approach that role because no single firm can move them at will. When a merger gives the firm power, it can move the readings that were serving as the measure. The measure then reports the firm's influence rather than its cost. That is Fig 2's collapse 1 under a different name: what the frames converge on is whoever had the most influence (Fig 2 :13-15). It is also the pattern named independently in measurement theory and accountability studies as Goodhart's law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Goodhart's law is a low-overlap corroborator from a different field, not a figure line and not a witness for the economics.

This yields a structural asymmetry that the question's either/or hides. A genuine efficiency gain changes contact: more output from the same inputs, registered against a ground the firm cannot move (Fig 11 :40 "An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."). A market-power gain changes the frame's leverage over the measure. Both can raise the merged firm's profit. Only the first survives the anchor test.

**Mode:** `general` for the anchor rule, `specified` for its application to price and wage readings. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 2, Fig 11 and Fig 10 :47 ("a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"), with Goodhart's law as low-overlap corroboration. **Refuter:** show that under the merger the price and wage readings still discriminate cost independent of the firm's strategic choice; or show that competition is not the anchor for the efficiency question because the question is not constitutively about a market outcome (Fig 2 :36 "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it.").

### F4: Bargaining power is the same anchor failure on the input side

Fig 11 :49 "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."
Fig 11 :50 "The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."
Fig 11 :51 "No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."

The DOJ and FTC 2023 Guidelines apply the same frameworks to mergers between buyers, explicitly including employers (Guideline 10). The BLS Monthly Labor Review (Thompson, 2024) finds that in 2023 the average US local labor market was highly concentrated under antitrust thresholds, that a 10 percent increase in employer concentration was associated with about a 0.3 percent decrease in average wages in the OLS specification and about 1.0 percent in the IV specification, and that a simulated merger of the top two employers would meet the enhanced market power presumption in 60 percent of non-monopsony local markets. Azar, Marinescu and Steinbaum (Journal of Human Resources, 2022) report that moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of concentration is associated with a 5 percent OLS to 17 percent IV decline in posted wages, and state plainly that "mergers of employers have the potential to significantly increase labor market power."

A large employer is not good or bad by kind. Its valence flips with the residual: a benefactor under a productivity residual, a malfactor under a wage residual. Fig 11 forbids fixing its sign in advance. What is not forbidden is the anchor test: if the merged employer can set the wage, the wage no longer reports labor's alternative value.

**Mode:** `specified`. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 11 and Fig 2, with the labour sources as secondary corroboration from one broad labour-economics evidence pool (they cite one another and share datasets, so they count as one witness). **Refuter:** a merger that increases employer concentration and is followed by wages tracking marginal product more closely, with an instrument that separates the two.

### F5: The aggregate "usually" is a filter, not a witness

Fig 9 :60 "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."
Fig 7 :13 "agreement compounds, evidence does not"
Fig 2 :40 "disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"

Concentration metrics (HHI thresholds, market share) are filters for the budget. They tell an agency where to spend attention. They do not decide the welfare verdict. The 2023 Guidelines themselves say the Guidelines "create no independent rights or obligations" and that enforcement decisions "require prosecutorial discretion and judgment". Many retrospective studies share data sources, industries and the enforcement-margin selection rule, so their agreement is one evidence pool talking. Fig 7 :13 applies.

**Mode:** `general`. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 9, Fig 7, Fig 2. **Refuter:** a filter whose crossing entails the verdict by a derivation that does not import the welfare standard as a premise.

### F6: The welfare standard is a declared convention, not a figure fact

Fig 10 :18 "a declared meaning is not a grounded one"
Fig 10 :49 "Declaring a meaning does not ground it."
Fig 10 :47 "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"

Whether to count only consumer surplus or total surplus is a choice of convention. The DOJ's own "Welfare Standards And Merger Analysis: Why Not The Best?" makes the case for a total welfare standard against the consumer welfare focus, and the literature records long disagreement. Fig 10 says a declared meaning is not a grounded one, and a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being a convention. So the figures constrain the form (the standard must remain checkable and violable, and the person applying it must declare the stake) but do not choose between the two standards.

**Mode:** `general` for the convention claim; the particular choice is a `gap`. **Status:** single-source for the figure rule (Fig 10), corroborated by the existence of an explicit intra-agency debate. **Refuter:** derive one welfare standard from the figures without declaring it.

### F7: What survives the composition: a change in contact, not a change in size

Fig 11 :39 "So the answerable question is not what intelligence is, but what makes a system capable. And capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."
Fig 11 :40 "A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."
Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."

Firm size is a change in the agent. A cost saving that increases output is a change in contact with the ground. Fig 11 :40 separates them. Williamson's 1968 tradeoff model is the field's own version of the same separation: a merger with nontrivial real economies produces a net negative allocative effect only if it also produces substantial market power and relatively large price increases. The DOJ historical account records that "cost savings almost always benefit consumers because even a monopolist would pass some portion of any cost savings on to its customers, unless its demand function was perfectly inelastic." That is a conditional statement about a mechanism, exactly the kind of claim a figure can constrain but not settle, since the demand elasticity is owed to measurement.

**Mode:** `specified` for the contact distinction, `general` for the figure rule. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 11, Fig 4 and the historical account, low overlap. **Refuter:** a documented merger that increases output at lower cost with no change in any anchor reading, which would show the contact claim is not operational here.

### F8: The retrospective is an instrument, not a new primitive

Fig 6 :32 "An instrument is not built from a description. It is improved from a worse instrument already returning something."
Fig 6 :23 "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."
Fig 6 :25 "An import adds no atom to the world."

The FTC retrospective program is described as assessing "how accurately a pricing pressure index or a merger simulation model predicted a post-merger price change" by comparing predictions with outcomes estimated using difference-in-differences against unaffected markets. That is an instrument improved from a worse instrument: prediction checked against outcome. Nothing new is added to the ontology. The empirical literature also records the limits of the instrument: most retrospectives study horizontal mergers and short-run price changes, fewer than 100 mergers have been studied out of tens of thousands, and selection is on the enforcement margin. Those are budget and reach facts, not proof of absence (Fig 8 :39 "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence.").

**Mode:** `specified`. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 6, Fig 8 and the FTC program description. **Refuter:** a retrospective design that does not improve on a prior instrument yet still settles the per-slot residual; or a figure line allowing a description to generate an instrument.

### F9: Agreement between economic studies is not corroboration of the shape claim

A finding that prices rose after close-rival mergers (FTC hospital retrospective materials, various industry studies) and a finding of near-zero average price change with large heterogeneity (Bhattacharya, Illanes and coauthors, 2025) are often read as a conflict. Under Fig 8 :50 they are not two independent frames if they draw on the same enforcement-margin selection and the same outcome classes. Fig 8 :50 "But shared atoms are not the signature. Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence." Convergence would require separate derivation, which requires the record of where each came from. When the record is missing, treat them as one witness and send the conflict back to the census (Fig 2 :35).

**Mode:** `general`. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 8 and Fig 2. **Refuter:** two studies with genuinely disjoint data, disjoint selection rules and disjoint outcome definitions that disagree on the same per-slot sign.

### F10: The residual's empirical sign is unreached, not silence

Fig 9 :32 "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"
Fig 13 :134 "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"
Fig 13 :136 "It can say the question is not one question and must be split before it is searched again."

No figure line fixes whether a given class of mergers, in a given market and period, lowers cost more than it raises price or wage. The figures fix the shape of the test and the disqualification rules. The sign is owed to a ground reading: a retrospective with a valid control, an anchor outside the merging parties' influence and a stated per-slot population. Until that is run for a defined class, the honest mark is `gap` on sign and frequency, reported as a verdict. Which class to study is a budget decision, not a witness.

**Mode:** `general` for the limit, `gap` for the sign. **Status:** triangulated across Fig 9, Fig 13, Fig 8. **Refuter:** a figure line that yields the sign for a named class, or a completed measurement meeting F3's anchor condition.

## 5. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per Fig 10's requirement that a falsifier be stated in advance and the seat-map rule that "a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."

The shape claim (the question is a union of per-slot anchor tests, the efficiency projection is self-scoring until grounded, market power moves the anchor, and the aggregate usually is refused) is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. A figure line directly governs the sign of the efficiency-versus-power tradeoff for a named class of mergers, under a reading that survives the strain test, so that the `gap` on sign closes from the figures alone.
2. A per-slot ground measurement with a valid control shows that average cost fell and price, wage, variety and innovation did not move against an anchor independent of the merging parties' influence, with the counterfactual specified before the event.
3. Competition is shown not to be the anchor for the efficiency question, because price and wage readings remain discriminating after the firm acquires power (Fig 2 :36).
4. A typed bond in Fig 12 admits "two independent firms become one" as a composition of held atoms, which would reopen the structural claim withdrawn in `06_isomers_and_order.md` Q1.
5. The four slots are shown to reduce to one quantity under a transformation that preserves each per-slot verdict, so aggregation is lossless.
6. A declared welfare standard is derived from the figures without being declared, so F6's convention claim fails.

If any fires, the relevant finding is withdrawn and the census is rerun. Until one is run, no part of this is labelled `grounded`.

## 6. Recommendation

1. Do not answer the either/or as put. Split it into the four slots and run one loop per slot (Fig 9 :54, Fig 1 :19).
2. Lead with the anchor test, not with a position on mergers. Ask what would count as a failure of the efficiency claim (Fig 10 :52 "Bring a characterised residual, never the source.").
3. Treat party-projected efficiencies as self-scoring until verified against a ground the parties cannot move (Fig 1 :27; DOJ verifiability requirement).
4. Treat concentration metrics as filters for the budget only (Fig 9 :60).
5. Keep the welfare-standard choice visible as a declared convention and declare the stake of whoever applies it (Fig 10 :18, :59).
6. Send the generator, the shape claim and the refuter, not a recording of a conclusion (Fig 4 :25 "A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion.").

## 8. Strains and re-census, all visible

- **Fig 13 versus organizational composition.** Strain dissolved by re-census: different objects. Fig 13 governs substrate kinds; D1 and D2 govern frames and markets.
- **Fig 12 isomerism versus change in actor count.** Strain resolved by withdrawal of the direct reading and retention of the narrowed two-design analogue.
- **Fig 12 two currencies versus the Williamson tradeoff.** Strain named, not hidden. Bits and actions do not add; dollars and dollars do. The figure is silent on a single-currency tradeoff, so it is not cited for a verdict here.
- **Fig 2 anchor versus real markets.** A standing objection: real prices are always partly determined by firms, so no market is a perfect anchor. Re-census: the question is constitutively about market outcomes (Fig 2 :36), and the anchor is not the observed price but the discriminating benchmark that the firm cannot move at will (Fig 2 :37-38, "It is only an instrument for is this code correct, and instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure."). The degree of movement is the thing to measure, not a reason to abandon the test.
- **World conflict.** Bhattacharya, Illanes and coauthors (near-zero average, large heterogeneity) versus the older retrospective literature (price increases found over half the time, per the FTC hospital volume). Returned to the census as a union-reporting problem, not used to flip a verdict (F9).

## 9. Verdict summary

The shape survives. The question is a union of four slot-indexed anchor tests, not one scalar. The efficiency projection from the merging parties is self-scoring and is not a witness until verified against a ground the parties cannot move. Market power is structurally the failure of that ground, on the output side and the input side alike. Concentration metrics are budget filters, never verdicts. The welfare standard is a declared convention whose keeper must declare the stake. The empirical sign and the frequency of "usually" are a `gap`, reported as a verdict, unreached at this budget rather than unreachable in principle, with six pre-registered refuters in section 5. Verdicts per domain are kept separate and never averaged. Nothing here is `grounded`.
