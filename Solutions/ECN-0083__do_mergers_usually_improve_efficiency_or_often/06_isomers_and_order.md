# Structure, bond type, order and analogues for the merger question

**Mode: `specified` on the structure claims, `general` on the figure rules. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading. See `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7.**

## RESIDUAL

The question treats "merger" as one operation with one sign. Two structural questions are owed before any verdict: (1) is a merger a bond under Fig 12's typed operator set, and (2) can two merger configurations be isomers, so that "same ingredients, different arrangement" applies? A third question is the dependency order of the checking chain, and a fourth is whether the two currencies in a welfare tradeoff can be added.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the economics frame: two firms with assets, plants, management, customer relationships, supplier contracts and workers. Bonds available under Fig 12: `+`, `∥`, `≡`, `→`, `⇒`, `−`, `⋛`, plus the unary `¬`. No seventh bond is available. Anchors: a cost counterfactual for S1, competitive price and wage readings for S2 and S4, entry and innovation measures for S3. Currency: cost, price, wage, quantity, variety, time to entry. Scale: firm, market, welfare convention.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Is "two firms become one" a Fig 12 bond? WITHDRAWN

Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."

Run the six identification tests (Fig 12 :59 to :89):

| candidate | test | result |
|---|---|---|
| `+` conjunction | swap the operands, does meaning change? | No, but the result is one firm, not both holding; the two firms do not both remain as separate operands |
| `∥` disjunction | remove one, does it still work? | The merged entity may need both asset sets; not robust in general |
| `≡` identity | substitute one for the other, is anything lost? | Yes, the firms are not the same referent |
| `→` sequence | swap them, does it become a different thing? | Order of acquisition can matter, but the bond describes the relation between events, not between the firms as co-present operands |
| `⇒` production | is there a B before A fires? | The merged firm did not exist before; this is the closest fit for the *event*, but it produces a new entity rather than joining two held atoms into one term |
| `−` subtraction | is the second operand's absence the point? | The disappearance of one decision-maker is consequential, but it is not the point of the transaction |
| `⋛` comparison | is it about which is larger? | No |

No test identifies a clean bond. More importantly, a merger changes the actor set: two independent decision-makers become one. Fig 12 compounds *held* atoms; it does not merge the atoms themselves. Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom" permits a settled firm to serve as an atom at the next level, but it does not fuse two atoms into one.

**My earlier reading that a merger is a within-kind bond under Fig 13 was wrong.** Re-census: firms are frames, not substrate states or structures; Fig 13 is silent on organizations. The direct bond reading is withdrawn and recorded here rather than erased.

**Falsifier, written before the conclusion:** if a typed relation in Fig 12 is shown to admit the reduction of two operand-firms to one operand-firm with the bond carrying the change in decision rights, Q1 is reopened. Until then, "merger" is an imported operation, not a figure bond.

### Q2: Are two merger designs isomers? The narrowed analogue survives

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

The direct before-and-after reading fails: the atom set changes from two firms to one. But a real isomer claim is available one level up. Take the same two asset sets, A and B, each settled as an atom after the transaction, and compare two designs:

- **Design 1, horizontal integration of overlapping operations.** A and B are joined under a symmetric co-presence reading, with shared plants consolidated.
- **Design 2, vertical or complementary integration.** A and B are joined under a productive or sequential reading, with distinct assets retained.

Same atoms (the two settled asset sets), different bond arrangement, different behaviour (different price, wage and innovation residuals). This is Fig 12 :117 applied to configurations, not to the merger event itself. Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." Both designs belong to the functional group "combination under common control"; the bond qualifier decides which substance.

**Falsifier:** if the two designs can only be realized with different asset sets, so that the atom sets are not the same, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. This is checkable from transaction records, not from the figures.

**Mode:** `specified`, ungrounded until the design comparison is measured. **Status:** single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12), with the falsifier making it testable.

### Q3: Dependency order of the checking chain

Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step"
Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

The chain for this question:

1. **perception.** Can the instrument tell a cost reduction from a power-induced price movement? This is the contested link. The FTC retrospective program's reliance on difference-in-differences against unaffected markets is an attempt to install it.
2. **vocabulary.** Terms for what a merger affords in each slot: cost saving, market share, diversion ratio, wage markdown, entry barrier.
3. **objective.** What counts as progress: a per-slot change against an anchor, not an increase in the number of studies or the size of the HHI.
4. **planning.** Design the before-and-after study with a valid control and a pre-registered refuter.
5. **learn and carry.** Methods cross up (Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."), so a validated retrospective design travels to the next case; a particular result does not.

Most published effort in this literature is at links 4 and 5. Link 1 has instruments (retrospectives, diversion estimates), but the literature itself records that they are short-run, price-focused and selected on the enforcement margin. That is a link with instruments of limited reach, not a link that works perfectly, and not a link nobody has looked at. The honest reading is between the two: effort spent at link 4 cannot rescue a weak link 1, so more retrospectives of the same design do not by themselves close the `gap`.

**Falsifier:** a retrospective design that reliably separates cost from power in a named class of markets with a stated control would move link 1 from contested to working and close the empirical `gap` for that class.

### Q4: Two currencies: attempted and withdrawn

Fig 12 :125 "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."
Fig 12 :126 "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."
Fig 12 :130 "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."

I initially tried to read Williamson's welfare tradeoff as an instance of this: cost savings versus deadweight loss as two incommensurable currencies. That is wrong. Williamson's two terms are both in money; the tradeoff is deliberately a single-currency calculation. Fig 12's line prices the *description* in bits against the *actions* needed to settle it, which is a different comparison entirely. Applying it to dollars would be a strain, and a strain means the wrong quantity.

The reading is withdrawn. What survives is a narrower use: the cost of *describing* a claimed efficiency (a projection, cheap, self-scored) and the cost of *settling* it (a retrospective with a control, expensive, ground-facing) are in different units and do not add. A literature that counts many projections as if they were settlements is counting the denominator, exactly the failure named in `THE_FORMULA.md` step 8: "a library that grows is not a reach that grows". This narrower use is about research practice, not about the welfare numbers, and is stated as `general`.

**Falsifier:** a single measure that prices both the description of a claim and the actions needed to settle it, with a declared exchange rate that preserves both rankings, would dissolve the distinction.

### Q5: Order and negation in the verdict

Fig 5 :53 "refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so". The term held by the question is "a single usually verdict is available". What is owed first is a refusal of that term, not an explanation of which direction it points. Formula step 2 routes REFUTED before MINT. The negation operator `¬` is unary (Operators table :70-72), so refusing the scalar verdict does not require finding a substitute bond; it removes the standing of the term "usually" to be composed over, and leaves the four slots standing.

## CONCLUSION

A merger is not a typed bond in Fig 12's set, and the attempt to make it one is withdrawn with the reason preserved. A genuine but narrowed isomer claim is available between two settled designs over the same asset sets, with a falsifier. The checking chain is ordered by dependency, and its weak link is the separation of cost from power, not the volume of studies. The two-currencies line does not govern the Williamson tradeoff; that attempted reading is withdrawn, and only the narrower research-practice use survives. Refusal precedes mint, so the scalar "usually" is refused before any direction is asserted.
