# Expert verification packet: the Tether on merger efficiency and bargaining power

**What this is:** a claim in a form an industrial organization economist, competition lawyer or antitrust economist can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `general` on the shape, `specified` on each per-domain finding, `gap` on the empirical sign and frequency, not `grounded`.** This packet is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading. It cannot settle the question it reports on.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

"Do mergers usually improve efficiency, or often reduce competition and bargaining power" is not one question with two opposite answers. It is a union of at least four slot-indexed comparisons (cost and output, price and variety, innovation and entry, input-side terms including wages), each requiring an anchor the merged firm cannot move. An efficiency projection from the merging parties scores itself and is not a witness until verified against a ground the parties cannot influence. Where a merger confers power over price or wages, the readings that were serving as the measure begin to report the firm's influence rather than its cost, which is an anchor that has become a frame. Concentration metrics are budget filters; the choice of consumer versus total welfare is a declared convention. The empirical sign and frequency are a `gap` with pre-registered refuters.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1: framework-internal plus field-facing; checkable by reading Figure 1 and the 2023 Merger Guidelines

- **Statement:** a cost saving projected by the merging parties is a frame-internal quantity and cannot settle the efficiency slot until it is verified against a ground the parties cannot move.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 1 :27 "They are frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces." Fig 1 :23 "The ground enters as the only metric."
- **Field check:** the DOJ and FTC 2023 Guidelines, section 3.3, require cognizable efficiencies to be "verifiable, and have been verified, using reliable methodology and evidence not dependent on the subjective predictions of the merging parties or their agents", and state that "efficiencies projected by the merging firms often are not realized."
- **Refuter:** a case in which the projecting frame cannot in principle influence the measurement, so the projection is not self-scoring; or a figure line that permits a frame to score itself under a declared code; or a verified retrospective showing the projection was accurate against an independent counterfactual.

### C2: framework-internal; checkable by reading Figure 2

- **Statement:** competition is the anchor for the efficiency question, and market power moves the anchor. A merger that gives a firm power over price or wages degrades the instrument that would otherwise discriminate cost from influence.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :29 "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks. A measurement, an experiment, a counter, a market, a patient outcome. If it moves when"; Fig 2 :33 "The anchor must not update."; Fig 2 :34 "If it does, panel 3 turns into panel 1: the anchor becomes another frame, everything drifts together, and the drift is coherent, which is why nobody notices."
- **Field check (low overlap, corroboration only):** Goodhart's law, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Note that this is a measurement-theory pattern, not a figure line and not an economics witness.
- **Refuter:** show that under the merger the price and wage readings still discriminate cost independent of the firm's strategic choice; or show that competition is not the anchor because the question is not constitutively about a market outcome (Fig 2 :36 "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it.").

### C3: framework-internal; checkable by reading Figures 9 and 1

- **Statement:** the aggregate "usually" is an averaging error over a union of overlapping conditions, so no scalar verdict is admissible without a defined population and per-slot results.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." Fig 1 :22 "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :60 "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."
- **Field check:** Bhattacharya, Illanes and coauthors (NBER WP 31123, 2025) report average price effects of completed CPG mergers between minus 0.6 and plus 1.0 percent with a standard deviation of 4.0 to 7.5 percentage points and quartiles of minus 2.1 and plus 3.7 percent. A single signed "usually" discards that distribution.
- **Refuter:** show that the four slots reduce to one quantity under a transformation preserving each per-slot verdict, or produce a population-defined census of mergers for which one scalar predictor predicts every slot.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not that all mergers reduce competition, and not that efficiency savings are never real.
- Not that a concentration threshold or a market-share figure decides a case. These are filters for the budget.
- Not that consumer welfare is the right standard, or that total welfare is. Fig 10 :18 "a declared meaning is not a grounded one"; the choice is a convention and is declared as such.
- Not a policy recommendation to block or clear any transaction.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement here settles any per-slot sign.
- Not that the empirical frequency is unknowable. It is unreached at this budget; Fig 8 :39 "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."

## 5. What would settle it

1. **Threshold test (C2):** after the merger, do price and wage readings still separate cost from influence? If not, the anchor has moved and those readings cannot settle C1 either.
2. **Per-slot ground:** a before-and-after measurement with a control specified before the event, for each of the four slots, with the counterfactual independent of the merging parties' projections.
3. **Long-horizon evidence:** entry, innovation and non-price attributes, which the FTC retrospective program notes are currently under-covered relative to short-run price.
4. **Standing structural refuters:** six are written before the conclusion in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 5, repeated in `07_the_tether_answer.md` section 4 and in `09_watson_answer.md`.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 1 :19, :22, :27 and Fig 2 :29-30, :33-34, :36, four short passages.
2. Check C1 against the 2023 Guidelines section 3.3. Is the verifiability requirement operationally the same disqualification?
3. Check C2 against one market you know. After a close-rival merger, did the price or wage reading still discriminate cost, or did it start reflecting the merged firm's influence?
4. Check C3 against one retrospective you know. Was the studied population defined by an ex ante rule or by enforcement interest?
5. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
