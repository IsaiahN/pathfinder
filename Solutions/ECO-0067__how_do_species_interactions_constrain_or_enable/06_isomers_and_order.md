## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the two regimes isomers?

Fig 12 :117, "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 12 :123, "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two regimes are isomers **only under a fixed-atom condition**:

- **Regime C (constraint read).** Focal slot, predator, defence trait, season, the relation `predator → foraging-efficiency`, a threshold `⋛` against the current reach.
- **Regime E (enablement read).** The same focal slot, the same predator, the same season, and the relation `predator → defence-trait`, with the same threshold `⋛` against a different residual.

Hold the atom set `{focal, predator, defence trait, foraging trait, season, current reach, new binding}` fixed and change only which trait is ranged against which residual. The two regimes then satisfy Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different substance. They are **isomers**. The difference is decisive in behaviour (foraging collapses in one, defence rises in the other) and invisible in a formula that records only the interaction type "predation."

If the atom set is **not** fixed, for example if the enabling regime requires a third partner, a different abiotic condition or a dispersal stage absent from the constraining regime, the relation is composition plus import, not isomerism. Fig 13 :115, "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."

**World check.** Thompson 2010 reports that an insect "functioning both as a pollinator and a floral parasite can be strongly mutualistic in some habitats but commensal or antagonistic in neighbouring habitats." Holland and Bronstein 2008 say interaction outcomes "vary along a continuum." Those are the same actor pair under different arrangements, which is the isomer claim in field language. They do not add a new atom to the pair; they change what is ranged and what is measured. Triangulated for the shape; unmeasured for this question's specific pair.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one regime can be written only with an atom the other does not require, the isomer claim fails and the compound-plus-import reading stands. This is a live possibility for hotspot versus coldspot comparisons, where the hotspot may need a third party the coldspot lacks.

### Q2 — What is the order?

Fig 9 :27-28, "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit." Fig 8 :53, "Residual first, frame second." Fig 11 :43, "The habitat is enumerated, never composed."

The dependency order (Fig 3 :20, "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance") is:

1. **Name the focal slot and the trait.** Without a slot there is no residual, only a topic.
2. **State the residual in effect terms:** prediction versus observation at that slot. Fig 1 :19, "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."
3. **Enumerate the habitat outward by cascade,** including second and third order (Fig 11 :52-53). Do not compose a list from interaction categories.
4. **Refuse before mint.** Formula step 2: a term held that predicts and whose residual persists and does not trend down is the candidate for refusal, not for a new explanation. If "predation constrains defence" has been held and keeps failing, the refusal is owed before a new mechanism is minted.
5. **Decide reach versus import** for each slot. Fig 6 :21, "MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom." Fig 6 :23, "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."
6. **Settle the first-order claim before composing a second-order one.** Fig 12 :104, "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." A community-level statement about "the effect of interactions" is second order and is legal only after the per-slot molecules have settled.
7. **Measure R_T on the way back down** (Fig 4 :30). A shorthand that survives the trip from field to figure and back without loss has not been checked; one that drops the per-slot indexing, the import provenance and the response discriminator has a nonzero gap and must say so.

Effort spent above step 5 cannot rescue a break at step 3. Fig 3 :19, "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." A meta-analysis of interaction effect sizes is step 6 work; it cannot repair a missing habitat enumeration at step 3.

### Q3 — Cycle and catalyst structure

Fig 12 :121, "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Predator and prey, host and parasite, and competitor pairs are cycles in effect terms: the output of one turn becomes the input condition of the next, and the sign (escalation, attenuation, oscillation) decides whether the loop closes.

Fig 12 :119, "catalyst. A part that survives its own use." A pollinator that persists across seasons, a seed disperser, a mycorrhizal partner, or a niche-constructing organism that leaves a modified environment is a catalyst in the figure's sense. A spent partner (a single-season consumer that depletes the resource) behaves differently across uses. Most interaction vocabularies record the actor but not which of these it is, so "the same pollinator" can hide two different structures.

Fig 13 :88, "The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no two passes begin from the same place." This is why a cycle is not a replay. The second generation begins from a remainder the first left.

**World check.** Nowak 2006 derives the repeated-encounter condition `w > c/b` for direct reciprocity and `b/c > k` for network reciprocity. Those are cycle conditions in mathematical language. Laland et al. 2016 describe ecological inheritance, where modified selection persists beyond the constructor's lifetime. That is catalyst structure in field language. Low overlap with the ecology pool, so both corroborate the mechanism shape; neither settles a field case.

**Falsifier:** if a frequency-dependent interaction system can be represented by an acyclic chain with no loss of sign or closure behaviour, Fig 12 :121 does not govern it.

### Q4 — The two currencies

Fig 12 :125, "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." Fig 12 :126, "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."

A compact separator ("predator enables defence by opening reach") is cheap in bits and expensive in actions: it takes seasons of selection measurement to settle. A long individual-based model is expensive in bits and may be settled by one well-placed exclusion cage. Fig 12 :128-129 says the two readings come apart in both directions, and Fig 12 :130: "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."

This gives the order a budget filter. Effect sizes and literature counts decide how many field seasons to spend, not which claim is true. Fig 9 :60, "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."

**Falsifier:** a common currency that prices description and settling actions without loss, demonstrated on a case where the two currently rank candidates in opposite orders, would break this part of the derivation.

### Q5 — Why the union is unbundled before it is searched

Fig 9 :55, "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :57, "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either." Fig 9 :58, "It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

The three overlapping conditions are contact, slot-indexed residual, and current reach. A single lever that only changes contact (introduce a partner) leaves the intersections standing where the residual is not indexed or the atom is not reachable. A single lever that only improves the focal species in isolation changes nothing (Fig 11 :40, "A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves").

Management has an additional asymmetry. Fig 11 :61, "The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not." A restoration that introduces a mutualist can add contact; it cannot subtract the habitat it lands in. And Fig 11 :66, "Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another." A common-garden test substitutes a habitat and inherits actors it did not choose.

## CONCLUSION

The two regimes are isomers over a fixed atom set, same actors and relations arranged differently against different residuals. Where the atom set changes, they are a compound plus an import, and the import needs provenance and a shadow test. The order is: slot, residual, habitat enumeration, refusal before mint, reach versus import per slot, settle first-order before composing second-order, then measure the round-trip gap. Cycle and catalyst are the two structures that a flat interaction list cannot write. Description cost and settling cost are two currencies and must be held apart.

What survives is the separator, not a named partner or a net percentage. Fig 9 :32, "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself." Fig 13 :134, "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence."
