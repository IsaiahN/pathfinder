# Structure and order: cycle versus chain, union versus partition

**Mode: `specified` on the structural ordering, `general` on the bond-level distinction, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The question's "A or B" conceals two structural questions the main audit only named: (1) are selection and reproduction the same kind of composition, so that one threshold could distinguish them, or different bonded structures? (2) does the boundary question order a chain of dependencies, or does it bundle a union of overlapping conditions? This file derives those two points and states a falsifier before concluding.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are "selection" and "reproduction" the same bond?

No. The Operators table separates two ordered bonds:

- :31 sequence, `→`: "A then B; B needs A to have happened."
- :37 production, `⇒`: "A produces B; B did not exist before." And :40: "different from sequence: the second operand is not waiting, it is absent until the first fires."

Selection is production-like: variants are produced that did not exist before, and the ground keeps some. Reproduction is sequence-like plus cycle: an existing loop re-enters as input. Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." So the two options in the question are not two values of one variable; they are different bond types over different operands. Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance." A single "first life" threshold would have to record which bond holds, and a flat formula records only ingredients.

**Structural result:** the A-or-B question is a type error before it is a historical question. That is the bond-level reason for F1's union reading in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`.

### Q2 — Are the two options isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

- As the question writes them, selection and reproduction do **not** share the same atom set. Selection is a population-level relation over time; reproduction is an individual-level closed loop. They are therefore not isomers under Fig 12's definition; they are two different structures, one of which (the loop) is made of material the other supplies.
- The stronger, testable version: if both are written over the shared substrate set {space, time, energy, gradient, network, spectrum}, differing only in which structure mediates which crossing, then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth taking to a chemist, because it can be refused.
- The weaker relation is Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." A catalytic, self-maintaining substructure is the functional group common to both chemical selection and biological reproduction. Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use."

### Q3 — What is the order: chain or union?

Both, at different ranges, and conflating them is the second structural error.

**Chain.** Fig 3 :19-20: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not. The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Within the process, dependency runs: variation and differential persistence first, closure of a reproductive cycle second, a definable individual third. Fig 11 :39: "So the answerable question is not what intelligence is, but what makes a system capable. And capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone." Capability is a property of system and habitat, so it cannot be the first link.

**Union.** Across the question as asked, the conditions overlap. Fig 9 :54-55: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again. A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :57-58: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either; it stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

So: the *process* is a dependency chain; the *question* is a union of a chain question, a capability question and a convention question. A single lever placed at reproduction clears only the region where the capability condition alone holds and leaves the selection condition standing, which is Fig 9 :55-56 exactly.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if it can be shown that differential persistence and reproductive closure are a partition, that is, that no variant can persist differentially before a bounded individual closes a loop, then the union reading fails and the chain alone governs. The claim would be withdrawn.

### Q4 — What crosses between the chemical and organismal scales?

Fig 10 :68-69: "Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between. That is why there is no top rung and no regress: a generator crosses, an instance does not." Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."

What crosses from chemical selection to biological reproduction is not a recording of the first replicator; it is a generator, the capacity to close a self-maintaining loop under differential persistence. The level it lands on remakes it. Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." So a settled replicating system becomes an atom for the organismal level, and the boundary drawn at either level is level-relative, not a single fact about the world.

Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance." A metabolism-first route and a replication-first route can compose the same functional outcome, which is why a boundary defined by route is unstable and a boundary defined by shape is not.

## FALSIFIER (standing, one paragraph)

The structural claim fails if any of the following holds: (i) selection and reproduction are shown to use one bond type over one atom set, so Fig 12 :117's isomer test is inapplicable and the type-error finding collapses; (ii) the process is shown to be a union rather than a dependency chain, so reproduction does not consume selection and Fig 3 :19-20 is misapplied; (iii) a settled chemical replicator is shown not to become an atom at the organismal level, breaking the level-relativity of Q4. Any of these is a ground-visible result, not an argument, and would be recorded in `11_audit.md` with provenance before the conclusion were revised.

## CONCLUSION

Selection and reproduction are **different bonded structures**, not two values of one threshold: selection is production-like and population-level, reproduction is a closed cycle and individual-level. They are not isomers as the question writes them, but they become a real isomer claim over the shared substrate set, with a stated falsifier. The *process* is a dependency chain with selection upstream; the *question* is a union of three questions that must be unbundled before search. The crossing between scales carries a generator, not a recording, and every boundary drawn there is level-relative. This rescues what is defensible in both A and B without dating a first life.
