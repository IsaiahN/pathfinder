# Supporting derivation: structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6). Every conclusion below is a constraint on the form of an answer, not an empirical claim about any industry.

## RESIDUAL

The primary audit leaves one question open for this file: if the cost-side and price-side mechanisms are a compound, what *structure* joins them, in what *order*, and under what *analogue*? Specifically: are they isomers over a shared atom set, a cycle, or a functional group? The answer determines what a refuter would have to look for.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: a firm, its cost function, its price, its market share, entrants, and a measurement instrument. Bonds held: `+` conjunction, `→` sequence, `⇒` production, `−` subtraction, `⋛` comparison, plus the unary negation `¬` (Operators table). No new primitive is admitted. The two mechanisms are treated as molecules over these atoms, not as new atoms.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are the two mechanisms isomers, or members of a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Write the cost-side molecule as `cost function + output → lower unit cost` and the price-side molecule as `market share + entry barrier → price above marginal cost`. Over the full shared atom set `{firm, cost function, price, market share, entrants, instrument}` they use the same atoms with different bonds and different outcomes. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers** at that level.
- But as the question actually frames them they share only "firm" and "output"; the stronger isomer claim needs the full shared set to be explicit. The weaker, safer reading is the **functional group**: both are `firm → condition → market outcome`, and the qualifier says which member. Fig 12 :123: *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- **Which is testable:** ask whether either mechanism can be written without an atom the other requires. If neither can, the isomer claim stands; if one can, drop to the functional group. This is the falsifier, written before the run.

### Q2: What is the order: chain or cycle?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- `→` is sequence (*"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, Fig 12 :30), and a chain cannot close. The two-mechanism story in the question is a chain: either scale comes first or power comes first.
- The IO sources describe a loop. A cost advantage lowers the scale an entrant needs to be viable, which raises incumbent share, which deepens the cost advantage. A markup funds foreclosure, which protects the markup. The sign of each loop decides whether it closes. Fig 12 :121 governs this, not Fig 12 :30.
- Consequence for the lever in Fig 9 :55-56: a chain reading predicts that cutting the first link clears everything downstream. A cycle reading predicts the intersection survives because the loop re-enters. The figure's single-lever rule and the cycle rule agree; the question's "or" agrees with neither.
- **Falsifier:** measure both loops in one market and show one is flat while the other closes. That breaks the compound's cycle claim for that market and lets a single lever work there.

### Q3: Does size change the rule or the cast? (re-census of a strain)

- Fig 4 :19-20: **"The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked, whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."**
- An earlier reading of this file treated Fig 4 as denying economies of scale. That was a strain, not a conflict. **Re-census:** Fig 4 governs the loop and the rule for naming what worked. The economics of scale (indivisible plant, fixed cost spread over more units, bulk purchasing, division of labour, learning) governs the actors, the currency and the anchor. Different objects. Both are retained.
- Fig 11 :39-40: **"So the answerable question is not what intelligence is, but what makes a system capable. And capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."** A large firm's advantage is a property of firm-and-habitat (demand, input prices, technology, entry conditions), never of the firm alone.
- Fig 11 :49-50: **"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries. The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."** Size is such an actor: beneficial to the cost residual, harmful to the competition residual.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a firm whose production loop and naming rule change solely because it is larger, with actors, currency and anchor held fixed. That would break Fig 4's invariance for this domain.

### Q4: Which bond joins the two mechanisms?

- Operators table: `⋛` is *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, with the test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."* It is an **ordered** bond, not a symmetric join.
- `+` (conjunction) would say both hold at once but records no ordering and no direction. The question's word "or" is not any of the eight operators; it is a natural-language disjunction that the compound reading has to unbundle before a bond can be assigned.
- Under the figures the appropriate joining is `⋛` only where a threshold is actually at issue (for example, where cost subaddibility crosses the relevant demand), and a cycle elsewhere. Claiming `⋛` for the whole relationship would be a strained reading, so it is not used that way here. Fig 12 :90: *"A relation that fails all six is not a bond you are missing. It is a claim that has not been made yet."*
- **Falsifier:** show that the relationship between cost advantage and entry deterrence passes none of the seven bond tests and is not a cycle either. Then the compound has no licensed internal structure and F4 must be withdrawn.

### Q5: The two prices of the answer

- Fig 12 :125-126: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add. Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- The compound shape is cheap to describe in bits: two conditions and an intersection. It is expensive to settle in actions: a cost-function study and a markup study on the same market, with a discriminator that separates them. A solver who counts only description cost will propose an arrangement it cannot afford to test.
- Fig 12 :128-129: *"A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."* Here the short arrangement over a costly operand is exactly the case: cheap to say, expensive to check.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a market where a single published statistic settles both mechanisms at negligible action cost. Then the two-currency warning does not apply to this question.

## CONCLUSION

The two mechanisms are best read as **isomers over a shared atom set** at the strongest level, and as members of one **functional group** at the level the question actually states. Their order is a **cycle with a sign**, not a chain, which is why a single lever leaves the intersection standing. Size changes the cast, not the kernel, and its valence is relative to the residual. The joining bond is `⋛` only where a threshold is genuinely at issue; elsewhere the structure is cyclic. The shape is cheap to state and expensive to settle, so the budget must be counted in actions as well as bits.

Each claim carries a falsifier written before the run. No new primitive was admitted and no import was claimed, so Fig 6's source gate is not invoked.
