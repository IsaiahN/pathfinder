## 0. The question under audit, stated as an effect

> Can managing one ecosystem service undermine other ecological functions that are less visible or slower to change?

Restated as an effect: a manager acts on a habitat to raise the supply of one service S (the visible, fast, measurable one). The question is whether that action changes the contact structure so that other functions F (diffuse, slow, unmeasured) degrade. Two options and what each leads to:

- **Option A, single-lever management is safe by default.** It leads to a world where the measured service metric is a sufficient statistic for habitat health. That requires the metric to carry every live slot of the residual, which Fig 1 denies.
- **Option B, single-lever management can undermine unmonitored functions.** It leads to a census of contact outward from the lever, separate residuals per function, and permanent monitoring of slow variables. This is the option the figures derive.

**What an answer would look like, bound as an ordinary term (formula step "Direction").** The sought shape is written down before the run so it can accrue a residual like any other term:

> `S_visible -> (cascade over unmonitored slots) -> F_slow`

The middle term is held as an unknown cascade, not a name. The claim is refusable: show a lever whose cascade reaches no other slot, or show that the residual is properly aggregated, and the shape fails. This binding is the pre-registered shape, not a conclusion smuggled in as a premise.

## 1. Residual, arity, symmetry, scale

**R (the residual).** The gap between the predicted effect of a single-service intervention and what the habitat actually does on functions that are not in the intervention's metric.

- **Arity:** many. The habitat is a set of actors, conditions and relations (Fig 11 :43-48), and R is indexed per object slot (Fig 1 :19). This is not a one-slot question with noise.
- **Symmetry:** asymmetric. The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another (Fig 11 :50), so the property of being good for the ecosystem is not carried by any actor. There is no standing list of helpful interventions.
- **Scale:** cascading. The residual that matters can be three steps out from first-order contact (Fig 11 :52). Slowly changing variables are precisely the ones a short monitoring horizon misses (Rodriguez et al. 2006, `10_source_ledger.csv`).

**Strain test applied before choosing a line:** a reading that needs service S and function F to be independent to make the trade-off story work would strain against Fig 11 :47 (relations include dependency and competition). That strain says the domain is a union, so the census below uses a union, not a partition.

## 2. Domain census

One line per domain with actors, currency and anchor. No nameable anchor means it is not a domain yet.

| domain | actors | currency | anchor | question type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D1, the managed service** | manager, instrument, target actor or process, the service metric | service output (visible, fast, priced or counted) | the measurement of the service. This is **not** an anchor: it moves when the manager pushes on it (Fig 2 :29-30). It is a frame instrument. | partition boundary: which slot is being optimised |
| **D2, the unmonitored functions** | soil formation, nutrient cycling, water regulation, pollination, carbon storage, species interactions | the functions themselves, mostly unpriced and slow | the ground: measured state of those functions over time. This is the missing anchor in D1's frame. | union with D1: overlapping conditions in one habitat |
| **D3, the habitat / field of contact** | everything in contact with R, then what contacts those, outward (Fig 11 :44); actors, conditions (light, water, temperature, season, terrain), relations (pollination, predation, competition, dependency) | contact | the ground, which does not update (Fig 11 :73) | enumerated, never composed; the loop that owns the cascade |

**Partition or union.** Union. Fig 9 :54-56: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again. A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."* A service and an ecological function are overlapping conditions of one habitat, not separate problems. This is why "manage the service and the functions will follow" fails.

**Order by dependency (Fig 3).** Fig 3 :19-20: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not. The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*

1. **Perception / vocabulary** (links 1 and 2): enumerate the habitat and its cascades. This is the link with no instrument in D1's frame (Fig 3 :25-27), and therefore the link most likely to be assumed rather than measured.
2. **Objective** (link 3): say what counts as progress, which service is being raised.
3. **Planning** (link 4): choose the lever under incomplete information.
4. **Learn and carry** (link 5): monitor slow variables and update (Fig 3 :14-15; Rodriguez et al. 2006).

Most management effort lands above the break, where the service metric already exists. That is the failure Fig 3 names, and it is the practical content of the question.

## 4. Findings

### F1 — A single lever on a union leaves the intersections standing (decisive, general)

Fig 9 :54 and :56, quoted in §3, give the structure directly. The managed service and the unmonitored function are overlapping conditions of one habitat. A lever that targets only the service condition clears the single-condition region and leaves the intersection bound by the second condition. So "raise service S" does not entail "F is fine"; it entails "F was never the target."

This is a `general` structural result. It does not name S or F. Mode: `general`, triangulated across Fig 9 and Fig 11 (low overlap: one is about search decomposition, the other about habitat contact).

### F2 — Contact cascades past first order (general, with a figure-reported case)

Fig 11 :44 and :52. Direct-contact scoping is exactly how the figure's own case failed: DDT reached the eagles three steps out. The figure cites Carson, *Silent Spring*, 1962 and notes the mechanism (biomagnification through the food chain to eggshell thinning) was established over the following decade. That lag is the "slower to change" part of the question.

**Important provenance note:** this case is figure text, so it is a primary quote, not an independent world witness. It is also a retrodiction (a case the framework describes), not a forward test this solution ran. Retraction of an earlier reading is recorded in §8.

Mode: `general` for the cascade rule; `specified` for any claim about a named chemical or taxon.

### F3 — The service metric is the wrong kind of object to certify habitat health (general)

Fig 1 :19, :13 and :22; Fig 2 :29. R is indexed per slot. A global R near zero with one live slot is a legal state. Aggregation is how the live signal disappears into an average. The service metric is a frame instrument that moves when pushed, so by Fig 2 :29 it cannot be an anchor for the question "is the habitat healthy."

This is the exact mechanism behind "less visible": the slow function is not absent from the world, it is absent from the metric. Mode: `general`, triangulated across Fig 1 (agent metric) and Fig 2 (anchor definition), low overlap.

### F4 — The less visible functions are the residual, not an oversight (general)

Fig 11 :48: *"You do not invent the list. You read it off the world, and what you cannot perceive or measure yet is the residual."* Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"* and Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."*

So "we did not measure eggshell thinning" is not evidence that eggshell thinning did not change. The honest report is that the slot is unresolved. Mode: `general`, single-source for Fig 13's wording but triangulated across Fig 11 and Fig 8.

### F5 — Valence is relative to the residual, so there is no universally safe lever (general)

Fig 11 :49-51. The same actor is beneficial for one residual and harmful for another. A fertilizer regime that raises crop yield can degrade water quality; the figure's own ecological register lists predation and competition as relations, not as good or bad kinds. World corroboration: Raudsepp-Hearne et al. 2010 found crop and pork production negatively correlated with all regulating services measured, while carbon sequestration and forest recreation had the most positive correlations with other services. That is valence relative to the residual, measured. Mode: `general` for the rule, `specified` for the empirical pattern.

### F6 — Management is asymmetric and permanent (general)

Fig 11 :61 and :63. Introduction is available, exclusion is not; contact is a fact about the world that does not consult the manager. Therefore a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state. This answers the implicit "after we fix the service, are we done?" with no. Mode: `general`, single-source (Fig 11), corroborated by the world literature on monitoring slow variables but not derived from it.

### F7 — The chain tends to break at the vocabulary link, not the metric link (general)

Fig 3 :19 and :25-27. Perception and planning have obvious failures and obvious tests. Having terms for what the situation affords has neither. In this question the service metric is a working link; the missing link is the vocabulary and instrument for the slow functions. Optimising the service link cannot rescue it. Mode: `general`, single-source for Fig 3, corroborated by Rodriguez et al. 2006's recommendation to monitor long-term evolution of slowly changing variables (an instrument prescription, not a figure line).

### F8 — World sources agree, but they are one evidence pool (corroboration only)

The four ecology sources read (`10_source_ledger.csv`) agree on the trade-off shape: Bennett et al. 2009 ("often results in substantial declines in the provision of other ecosystem services"); Raudsepp-Hearne et al. 2010 (provisioning negatively correlated with regulating and cultural services); Rodriguez et al. 2006 (trade-offs span space, time and reversibility, and slow variables need monitoring); Millennium Ecosystem Assessment 2005 (modifying one service generally changes others). They are a **single pool**: Raudsepp-Hearne cites Bennett and Rodriguez, Rodriguez is a Millennium Ecosystem Assessment scenario paper, and three share authors. Their agreement is one witness (Fig 2 :40), not four.

Two genuinely low-overlap corroborations:

- **Human cognition.** Simons and Chabris 1999, *Perception* 28(9): *"we perceive and remember only those objects and details that receive focused attention."* Predicts that a manager monitoring one service will fail to notice an unexpected change in an unmonitored function, even if it is large and spatially close. Corroborates the "less visible" half. Field: cognitive science, essentially zero overlap with ecology.
- **Institutional design.** IPBES (`ipbes.net/about`, opened 2026-11-23) frames its role as strengthening the science-policy interface for biodiversity and ecosystem services. Its existence is a world-level acknowledgement that slow, diffuse functions need an anchor that is not the market metric. Corroborates F6's governance consequence, at low overlap with the ecology pool.

## 5. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per the standing rule: *"A falsifier written before the run is a design instrument."*

The shape claim *"a single-service lever can undermine unmonitored slow functions"* is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. **Cascade bound.** A lever is demonstrated, by measurement at first, second and third order, to leave every other slot's residual unchanged. This breaks Fig 11 :44 and :52.
2. **Aggregation succeeds.** A global residual is shown to be a sufficient statistic for every slot, so a near-zero global R entails no live slot residuals. This breaks Fig 1 :19 and :22.
3. **Partition, not union.** The managed service and the other functions are shown to be disjoint conditions with no shared variables. This breaks Fig 9 :54-56.
4. **Empirical reversal.** A multi-service landscape study finds zero negative pairwise correlations among services under intensification, or finds that slow variables never move after the intervention. This would not break the `general` figure shape, but it would retract the `specified` ecological predictions in F8.

If 1, 2 or 3 fires, the figure-derived shape is withdrawn. Until a refuter is run, nothing is labelled `grounded`.

## 6. Recommendation

1. Lead with the shape, not the ecology examples. The examples are retrodictions; the shape is the derivation.
2. State the mode on every claim (§4, §9). "Can undermine" is a possibility claim established structurally, not a measurement of a named ecosystem.
3. Enumerate the habitat outward before choosing the lever (Fig 11 :44). Treat the slow functions as live slots with their own residual, not as externalities.
4. Monitor the slow variables (Rodriguez et al. 2006). Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
5. Use the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv` and ask for a refutation, not belief (Fig 2 :35, Fig 10 :42).
6. Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."* Send the derivation and the refuter, not a campus of agreement.

## 8. Findings after re-census

The shape survives. A managed service is a lever on a habitat that the manager did not author; the habitat is enumerated, not composed; the residual is indexed per slot, so a good average can hide a live, slow slot; contact cascades past first order; and a single lever on a union leaves intersections standing. The specific ecology findings survive as `specified` corroboration from a single evidence pool plus two low-overlap frames. Nothing here is `grounded`, and the question of which service in which ecosystem is being undermined is not answered by this folder.
