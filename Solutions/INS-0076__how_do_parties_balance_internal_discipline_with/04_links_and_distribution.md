## Four things in the wider literature that bear directly on this question

1. **Cohesion is not the active ingredient; dissent is.** Janis is often read as *"cohesion causes bad decisions."* The Groupthink article is more careful: a highly cohesive group avoids groupthink *"If the group encourages individual dissent and alternative strategies to problem solving."* That is exactly the framework's distinction between agreement as a convention and independence as the thing that makes a verdict mean something (Fig 2 :40-41). Use this to resist the lazy reading that discipline must be reduced to get diversity. What is required is a refusal seat, not the absence of cohesion (Fig 5, Operators :58).
2. **Breadth without a bond loses governing capacity.** The Big Tent article reports that the Five Star Movement's catch-all formula *"has limited its ability to become a mature, functional, effective and coherent contender for government."* This is the other half of the question: representation of diverse views without a convention cannot act. It corroborates D1 without overstating it as a ground reading.
3. **Unanimity and blocking are both failure modes.** The consensus literature treats apparent unanimity as a symptom of groupthink and at the same time records that a universal block *"gives an enormous advantage to anyone who supports the existing state of affairs."* A party can lose the verdict by suppressing dissent or by giving any single actor a permanent veto. The separator in `01` F5 and F6 holds both, which is why it is not a simple "more dissent is better" claim.
4. **Independence is a precondition, not a headcount.** Condorcet requires an independent p above one half, and Page's theorem subtracts predictive diversity from average error. Adding more members who read the same briefing adds no surplus (Fig 8 :48). Both are OUTWARD imports with a source gate and a shadow test; both corroborate Fig 2 :42. They do not supply the missing ratio.

Figure-grounded reasons:

- **Fig 10 :52** - *"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."* Send the separator and the refuter first. Sending the full framework before anyone has described their own party's residual sends a recording, not a generator (Fig 4 :25).
- **Fig 10 :42** - *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* A recipient who has absorbed the whole framework before giving a verdict has joined the frame being checked.
- **Fig 9 :27-28** - ask what they would expect to see if the separator were true before giving them any of the links. The figure says *"any frame you pick will seem to fit"* if you describe it afterwards.
- **Fig 7 :35** - *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict."* A receptiveness-ranked recipient list produces a convention, not corroboration.
- **Fig 2 :40** - a list selected for likely agreement is a shared evidence pool with extra names.

**Recommended order in a reply:** (1) the separator and its four refuters; (2) ask what they would count as a failure and which anchor they would use; (3) only then the links, with the note that the figures, not the summary, are the authority; (4) record their stage-1 verdict before showing any theory and never pool later agreement as corroboration.

## The two-stage offer

1. **Stage 1 before stage 2.** Fig 10 :52 and Fig 2 :45: *"Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."* The recipient checks the output without holding the framework's interior.
2. **Stage 2 is opt-in by construction.** Once a recipient has absorbed the theory their agreement stops being evidence. Stage-1 verdict is recorded first; later agreement is never pooled (Fig 2 :40).
3. **Stage 3 sends a method, which is permitted.** Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."*
4. **Stage 3 still needs a ground per party.** Formula "Direction": a frame cannot check the direction of its own question. Each user writes a refuter before running the formula and states the mode of the result.
