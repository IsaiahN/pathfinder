## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Does "shape, not a name" forbid naming a level or a mechanism? CORRECTION TO MY OWN FIRST READING

- The canonical README: *"what a missing thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)."* The subject is a missing primitive, one not held in `Γ`.
- A resolution, a grid size, and an agent rule are not primitives; they are bindings onto atoms already held or imported from the modelling frame. Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"* governs the missing instrument, not every specific value.
- ⇒ Naming a candidate level or mechanism is **not** forbidden. What is forbidden is (a) presenting it as derived where the framework returns a shape, (b) claiming it moves the wall when it adds no atom (Fig 6 :22, *"IMPORT adds an atom"*), and (c) holding it as an unbound preference that no check can see (Fig 5 :65).
- **I overstated this earlier.** The first pass said the framework forbids naming any level at all. Retracted as applied to held bindings. Triangulated (README + Fig 5 :65-66 + Fig 6 :22).

### Q2 — Are a fine and a coarse model over the same atom set isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 13 :124: **"the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."**
- Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**
- A fine model and a coarse model that describe the same system with the same entity vocabulary but different aggregation bonds (one keeps slot-level detail, the other pools slots) share the atom set and differ in arrangement. They are **isomers** in Fig 12's sense: the difference is invisible in a list of variables and decisive in behaviour (one predicts the slot-level residual, the other does not).
- The weaker reading: if the coarse model drops atoms entirely rather than rebonding them, it is not an isomer but a **subtraction** (Operators table :43, *"A with B removed or absent"*). The distinction is testable: compare the two atom sets. Equal sets with different bonds give the isomer claim; unequal sets give subtraction plus import or loss.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit a fine/coarse model pair with identical atom sets and identical behaviour on the residual that matters. The isomer claim fails, and the pair is one model wearing two spellings (Fig 12 :92, under a symmetric bond *"k operands have k! spellings and one term"*).

### Q3 — What order is the level choice?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** Level choice is not a separate top-level optimisation; it is the vocabulary/objective link (Fig 3 :25-26), which consumes perception and feeds planning.
- The broken link in this question is exactly there: a modeller has terms for what a situation affords but no instrument for what would count as progress at a given level (Fig 3 :27, *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*).
- Therefore choosing detail is **upstream** of running and analysing the model. Optimising the analysis link cannot rescue a missing objective link (Fig 3 :19, *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*). This is why "easier to analyse" cannot decide the level: it improves a link that already works.

### Q4 — What is the direction across the scale membrane?

- Fig 4 :21: **"The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a filter with a direction."**
- Fig 4 :22: **"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."**
- `T_A` (going up) throws away detail, which is what makes the result reapplicable; `T_E` (coming back) puts it into a specific situation. *"Going up throws away detail; that is what makes the result reapplicable. But it means the trip is not reversible"* (Fig 4 :28).
- So the order is a **round trip**, not a one-way refinement. A method learned at the fine level can cross up as a method; a recording of one fine-level success cannot be replayed down (Fig 4 :25). The gap `R_T` is measured on the return leg (Symbols table :77).
- Fig 13 :74-75: **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."** and **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."** A scale crossing in our modelling sense is not the same as a `state-state` bond in the six-term substrate; applying Fig 13 literally to model resolutions would be a strain. **Re-census:** Fig 4 governs the scale transform, Fig 13 governs the six-term composition rule. Different objects. Strain dissolved.

### Q5 — What kind of thing is the union of the four conditions?

- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- The four conditions of `01` form a functional group: any adequate model-selection procedure must contain them, and the specific level and mechanism are qualifiers that say which member. This is why a universal level is not a member of the group but a name for a particular instance.
- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."** Bits price the description; actions price finding out whether the arrangement holds. A coarse model can be cheap in bits and expensive to settle, or the reverse (Fig 12 :128-129). Reading only one currency is the choice made blind.
