## DERIVATION

### Q1: Union or partition?

Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."

A partition would allow one search over one indicator. A union requires one loop per cell and a separate loop for the intersections. The question as written bundles a property of the system (approach to a threshold) with a property of the forcing (noise, rate, shock). Both can be present at once. Under Fig 9 :57, "A case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound." So the intersection is its own object and cannot be cleared by the single-condition lever.

- **Conclusion:** union. Unbundle before search. `general`, triangulated with the essay's bundle rule in `05`.
- **Falsifier:** a derivation reducing the forcing conditions to the threshold condition would make it a partition and reverse this.

### Q2: Are the three tipping mechanisms isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Write each mechanism as a molecule over a shared atom set:

| mechanism | arrangement | what differs |
|---|---|---|
| B tipping (threshold approach) | state variable bonded to a control parameter that crosses a boundary | the system's own branch loses stability |
| N tipping (noise escape) | the same state variable bonded to a stochastic forcing term | the system leaves the basin without losing stability |
| R tipping (rate failure) | the same state variable bonded to a rate of parameter change | the system fails to track a stable branch |

All three share state variable, parameter and forcing atoms. They differ in which bond carries the transition. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance, and the difference is invisible in a raw time series (the "formula" of an observed jump) but decisive in what an instrument must measure. Under Fig 12 :124 they are members of one functional group, abrupt qualitative change, with the qualifier naming the mechanism.

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." Each mechanism, once settled against the ground, can serve as an atom at the next level, which is why a union of three settled mechanisms can be composed into one research programme without merging them.

- **Conclusion:** isomer claim `specified`, ungrounded; the shared functional-group claim is the weaker, safer form. Fig 12 is single-source for the definition; the physics check is world corroboration only.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if one mechanism requires an atom the others do not (for example a noise term that cannot be written over the shared state-parameter set, or a rate that is not a property of the same parameter), the isomer claim fails and the three are merely a list, not isomers. A measurement showing identical warning signatures at identical resolution for all three would also collapse the behavioural difference that Fig 12 :117 requires.

## CONCLUSION

The residual is a union, not a partition. The three named tipping mechanisms are isomers over a shared atom set, or at minimum members of one functional group, with the difference carried by the bond and invisible in the raw observation. Work is ordered by dependency, perception first and refusal before mint. The framework's shrinking test is an analogue of critical slowing down, not the same quantity. The isomer and union claims need no new framework primitive: they are bindings from the dynamical-systems frame, provenanced as such, and `specified` until a ground settles them.
