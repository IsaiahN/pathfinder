# What kind of comparison, and in what order

**Mode: `specified`.** A simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The shape in `01` says *how* to compare without forced categories: separate frames, ground, effects, conventions, declared stakes. This file answers the two structural questions that shape leaves open:

1. What **kind of bond** is the comparison itself? Is it symmetric, ordered, or a threshold?
2. Are two knowledge systems over the same world **isomers** in Figure 12's sense, and what **order** must the comparison steps take?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation are not the six physical terms of Fig 13. That application was retracted in `01` §5. The atoms here are the shared phenomena in the habitat: the same plants, seasons, bodies, materials and skies that both a scientific frame and a craft or Indigenous frame encounter. Fig 11 :43: **"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."** Bonds held: the seven plus negation; the one at issue is `+` (conjunction, both hold at once) versus `⋛` (comparison, a threshold between two quantities) versus `→` (sequence). No new primitive is introduced.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: The comparison is a conjunction, not a threshold or a sequence

Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**

- A **threshold** `⋛` compares two quantities on one scale. That is exactly the common measure the question refuses to impose. Applying ⋛ to two knowledge systems presupposes the shared metric that is in dispute.
- A **sequence** `→` says one system must come before the other. That imports the hierarchy (primitive to modern, folk to scientific) the question wants to avoid.
- A **conjunction** `+` says both rangings hold at once, each against the ground. It is symmetric: swapping the two frames changes nothing. The comparison is therefore not a new substance built out of the two systems; it is the co-presence of two independent settled rangings, plus a record of each one's provenance.

Fig 8 :45: **"Two frames together reach what neither reaches alone."** The surplus is in the *difference* of closures, not in a merged vocabulary. Fig 8 :48: **"Two frames that overlap almost completely are one frame wearing two names."**

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a comparison must order the two systems (one as the measure of the other) or must reduce them to a single scalar to produce a verdict, then the bond is not `+` and Q1 fails.

### Q2: Two knowledge systems over one habitat can be isomers

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

A scientific botany and an Indigenous plant taxonomy may classify the same flora in the same valley. Same atoms (the plants, the seasons, the uses), different bond (the arrangement principle: morphological descent versus ecological and relational role), different substance (two bodies of knowledge with different predictions and different practices). On Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**, not imperfect copies of one another. The difference is invisible in a flat list of which plants are named, which is why forcing both into one species list destroys the very thing being compared.

This is the structural warrant for the world-side observation that *"TEK does not function as a universal set of principles, but as a family of culturally situated rules and practices rooted in specific places and ecological worldviews"* (Wikipedia TEK, fetched 2026-11-23). The world source corroborates; it does not decide.

**Constraint, recorded.** Isomer status requires the *same atoms*. If one frame has access to an entity the other cannot perceive or measure at all (a microbe before microscopy, a satellite-derived drought index), the atom sets differ and they are not isomers. The claim is therefore local to a shared habitat and a shared scale, not a global claim about "science versus tradition."

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit two compared systems where one requires an atom the other does not have contact with. The isomer claim fails for that pair; the weaker reading is that they are different frames over overlapping but non-identical habitats, which is still comparable under F1 to F4 of `01` but no longer an isomer claim.

### Q3: The union must be unbundled before it is compared

Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**
Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**

The four tradition-types in the question are not four levels of one ladder and not four samples of one variable. They are overlapping conditions. Where a craft tradition and a scientific discipline both address the same material, the overlap is a compound with its own loop; it must be described separately before either frame is compared to the other. A single lever (one universal category scheme) clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds.

- **Falsifier:** one intervention clears all four regions and leaves no categorically different residue. Then the problem was a partition and the unbundling step was unnecessary.

### Q4: Dependency order: vocabulary before comparison

Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

The order is fixed by dependency, not by importance:

1. **perception:** what does each practice do, and what contacts it (Fig 11 :43);
2. **vocabulary:** what does each situation afford, in the frame's own terms;
3. **objective:** what counts as progress or settlement in that frame;
4. **comparison:** only now, range both against the ground and compare the *shape* of each ranging.

Effort spent above a break arrives at a step that cannot use it. A historian who perfects the final comparative taxonomy before naming each frame's affordances is working above the break (Fig 3 :19).

- **Falsifier:** a comparison that succeeds while step 2 is genuinely absent, measured by a ground both frames are separately corrected by.

### Q5: One analogue worth keeping: the convention as a catalyst

Figure 12 names a catalyst as a part that survives its own use (Fig 12 :119). A shared descriptive convention in a comparison has the same shape: it is used in every act of description and is not consumed by any of them. That is why it is dangerous. A part that survives its own use is easily mistaken for a neutral fixture rather than a term the historian introduced. The discipline is to stamp its origin and date (Fig 10 :62: *"A ruling carries provenance like an atom"*), so a later reader can locate it. This is an **analogue**, not a derivation: the figures govern composition, and the claim about conventions being dangerous is already established in F1 and F5 without it.

## CONCLUSION

The comparison is a symmetric conjunction of independent rangings, not a threshold and not a sequence. Two knowledge systems over a shared habitat and a shared scale can be Fig 12 isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The comparison must be unbundled before it is searched, and the steps must be ordered perception then vocabulary then objective then comparison. All four claims are `specified`, local to a shared habitat, and carry the falsifiers written above. No seventh category, no common measure, and no ordering of traditions is introduced.
