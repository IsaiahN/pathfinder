# Solution HIS-0081: figure-grounded package

**Question:** How can historians compare scientific knowledge with practical, Indigenous, religious, and craft traditions without forcing them into the same categories?

**Source:** history of science and knowledge.txt line 100.

**Section:** 9. Knowledge outside formal science.

**Mode:** `specified` on the structural shape of a category-free comparison, `general` on the anchor and independence rules. Nothing here is `grounded`: no measurement or historical case study was run. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading, so it cannot settle the question it reports on (see `05_what_shape_means.md`, and the Recursive Self-Improvement Trap on simulacra).

**Status:** done.

**Registry status:** pending (set by the registry pass, not by this folder).

## What is being decided, in effect terms

A historian faces several knowledge systems and wants to compare them. Two options fail:

| option | what it leads to |
|---|---|
| A: put every system into one shared category scheme | the distinctive content is lost; the verdict is the historian's frame talking; this is the "forcing" the question names |
| B: declare the systems incommensurable and stop comparing | silence; no checkable claim; the question goes unanswered |

The residual is the third option: what operation permits comparison while preserving the difference that makes comparison informative. The derived answer is **triangulation against a shared ground**, not translation into a shared vocabulary. Each frame is ranged separately against what the world returns; agreement in categories is treated as a convention or as one evidence pool, never as a witness. The comparison is a union of independent loops, not a single categorization (Fig 2 :45, Fig 9 :54).

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with the claim.
- No bulk-send plan.

Reason: selecting for receptiveness manufactures the correlation that disqualifies a verdict (Fig 2 :40), and a verifier who has already absorbed the framework cannot verify it (Fig 10 :42). A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
