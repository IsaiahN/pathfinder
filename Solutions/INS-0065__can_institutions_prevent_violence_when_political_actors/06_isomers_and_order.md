# Structure, order and analogues for the two-condition shape

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

`01_figure_grounded_audit.md` leaves two conditions unbundled: C1 (an assurance that does not update under pressure from a party) and C2 (a channel that keeps that assurance reachable at the actor over time). Three questions are owed:

1. Are C1 and C2 **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or two different compounds?
2. In what **order** do they compose, and does the order matter?
3. Do the **analogues** brought in from game theory, social psychology and public health pass the shadow test, or are they echoes that merely resemble the framework?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are C1 and C2 isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As written in `01`, C1's atoms are {institution, assurance, powerful party} joined by a non-updating relation; C2's atoms are {assurance, channel, actor-slot} joined by a reachability relation across time. They do **not** share the same atom set. By Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**. They are two members of the same functional group, "a condition required for compliance to remain a settled prediction", with different atoms in the slots (Fig 12 :124).
- The stronger version: if both are written over the full shared set {institution, assurance, actor-slot, powerful party, channel, time}, differing only in which relation binds which atoms, then they are isomers, same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it predicts that the two failures are substitutable in a way the weaker claim does not.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if C1 can be satisfied without any channel term, or C2 can be satisfied without any assurance term, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 :117, :124) with the political-science observation that external support is a separate, habitat-level condition, so one witness for the stronger form.

### Q2 - What order do they compose in, and does order matter?

- Fig 12 :72: **"sequence. A then B; B needs A to have happened"**, symmetric = no.
- C2 presupposes C1. You cannot maintain a channel to an assurance that never bound; a repeated signal that the institution will bend when pushed is a signal of exactly the opposite of safety. So the legal order is `C1 → C2`, and the reverse order is not a different spelling of the same term.
- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** Investing in monitoring (C2) before the assurance binds (C1) is effort spent above the break.
- A channel that degrades is not the ground decaying. Fig 10 :24: **"the ground does not decay, the channel does."** So a C2 failure is diagnostically different from a C1 failure even when both end in violence.
- Fig 12 :103: **"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."** A settled assurance becomes an atom for the next handoff: Fig 12 :104, **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."** A decade of peace is a chain of such handoffs, each one using last period's settled assurance as an operand.
- **Falsifier:** a documented case where a robust channel to an unbound assurance prevents violence across multiple handoffs, with the assurance shown not to have bound at any point. That would reverse the dependency order and break Q2. Mode `specified`, unmeasured.

### Q3 - The negation in the actor's belief

- The operative belief is not "the assurance is absent" but "the assurance will NOT hold for me". Operators :57: **"is the point that it is false, rather than absent? Yes."** Operators :58: **"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"**; Fig 12 amendment :139: **"Without a unary operator a system can write what a situation contains, what follows what, and how far along it is - and it cannot write that any of those is false."**
- So the actor's expectation is a **refusal of the institution's claim**, not a missing atom. A institution that responds by adding more assurances is composing more instances of the term it has already refused; `¬φ` is not repaired by appending another `φ`. This is the structural reason why "say it again, louder" fails.
- A vocabulary that holds only bonds can say what a situation contains and cannot say that the arrangement is wrong. Here that is the difference between publishing another guarantee and changing the guarantee so that it binds.
- **Falsifier:** a case where repeated identical assurances, with no change in their binding force, convert an actor's "will NOT protect me" belief into compliance that survives a genuine test. Under Q3 that should be impossible. If observed, the negation reading is wrong for this domain.

### Q5 - The round trip R_T

Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

1. **Sent up (x):** the verbatim figure set, chiefly Fig 2 :30, :33-34 (non-updating anchor), Fig 10 :24-26 (channel decay), Fig 9 :57 (union), Fig 11 :49-51 (valence relative to residual).
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** the shorthand used in `07_the_tether_answer.md`, "credible assurance plus reachable channel, kept separate".
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** a concrete policy claim, "this power-sharing constitution with this monitoring mission will prevent violence in this country".

**R_T is nonzero.** Three things the shorthand holds do not survive the trip back down:

- which specific atom fills the credibility slot (the framework returns a shape, not a name);
- the per-slot indexing of the residual, which a national policy claim tends to pool into an average (Fig 1 :22, Fig 10 :26);
- the source gate and shadow test for the imported guarantor, which a policy claim routinely omits (Fig 6 :12-13).

One thing survives cleanly: a binding condition and a channel condition must be assessed separately, and the binding condition comes first. That is the residue worth sending. R_T is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints; it was not measured numerically because no ground reading was taken here. Treating "the figures and the formula are the same thing" would set R_T to zero and would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names. `11_audit.md` checks this round trip independently.

## CONCLUSION

C1 and C2 are **not isomers as written**; they are members of one functional group with different atoms, and the stronger isomer claim over a shared atom set is a real but unmeasured test. Their order is `C1 → C2`, ordered by dependency rather than importance, and a settled assurance becomes an atom for the next handoff. The actor's expectation is a **negation**, a refusal of the claim rather than an absence, so appending more of the same assurance cannot repair it. All six analogues pass the shadow test with declared provenance; two are from the question's own field and are counted as one witness, not independent corroboration for the framework. The round-trip gap is nonzero and is recorded rather than assumed away.
