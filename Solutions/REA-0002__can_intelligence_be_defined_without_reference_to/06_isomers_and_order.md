## Q1 - Are the human-free definitions isomers of one another?

- Fig 12 :117 (quoted above).
- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Read the three human-free candidates over the shared atom set `{agent, environment, goal, resources}`:

| definition | atoms held | bond type |
|---|---|---|
| Legg and Hutter, "achieve goals in a wide range of environments" | agent, environment, goal | production plus ensemble weighting over environments |
| McCarthy, "computational part of the ability to achieve goals in the world" | agent, environment, goal | subtraction (`the computational part of`), then goal achievement |
| Wang, "adapt to its environment while operating with insufficient knowledge and resources" | agent, environment, resources | conjunction of adaptation and a resource limit |

- All three keep the same atoms and change the bond. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in a list of ingredients and decisive in behaviour: Legg and Hutter's measure ranks agents by a universal prior over environments; McCarthy's definition rules out physical strength and superior sense organs; Wang's definition rules out systems with sufficient knowledge and resources.
- The shared substructure `agent → environment → goal` is the **functional group** (Fig 12 :123): it says what kind (a goal-directed contact term); the qualifier says which member.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if one of the three can be derived from another by re-wording alone, with no change in what it predicts about an agent, the isomer claim fails and the three are one definition in three notations. That would be convergent derivation, which Fig 8 :50 calls "corroboration at maximum independence", not three substances.

**Status:** `specified`, single-source on the figure side (Fig 12 definition of isomer), with the three world definitions read directly as the operands. The operational difference in what each excludes is recorded above and is checkable without the framework.

## Q2 - Is the imitation game an isomer or a different bond to a different atom?

- Turing (1950): "Instead of attempting such a definition I shall replace the question by another, which is closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous words." The replacement is the imitation game, in which the interrogator decides which of two respondents is a man and which is a woman.
- Turing's definition shares `agent`, `goal` and `environment` with Q1, but adds a bond to a **reference class: human performance**. The interrogator's judgment is the instrument.
- Decoding Tacit Knowledge, opened this session: "Sounding right isn't the same as being checked against the world, which is why I call the Turing test a test of fluency, not of accuracy."
- By Fig 12 :117 the imitation game is an isomer of the human-free definitions **only if** the reference class is counted as the same atom differently bonded. It is not: it adds an atom (the human foil and the interrogator) while dropping the ensemble of environments. Same ingredients are not present, so Fig 12 :117 does not apply.
- The correct relation is Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs." Turing imported a human-reference frame into the question of machine thinking. That import moved the wall of the definition debate for decades, which is evidence that the atom was genuinely new to the closure, not a re-spelling.
- **Falsifier:** show a mapping from the human foil and interrogator to an environment-ensemble term that preserves every prediction of the imitation game. If it succeeds, the two are isomers over a shared atom set and this distinction collapses.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated across Fig 12 (isomer test) and Fig 6 (import moves the wall), low overlap. World corroboration from Turing 1950 read in full and from the framework essay, which shares the figures' author and is counted as the same witness as the figures.

## Q3 - What order does the dependency chain require?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Applied here:

1. **Link 2, vocabulary.** Decide whether "definition" means a dictionary report, a working convention, or a verdict. Wang 2019 supplies the distinction: "a dictionary definition is descriptive as it summarizes the existing usage of the term, while a working definition is prescriptive as it specifies a proposed usage of the term." Fig 10 :46: "A convention makes no claim about the world; a verdict and an atom both do."
2. **Link 3, objective.** State what would count as progress: a per-slot residual that stops registering against a non-updating anchor, not a term that accrues signatories.
3. **Link 4, planning.** Only now choose an instrument. Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." The definitional literature has no instrument for the objective in step 2, which is why it is the break.
4. **Refuse before mint.** THE_FORMULA step 2 routes a term held to be false to REFUTED, "a term held is false; what is owed / is a refusal, not an explanation". Operators table :58: "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate." The term to refuse here is "a single definition of intelligence settles the question".
5. **Mint only if the bargain pays.** Fig 5 :43: "A new term is worth having when stating it, plus what remains unexplained after it, costs less than what was unexplained before." A new definition with 70 older definitions still unexplained does not pay.

**Falsifier:** a demonstrable instrument for the objective in step 2, used by the definitional literature before any new term is minted. That would move the chain's break and change the order. Then this section is re-censused.

## Q4 - What is the analogue that makes the question workable?

Fig 11 :41: "Which is the same move as asking what generates lift rather than whether a thing is a bird. The substrate question dissolves; the contact question does not." Fig 11 :42: "The lift move is Cayley's, 1799: separating the forces from the flapping is what made powered flight a problem anyone could work on."

The analogue for this question is: stop asking what intelligence *is* (whether the bird is a bird) and ask what produces the capability (which forces generate lift). The answer then has the same grammar as the lift answer: name the conditions, name the arrangement, name the instrument that settles whether the arrangement pays. The analogue is a **shape analogue only**. It does not license importing aerodynamics into intelligence research, and it does not name the forces. Fig 9 :32: "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."

A second analogue comes from animal cognition, a genuinely low-overlap field. *What is cognition?* (Chittka 2019) warns: "Anyone who asks \"which animals are cognitive, and which are not?\"" by a single all-inclusive definition "is doomed to disappointment", and "Taking the cognitive approach entails asking questions about what information is (in some way) represented by an individual". That is the per-slot discipline of Fig 1 :19 arrived at from comparative cognition. It corroborates the shape, not the framework.

## CONCLUSION

The human-free definitions are isomers over a shared atom set of agent, environment and goal, differing in which bond they use and what each excludes. The imitation game is not an isomer of them; it imports a new atom, the human reference class, which is why it moved the debate rather than re-describing it. The dependency order is vocabulary, objective, instrument, refusal, then mint. An agent-only definition is refused before a capability definition is considered. One strain remains live across the folder: whether the universal-intelligence scalar preserves per-slot residuals (audit S3). It is named here too and is not smoothed.
