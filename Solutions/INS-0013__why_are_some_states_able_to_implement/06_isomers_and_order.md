## Q1 - Are two states with the same formal institutions isomers?

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :124)
> **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** (Fig 12 :92)

Two states with an identical list of formal institutions, agencies, laws and staffing rules have the same atoms. If their bonds differ, that is, if one composes those atoms as a repeated command sequence and the other as a reconfigurable network with frontline feedback and imported local knowledge, they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with the difference invisible in the institutional inventory. This is the figure-grounded version of the intuition that "it is not what a state has, it is how it connects what it has", but it is stronger, because it says the arrangement is itself a substance and carries information the ingredients do not.

If the states share only a pattern (both use `case → routine → outcome`) while differing in which atoms occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: they are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), the qualifier saying which member. The presence of the pattern fixes the kind, not the outcome.

The distinction is testable. An institutional inventory that finds no difference between two states cannot distinguish isomer from functional-group; the bonds have to be recorded. Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two states with identical atoms and identical bonds nevertheless diverge in implementation consistency with no difference in ground contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D2), which would move the explanation out of D3.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated for the legality of the reading (Fig 12 and Fig 11 :50, different figures), single-source for the state application. The world corroboration is the state-capacity premise that a common formal apparatus can be described, plus Scott's observation that legibility schemes *"overlay, and often supersede, local practices"*; but these are different fields, and the first is an agent-internal framing re-mapped in F9.

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20-21)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)

Order for a state facing a changed field:

1. **Perception.** Can it read what is happening in the field at all? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests.
2. **Vocabulary.** Does its Γ have terms for what the changed situation affords, and can it say what would count as progress there? This is the middle link. Fig 3 :26: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."*
3. **Objective.** Can it state a comparison that would count as getting somewhere? Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot state a goal.
4. **Planning.** A route to that objective under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next problem.

The two conditions from the primary audit map onto this chain: C1 (contact) is mostly a failure of links 1 and 2; C2 (reach) is mostly a failure of link 5, because the state names what worked in its own terms but never imports a new atom. That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear both (Fig 9 :55).

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* The field-facing link (1) has administrative data and case records; link 5 has almost none. So budget will naturally pile up at links 1 and 4 while the break sits at 2 and 5.

**Falsifier:** a state whose chain demonstrably works at 1, 2 and 3 and fails only at 4. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim of F8 in the primary audit.

## Q3 - Competing explanations, and what each can and cannot reach

| **Luck / temporary information advantage** | an unearned early reading that persists while unfuted | Fig 8 :40: *"Underivable-in-principle and unreachable-at-this-budget are different facts"* | Cannot be excluded from inside a frame; Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* Honest `gap`, not a rival verdict |

No candidate is discarded by prestige or familiarity. Each is ranged against the same residual, in the order Fig 8 :53 requires. The point is not to pick one; it is to notice that the first two are largely one evidence pool (Fig 2 :31) and that the low-overlap fields corroborate mechanism, not the state-level fact.

## Q4 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the state-capacity emphasis on hard-to-copy administrative atoms, as the Γ operand under a closure that has not met a differing closure;
- the governance-indicators emphasis on measurable quality, as an instrument that must be ranged against the per-case ground, not substituted for it (F6);
- the legibility emphasis on the cost of simplification, as F3 and Fig 12 :115;
- the street-level emphasis on frontline routines, as C1 and Fig 1 :19 at a different scale;
- the bounded-rationality emphasis on satisficing, as C2 and Fig 6 :21;
- the normal-accidents emphasis on tight coupling and cascades, as F4 and F5.

It cannot generate the *name* of the specific reform that will deliver consistency next. That is the `gap` recorded in F10 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 (*"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*) and Fig 8 :39 apply. The source essay states why: an instrument that reads a result *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists"*.

## CONCLUSION

Two states with the same atoms but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117; two states sharing only a `case → routine → outcome` pattern are members of one functional group under Fig 12 :124. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the middle link (vocabulary and objective) and the carry link the usual breaks, which is why C1 and C2 need different instruments. The standard institutional explanations are mostly bindings of one evidence pool and are generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim has a written falsifier; the order claim has one; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
