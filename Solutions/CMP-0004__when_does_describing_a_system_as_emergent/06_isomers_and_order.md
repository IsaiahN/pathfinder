# Alias, isomer, and order in macro descriptions

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two researchers describe the same system at two levels. One says "the micro rules plus the word emergent". The other says "a settled macro term with a written bond". The word "emergent" is being used to cover at least three different relations: an alias, a genuine new substance over the same atoms, and a new atom at a higher level. The question asks which relation holds, and what would discriminate them.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: whatever the domain's micro frame supplies. Bonds held: the eight operators, of which `+ ∥ ≡ → ⇒ − ⋛` join two operands and `¬` refuses one. No new primitive is available without import. Question type: union. Alias detection and isomer detection operate on different things and must be run as two checks, not averaged into one "degree of novelty".

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Alias: when is the macro term just a second name?

- Operators :24-27: identity, `≡`, is "two names, one referent" with the test "substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."
- Fig 8 :13-15: "same atoms, different names" / "their union adds nothing" / "two names for one frame".
- Fig 6 :21: "MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."
- ⇒ If substituting the macro description for the micro description loses nothing, the two names share one frame. The word "emergent" has added vocabulary, not reach (Fig 5 :72: "a library that grows is not a reach that grows").
- **Falsifier:** exhibit two descriptions over the same atoms and bonds whose union moves the reach while substitution loses nothing. That contradicts Fig 8 and the alias verdict fails.

### Q2 — Isomer: when is the macro term a different substance?

- Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :5: "The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- ⇒ Take a domain whose micro description and macro description use the **same atom set**, for example a lattice of spins and a coarse-grained magnetisation field. If the two differ only in the bond (the arrangement or the map), they are isomers in Fig 12's sense: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. The macro term then earns the word "new" without adding a primitive.
- The weaker case is a **functional group**: Fig 12 :123, "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." Two systems sharing a `micro-to-macro-to-next` structure but using different atoms are members of the same functional group, not isomers.
- **Falsifier:** if the macro description requires an atom the micro description does not contain, the isomer claim fails and the case is either an import ( owed a source gate and shadow test) or a new primitive. That distinction is checked, not assumed.

### Q3 — Order: symmetric versus ordered bonds

- Fig 12 :92: "Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term, so a code that charges for the sequence overcharges by log₂(k!) while the bond term undercharges."
- Fig 12 :111-112: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one. A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."
- ⇒ If the micro-to-macro relation is symmetric (a conjunction of conditions), the order of discovery does not matter and the macro term is a state. If it is ordered (`→` sequence, `⇒` production), the order matters and the macro term carries a route. A rename typically occurs when an ordered relation is reported as a symmetric one: "these conditions are present, so the pattern is emergent" records the ingredients and discards the route.
- **Falsifier:** show a case where swapping the order of the micro-to-macro steps changes nothing in the behaviour, yet the explanation claims to have supplied a route. That exposes a mislabelled symmetric bond.

### Q4 — Level: when does the macro term become an atom?

- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"
- Fig 5 :69: "settling is necessary for a term to be composed over, and it is not sufficient."
- Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."
- ⇒ A macro term that is settled and composable is a new atom at the next level. Its novelty is level-relative. Carrying it up and back down is lossy, so the round trip gap must be measured rather than assumed zero. A word that has not been settled has not crossed the membrane; it is still in the frame that named it.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a composition that preserves all micro detail across the crossing. That would set the round-trip gap to zero and break the level-relativity claim.

### Q5 — Refusal: the case a vocabulary of ingredients cannot write

- Operators :57-58: negation, `¬`, "this does not hold", and "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate".
- Fig 5 :53: "refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so"
- ⇒ If the residual's content is that a held macro regularity is false, minting another "emergent" label is the wrong operator. The vocabulary owes a refusal, not an explanation. This is the one case where the rename failure is categorical rather than conditional: no amount of additional naming can fix a false term.
- **Falsifier:** show a false macro regularity that becomes true by adding a further macro label. If that happens, `¬` is not needed here and this finding fails.

## CONCLUSION

The word "emergent" covers three distinguishable relations. An **alias** adds a second name to one frame and no reach (Q1). An **isomer** uses the same atoms under a different bond and can be a genuine new substance without a new primitive (Q2). A **new atom** is a settled molecule at the next level, with a measured round-trip gap (Q4). Order decides whether the macro term is a state or a route (Q3), and a false term owes a refusal, not a label (Q5). This is the structural backing for the four-condition test: C3 is the alias/isomer discriminator, C4 is the level test, and the pre-registered refuter in `01` is the refusal.

This framing rescues what is defensible in the practice of calling things emergent without granting that the word itself is a result. `THIS PATTERN IS EMERGENT` remains a binding with provenance owed. `A MACRO TERM OVER A SHARED ATOM SET, DIFFERING ONLY IN BOND, IS A GENUINE NEW SUBSTANCE` is a checkable structural claim.
