# Isomers and order in institutional flexibility

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 5).

## RESIDUAL

The ordinary term "flexible versus rigid" treats the two as opposite amounts of one quantity. Two questions are owed: (1) over a fixed institutional atom set, are flexible and rigid forms **isomers** in Fig 12's sense, that is, the same atoms under a different bond; and (2) where does the dependency chain for institutional adaptation actually break, so that a reform effort lands on a step that can use it?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the institutional case (read off the world, not composed, per Fig 11 :43): multiple decision centres; redundancy of function; access to information; capacity to learn; a conflict-resolution path; a reference that does not update on reports; a seat able to refuse. Bonds available: the seven, of which `+` (both hold), `→` (sequence) and `⋛` (comparison) do most of the work here, plus negation (a refusal, the eighth operator, not a bond). No new atom is introduced without an import. Scale: first order inside one institutional domain, second order at the interface where one rule's valence for another residual is decided (Fig 12 :104).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are flexible and rigid forms isomers?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*

Take two governance forms over the same atom set: one where each decision centre ranges separately against a shared non-updating outcome, and one where the centres range against one another's reports and converge. The ingredient list is identical: same centres, same redundancy, same information access, same dispute path. The bond differs. In the first, the relation between centre and reference is a comparison against something outside the frame; in the second, it is mutual update, which Fig 2 :13-15 calls the coherent-drift collapse. By Fig 12 :117 these are **isomers**, not different amounts of flexibility. They will behave differently at the ground while the rulebook looks the same.

The fixed-atom condition is load-bearing and is **not** claimed for real societies without check. Gatto 2022 notes that polycentric governance embeds *"simultaneous change of institutions, stakeholders, norms, and (resource) infrastructure"*, so the atom set changes in practice. Where it changes, the relation is composition plus import, a different claim with a provenance cost (Fig 6 :24). **Triangulated for the figure definition (Fig 12 plus Fig 2); single-source and live strain for the empirical fixed-atom condition.**

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two arrangements sharing every atom are shown to differ only by a scalar amount, with the same bond assignment and the same behaviour at the ground, the isomer claim fails and the simple "more or less flexible" reading stands instead.

### Q2 - Which bond is the difference?

Operators table: `+` is symmetric, *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."* `→` is ordered, *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*

The useful distinction is not how many rules can change but **which relation may be changed**. A society that can rebind which centre reports to which anchor has changed the bond; a society that can only change the numerical threshold inside an unchanged bond has changed a parameter. The first can alter the closure's reach at the second order; the second cannot add an atom (Fig 6 :21). This is why a flexibility index that counts rule changes per year is measuring the wrong object: it counts spelling, not arrangement (Fig 12 :92: *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."*).

So the separator is structural: flexibility helps adaptation where it permits a re-binding or an imported atom with provenance; it creates uncertainty where it permits the reference itself to move; it creates capture where it lets a convention decide an outcome (Fig 10 :47).

### Q3 - Where does the chain break?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* The five links are perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry. The break relevant here is between vocabulary and objective (Fig 3 :24-27):

> **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."**

For institutional flexibility this is exact. We have abundant terms for what a reform permits (discretion, devolution, sunset clauses, waivers) and very few instruments for what would count as progress at a named slot, independent of the reform's own reporting. Baldwin et al. 2024 record that polycentric governance is adaptive *"conditional on the presence of effective systems for coordination and information exchange"*, and that without them *"dispersed authority was not adaptive."* Coordination and information exchange are the vocabulary link; the objective link is still unmeasured. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

**Falsifier:** exhibit a society with a working, measured objective link at the slot in question, where the flexibility debate still fails for lack of permission. That would move the break to the planning link and require a different derivation.

### Q4 - Catalysts and cycles: which parts survive their own use, and which loops close?

Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."* An independent court, an auditor, or a monitor that can refuse a report is a catalyst in this sense: it is not consumed by deciding a case, and spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses. Behnke 2024's safeguard against authoritarian drift, and Gatto 2022's *"continuous competition and conflict resolution"*, are the world-side counterparts of the catalyst. A captured regulator is the spent part: it survives institutionally but not functionally, and nothing in the rulebook records which it is.

Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input."* Institutional feedback is a cycle, and the sign decides whether it closes. A rule that is revised in response to a measured residual is a negative feedback that can close. A rule that is revised in response to a report generated under that same rule is a positive loop that drifts, which is Fig 2 :34 again at the second order: *"everything drifts together, and the drift is coherent, which is why nobody notices."* A chain cannot close by construction; the cycle is a distinct structure, not a long sequence.

**Falsifier:** show a self-reporting feedback loop that closes to the ground without an external reading, with the anchor demonstrably not having moved, and the catalyst/cycle distinction collapses here.

### Q5 - The two currencies do not add

Fig 12 :125-126:

> **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
> **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

A flexible constitution is cheap to describe (few clauses, broad discretion) and may be extremely expensive to settle (who actually gets the licence, and was the reference moved). A rigid one is expensive to describe and may take one action to refuse. Fig 12 :127: *"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."* A flexibility reform that scores well on the description cost and is never checked on the action cost is counting the denominator. Fig 5 :42: *"The acceptance test is a bargain, not a threshold."*

**Falsifier:** a case where description cost and action cost are shown to be the same quantity in the same units for an institutional composition, so that a single figure would suffice.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed atom set, flexible and rigid governance forms are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117): same ingredients, different bond, different behaviour at the ground. The difference is structural, which bond may change, not scalar, how many rules change. The dependency chain breaks at the objective link (Fig 3 :24-27), where a society has terms for what a reform permits but no instrument for what would count as progress at the slot. Independent monitors and refusal seats are catalysts (Fig 12 :119); reporting loops are cycles whose sign decides whether they close (Fig 12 :121). Description cost and action cost are two currencies and do not add (Fig 12 :125-126).

This rescues what is defensible in the ordinary question without accepting its premise: the interesting variable is the arrangement, indexed per residual, and the instrument that would settle it. It requires no new atom, no import that moves the wall, and no claim about a numerical amount. `metric = flexibility` remains an unproven binding; `two governance forms over a fixed atom set are isomers distinguished by which bond may change` is a checkable structural claim with a stated falsifier.
