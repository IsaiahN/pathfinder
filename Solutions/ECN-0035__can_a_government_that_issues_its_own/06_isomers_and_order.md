# Union, order and analogues: supporting derivation

## RESIDUAL

The main audit refuses "fiscal capacity is one stock" and substitutes three conditions. Two questions are still owed: (1) what is the structural relation between the three conditions, union, partition or isomer; (2) in what order must they be settled, and which link actually breaks. ## Q1: Are N, R and E isomers, a union, or a partition?

- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- The three conditions are **not a partition**. They overlap: a floating-rate issuer can also carry foreign-currency debt (N and E), and can also be at full capacity (N and R).
- They are **not isomers under Fig 12 :117** in the strict sense, because they do not share the same atom set arranged differently; each has a different anchor and a different currency.
- They are members of a **functional group** in the Fig 12 :124 sense: each has the shape "a capacity compared with a claim at a slot", with a different qualifier deciding which member. The group says what kind of question this is; the anchor says which member it is.

**Result:** the correct structure is a **union of overlapping members of one functional group**, not a partition and not a strict isomer set. This matters because a partition invites one verdict per box, while a union requires checking the intersections where one lever leaves the second condition binding.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the three conditions are shown to have disjoint ground anchors (no case in which two are simultaneously binding), the union claim fails and the partition reading stands.

## Q2: What bond is "can run out"?

"Run out" is not a conjunction. It is a threshold comparison between what is available and what is claimed at a slot. Operators table :48-51 defines ⋛ as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, and Fig 12 :87 repeats it.

- For N, the comparison is nominal units available against nominal obligations due. The government can always add to the left operand, so the comparison cannot fail by scarcity of its own units. It can fail by law or institutional choice, which is why the answer is `general` structural rather than `grounded`.
- For R, the comparison is real capacity at a slot against the real command implied by spending. The left operand is finite (Fig 13 :68, *"finiteness in a finite region is what makes the counting terminate"*), so the comparison can fail. Inflation is the reading of that failure.
- For E, the comparison is foreign exchange obtainable against foreign-currency obligations due. The left operand is outside the issuer's closure (Fig 6 :23), so the comparison can fail.

Fig 12 :125-126: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."* *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."* A debt ratio is a price in bits; a capacity check at a slot is a price in actions. Summing them into one "fiscal space" number is the error the figure names, not a fix.

**Live type strain, not hidden.** ⋛ is defined over two quantities. At R the operands are real capacity and real command; at N they are nominal units and nominal obligations. Are those the same kind of quantity at the same level? The figures do not say. An instrument is owed, not an argument. This is strain S1 in `01` section 7.

**Falsifier:** show that ⋛ cannot be applied across the nominal and real levels without a type error, and the threshold reading collapses into two separate, incommensurable comparisons. That would strengthen, not weaken, the union verdict.

## Q3: What is the dependency order, and where is the break?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*

| link | question | state | figure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 nominal financing | can the government make payment in its own units? | works | Fig 6 :21 |
| 2 real command | can those units command the specific resource at the slot? | typically the break | Fig 13 :68, Fig 1 :19 |
| 3 external obligation | can obligations in another currency be met? | downstream, conditional on 2 | Fig 6 :23 |

Most effort lands above the break, on link 1, because the measurements there are abundant. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* What is missing is the vocabulary for what a particular spending proposal affords at a particular slot. Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."*

Fig 11 :40: *"An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."* Issuing more units is an improvement to the agent; it changes nothing unless it changes contact with real resources. So fiscal capacity is a property of government-and-resource-habitat, never of government alone.

**Falsifier:** show that link 1 is actually broken for a floating-rate issuer (an involuntary own-currency default) and link 2 is not. That reverses the order and breaks this derivation.

## Q4: Is the ecology analogue an isomer or just a metaphor?

The carrying-capacity article (opened 2026-11-23, low overlap) defines the concept as *"the maximum population size of a living biological species that can be sustained by that specific environment, given the food, habitat, water, and other resources available"*. It also notes that *"Carrying capacity measurements over large areas also assumes homogeneity"* and fails where resources vary by region.

- As a **shape analogue**, it is strong: a finite capacity at a slot, measured against a claim, with a per-region variation that defeats aggregates. This matches Fig 13 :68 and Fig 1 :19.
- As a **strict isomer** under Fig 12 :117, it is not: ecology and public finance do not share the same atom set. Calling it an isomer would be the apophenia Fig 9 :27-28 warns against, picking the frame and then mapping the residual onto it.
- The honest label is **functional-group analogue**: *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member"* (Fig 12 :124). The kind is "finite capacity compared with a claim at a slot"; the qualifier is "ecosystem" or "monetary economy".

**Falsifier:** show a case in which ecological carrying capacity rises without any change in resources or technology merely by redefining the population's accounting unit. That would break the analogue, because it would make the capacity a convention rather than a ground.

## CONCLUSION

The three conditions form a union of overlapping members of one functional group, not a partition and not a strict isomer set. "Run out" is a threshold comparison, ⋛, whose operand type is strained across the nominal and real levels (named, not smoothed). The dependency order is nominal financing, then real command, then external obligation; financing works and the break is at real command. The ecology analogue is a functional-group analogue, not an isomer, and is labelled as such.

All four verdicts are `specified` or `general`, none `grounded`. Falsifiers were written before the conclusions in each section.
