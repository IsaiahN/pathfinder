# Union, order and isomers: the supporting derivation for INS-0018

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question pairs two predicates about one intervention. Three sub-questions are owed before the main verdict can be trusted: (1) is this a partition or a union, (2) what is the dependency order between the checks, and (3) do different contracting arrangements with the same ingredients count as different substances, so that a single "contracting" variable is inadequate?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (recap, not a substitute for the one in `01`)

Atoms held: a service function, a public principal, a provider, users, money, a metric, an outcome, oversight. Bonds held: the seven plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. R has arity two (performance and accountability), is asymmetric (introduction available, exclusion not), and spans two scales (service delivery first order, oversight second order).

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Partition or union?

Fig 9 :54-56: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again. A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."

Fig 9 :57-58: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

Are performance and accountability separate conditions or overlapping ones? They share the same service and the same metric, but the metric can play two different roles. When the metric reports the outcome, it serves both. When the metric decides the outcome, it serves neither (Fig 10 :47). That shared-but-different role is the signature of a union, not a partition.

⇒ **Verdict: union compound, unbundle before searching.** Do not average. Do not report a net score. Two loops, two anchors, one record.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** a single scalar that predicts both the service outcome and the correctability of failures out of sample would collapse the union into one condition and refute this Q1. (Same as refuter 4 in `01`.)

### Q2: What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

The links for this question:

1. **Perceive.** Can the service outcome be read at all, per case, before and after the change? (Fig 3 :4-5)
2. **Vocabulary.** Are there terms for what the contract affords: what can be monitored, switched, sanctioned, appealed? (Fig 3 :6-8)
3. **Objective.** Can the principal say what would count as progress in its own terms, against a ground that does not move? (Fig 3 :9-11)
4. **Plan.** Is there a route under incomplete information: contestability, benchmarking, staged monitoring? (Fig 3 :12-13)
5. **Learn and carry.** Is the result named, verified, and carried to the next service? (Fig 3 :14-15)

The middle links are where this question usually breaks. Fig 3 :25-27: having terms for what a situation affords and being able to say what would count as progress "has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments. A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." A contract can be perfectly audited for process while link 3 (what counts as progress against the outcome) was never measured. That is the most common reason a well-managed contract still fails the ground.

**Order by dependency from the figures:** refuse before mint (Formula step 2); settle contact before attributing a differential outcome (D1 before D3); keep the ground reachable while judging (D2 concurrent, never derived from D1); describe the residual before picking a frame (Fig 9 :27-28).

**Falsifier:** a case where a rigorous link-3 objective, once installed, fails to improve the later links, or where a strong link-5 carried result does not change the service outcome. Then this ordering is wrong.

### Q3: Are different contracting arrangements isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."

Consider two arrangements with the same ingredient list: the same service, the same number of providers, the same budget, the same numeric target, the same audit clause.

- **Arrangement X:** several providers compete, each with its own method and its own user-facing evidence; selection is against the residual the last provider surfaced; the metric reports the outcome and cannot be substituted for it.
- **Arrangement Y:** one incumbent, self-reported metrics, target attainment is the outcome; no selection between rooms.

Same atoms, different bonds. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, not variants of one thing: the difference is invisible in a procurement spreadsheet and decisive in the service. Treating "contracting" as a single variable with a single coefficient is the flat-formula error of Fig 12 :14-17: "the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not... nothing downstream can tell them apart, and nothing signals that anything was lost."

**Falsifier:** a controlled comparison in which X and Y produce the same outcome distribution and the same correctability profile across repeated rounds. Then the bond is free, the arrangements are one term, and the isomer claim fails. This is a real empirical question, not a rhetorical point.

### Q4: Which bond actually differs?

Fig 12 :46: "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."

- Contestability is **comparison** (⋛): a threshold relation between providers against a residual, Fig 12 :86-89 and Operators table :48-50.
- A target substituted for the outcome is also a **comparison**, but aimed at the metric instead of the ground. Fig 10 :47: "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one."
- The chain from contract to service is **sequence** (→): "A then B; B needs A to have happened" (Fig 12 :30). Reversing it, measuring the service only after the reporting deadline, produces a different substance.
- A missing complaint route is **subtraction** (−): "A with B removed or absent" (Fig 12 :80-82). Its absence is the point, and nothing in the provider list records it (Fig 11 :68).

⇒ The arrangements are not merely differently staffed; they are differently bonded. That is why a single "private versus public" coefficient loses the information that matters. Fig 12 :6-7 names the failure: "collapse 6: the flat formula", "collapse 7: the free bond".

### Q5: The catalyst check on oversight bodies

Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

An oversight body that regenerates and one that is spent by its first contested case are indistinguishable in an organigram. Fig 12 :119 says nothing records which a term is. So the existence of an oversight institution is not evidence of accountability; its behaviour across repeated uses is. This is the second-order version of F5 in `01`.

**Falsifier:** an audited oversight body that is demonstrably regenerating across repeated contested cases, with a record that distinguishes it from a spent one. That would show the property is observable and the bond can be priced.

## CONCLUSION

The question is a **union compound** that must be unbundled (Q1), checked in **dependency order** with link 3 as the usual silent break (Q2), and modelled as **isomers** rather than variants, because the same ingredients under different bonds are different substances (Q3/Q4). Oversight bodies must be checked as catalysts, for use across repeated cases (Q5). Every one of these claims carries a written falsifier. None is `grounded`.
