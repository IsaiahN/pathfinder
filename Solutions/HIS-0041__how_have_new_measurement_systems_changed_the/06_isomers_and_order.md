# Isomers, catalysts, and order in a category change

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit derives that a measurement extends representation and that the resulting category change is a mint, a refusal, or a contact change. Three supporting questions remain: (1) what is the structural relation between an old category and a new one over a fixed set of atoms; (2) what role does the measuring instrument itself play across repeated uses; (3) what order do the operations come in, and does reclassification subtract or refuse. This file answers those three with a falsifier each.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the six terms (Space, Time, Energy states; Spectrum, Gradient, Network structures) plus whatever category atoms a society already has in Γ. Bonds held: the seven, including ⋛ comparison and → sequence, and the unary operator ¬. No new primitive is available without import. Actors: instruments, categories, classifiers, classified people. Currency: bits to state a category and actions to settle whether it holds; these do not add (Fig 12 :125-127). Anchor: set per domain in `01`, section 1.2. Union, not partition: the same instrument can be a catalyst in D1 and an introduced actor in D2.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the old and new categories isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two readings, and the distinction matters:

- If the old and new categories use the **same atoms** (the same observed distinctions, the same units, the same states) and differ only in how they are bonded (which threshold is applied, which structure mediates, which cut is chosen), then they are **isomers**. The difference is invisible if you record only the ingredient list of the category, and decisive in behaviour: people are sorted differently, programs are triggered differently, and time series break. This is the strong and useful claim.
- If the new category requires an atom the old one did not have (a new instrument extended what can be represented, so a new ordering or gradient is now available), then they are **not** isomers. The relation is composition plus an import, and the import needs a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 8 :50-52).

The census makes a concrete prediction: the claim is strongest where a measurement changed the *bond* and not the *atom set*, for example when the same population is recut at a different threshold or when the same physical state is ordered by a different scale. It is weakest where a genuinely new sensorium produced a new ordering, which is exactly the case Fig 6 :26 governs.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either the old or the new category can be written without an atom the other requires, the isomer claim fails and the import reading stands. If both require exactly the same atoms yet behave identically, the isomer claim is vacuous. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 :117 with Fig 13 :121 on different routes arriving at the same substance); single-source for the historical application, which needs an instrument.

### Q2 - What role does the instrument play across uses?

Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

A measuring instrument that survives its own use is a **catalyst** in the figure's sense: it is not consumed by producing the reading, and it can be applied again. This is why a category can be reproduced across many enumerations with the same instrument and the same bond. But "nothing records which a term is" is the warning: a catalyst and a spent part look the same in the record until you check. An instrument that has been calibrated into a convention and now decides an outcome is a spent part behaving like a catalyst (Fig 10 :47: "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one").

In the people domain the instrument also acts as an introduced actor. Fig 11 :32: "introduce, never subtract." Once a test or a census category is installed you cannot subtract the habitat it created, and the two silent failures are stated verbatim at Fig 11 :68 "what you failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails" and Fig 11 :69 "what you unintentionally introduced is invisible until it acts".

**Falsifier:** show an instrument that is consumed by producing a reading yet still serves as the stable basis of a repeated category, or show an installed category with no introduced actors and no habitat change. Single-source on Fig 12 :119 and Fig 11 :32, triangulated across the two because they concern different objects (reuse versus habitat).

### Q3 - What order do the operations come in?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

The order is:

1. **perception** - the instrument already returns something that fails to resolve (Fig 6 :34-35);
2. **vocabulary** - the society has a term for what the reading affords (Fig 3 :7);
3. **objective** - it can say what would count as progress in its own terms (Fig 3 :10);
4. **planning** - a route under incomplete information;
5. **learn and carry** - name what worked, verify it, carry it (Fig 3 :15).

Category changes are reported at link 2, but the failure that produces them is usually at link 3. A society can have a new reading and a name for it and still have no term for what would count as progress, so the new category is adopted as a fashion or a convention rather than settled (formula step 5: "A gate is not the ground"). Fig 3 :22: "A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can." That is why a reading taken at the adoption stage is not a weak reading of the cause; it is a reading of nothing.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a case where a category change preceded any extension of representation and any term for what the reading affords, purely as a naming decision with no instrument and no objective, and show that it was settled by the ground. That would invert the dependency order.

### Q4 - Is reclassification subtraction or refusal?

Operators table :58: "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."

Fig 6 :42: "What is recorded only grows. What is reachable is derived, and it can fall. A refutation adds a term that refuses; it removes nothing, and the reach falls because something was added rather than because anything was deleted."

When a census drops a category or a diagnostic manual removes a diagnosis, nothing is deleted from the record. A refusing term is added, and categories built on the refused one lose their standing to be composed over. This is negation (¬), a unary operator, not subtraction (−), which is an absence of an operand. Fig 12 :139-140: "Without a unary operator a system can write what a situation contains, what follows what, and how far along it is - and it cannot write that any of those is false. A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."

So a history of categories that records only additions is a history of a vocabulary that cannot refuse. It will show a monotonic pile of categories and will miss that the reach fell when one was withdrawn. Fig 6 :47 names the open case: if a term and its refusal are both settled, the record holds a contradiction, and whether that is two scopes, an error in the ground, or a world that changed is not decidable here. That is exactly the situation a long historical series of classifications can present, and it should be recorded as open rather than forced.

**Falsifier:** show a category removed from a historical vocabulary where the record truly deleted the old term (not merely added a refusal) and where the reach did not fall. That would break the negation reading.

### Q5 - The looping case as a cycle

Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

In D2, a category is applied to people, the people change, and the new reading re-enters as input to the same category. The sign decides whether the loop closes: a category whose application improves the fit to its own criterion closes a positive loop and becomes self-confirming; one whose application makes the criterion fail opens the loop and can be refused. Fig 11 :49-51: "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries... No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual." A classification that helped one residual can harm another, so no universal verdict on "measurement helped" or "measurement harmed" is available. This is the figure-side structure of the looping effect the world sources describe, and the two are low-overlap witnesses, not one evidence pool.

**Falsifier:** a category applied to people whose output never re-enters as input, with no institutional or behavioral consequence at all. That case is a relabeling and is excluded from the people-domain claim by definition (F5 of `01`).

### Q6 - The two currencies of a category change

Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."

Fig 12 :126: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them and will not notice the shortfall."

A new category can be cheap to state and prohibitively expensive to settle (a new diagnostic label), or expensive to describe and cheap to refute (a long checklist with one decisive test). A history that measures only how many categories were minted counts the denominator; a history that measures only the cost of the instruments counts the other one. Fig 12 :128: "Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind." Summing them is the error the figure names, not the fix.

**Falsifier:** produce a category change where description cost and settlement cost are the same quantity in the same units, with no conversion loss. That would dissolve the distinction.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed atom set, an old category and a new one are **isomers** when they differ only in bond (Fig 12 :117); when the instrument extended what can be represented, the relation is composition plus an import and the isomer claim does not apply. The instrument is a **catalyst** (Fig 12 :119) until it becomes a convention that decides an outcome, at which point it is a spent part behaving as a catalyst. The order is perception then vocabulary then objective (Fig 3 :20), and reclassification is **negation, not subtraction** (Operators :58; Fig 6 :42). In the people domain the loop can close (Fig 12 :121), and the valuation of any category is residual-relative (Fig 11 :49-51). Costs come in two incommensurable currencies (Fig 12 :125-127).

What survives is a structural grammar for describing a category change without turning it into a list. What does not survive is the simple picture of a new measurement replacing an old category: it may instead re-bond the same atoms, refuse one, change contact, or import a new one, and these have different refuters.
