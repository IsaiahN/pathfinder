# Are early Earth settings isomers, and what is the order of the derivation?

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against verbatim figure text, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The source question (origins of life.txt line 18) names one object, "environments capable of supporting prebiotic chemistry", and section 18 of the same source names several candidate settings: deep-sea hydrothermal systems, shallow ponds, volcanic land, ice, or another setting. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. Under Fig 12, are those settings **isomers** (same atoms, different bond, different substance), or only members of a **functional group** (same kind, different member)?
2. What is the **dependency order** among the steps that would answer "when", and where does the chain actually stop?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: The settings as written are a functional group, not isomers

Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :123: "The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

As the source writes them, a vent, a pond, volcanic land, ice and a crust interior do **not** share an atom set. A vent requires rock pores plus a heat gradient; a surface pond requires light and wet-dry cycling; ice requires a temperature regime; a crust interior requires a different contact structure. They share only the kind "setting that composes water and a gradient over time". By Fig 12's definition they are therefore **not isomers** as named. They are members of a functional group: the group says what kind (an environment with water, gradient, feedstock and duration), the qualifier says which member (vent, pond, ice).

### Q2: The stronger form is a real isomer claim over the shared substrate

Recast every candidate over the full shared atom set {Space, Time, Energy, Spectrum, Gradient, Network}, so that each differs only in which structure mediates which state pair:

- a vent: Space(rock pores) bonded to Gradient(heat/redox), Network(reaction network), over Time;
- a pond: Space(surface) bonded to Spectrum(light) and Gradient(wet-dry), Network(cycling), over Time;
- ice: Space(surface) bonded to Gradient(temperature/freezing), Network(concentration in brine veins), over Time;
- crust interior: Space(pores) bonded to Gradient(redox), Network(catalysis), over Time.

Now the atom set is shared and the arrangement differs. Under Fig 13 :76 "A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is", these are genuinely different substances: same atoms, different bonds, different prebiotic behaviour. This is the isomer claim worth testing. It is `specified`, unmeasured.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a candidate setting can be shown to omit an atom that another requires (for example no duration, or no spendable gradient), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading of Q1 stands. Conversely, if two named settings differ only by name and not by bond, they are one term under identity (≡), not two isomers. Both outcomes are recorded rather than smoothed.

### Q3: What "first" is, and is not

Fig 12 :117 makes difference depend on the bond, not the ingredient list. "First" is not an ingredient. The Operators table defines ⋛ as "a threshold relation between two quantities" with the test "is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes." So "first" orders candidate slots by the date at which each becomes capable; it is a comparison outside the composition, not a seventh condition inside it. Adding "earliness" to the environment would be the flat-formula error of Fig 12: the arrangement is the substance, and the comparison is a separate operator.

### Q4: Dependency order, and where the chain stops

Fig 3 orders the chain by dependency, not importance. Applied here:

| link | what it is here | status |
|---|---|---|
| 1 perception | mineral ages and isotope ratios already return readings | works |
| 2 vocabulary | terms for what a setting affords: water, gradient, feedstock, duration | works |
| 3 objective | "earliest slot at which all four conditions hold" | stated, but not yet instrument-ready |
| 4 planning | which instrument moves the lower bound | the likely break |
| 5 learn and carry | carry the bound to later Hadean questions | unreached |

Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

Most published effort in this field lands above the break: more setting descriptions, more laboratory demonstrations that a setting *could* support a step of prebiotic chemistry. Those improve links 1 and 2. They cannot by themselves produce link 4, which is a time anchor from mineral evidence. This is the structural reason the answer is a bound, not a date, and it is independent of which setting turns out to be right.

### Q5: Recursion after settlement

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"

Once a specific setting is settled against the ground (a mineral or experimental result that discriminates sources), it becomes an atom for the next question: which settings coexisted, which survived impacts, and which lineage reached biology. That is a crossing to the next level, not an import that adds a seventh primitive. Until settlement, the setting stays a candidate and cannot be composed over.

## CONCLUSION

The named settings are a **functional group**, not isomers as written. Recast over the full shared six-term atom set they become a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier. "First" is a ⋛ comparison across slots, not a composable condition. The dependency chain stops at the link between stating the earliest-slot objective and having an instrument that returns the time; more setting descriptions do not repair it. Recursion is available only after a setting is settled against the ground.
