# Cycle sign, union order, and time scales: supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading
(`05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Given the residual of `01` section 0, three supporting questions are owed:

1. Why is the adaptive network a **cycle** rather than a chain, and what follows from that?
2. Are the convergence conditions a **partition** or a **union**, and in what order must they be read?
3. What changes when the state and topology time scales are separated versus coincident?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the six substrate terms (space, time, energy as states; spectrum, gradient, network as

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Why a cycle, and what does the sign do?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- An adaptive network satisfies the first clause by definition: topology is updated from state, and
  state is updated on the updated topology. A chain cannot model this; it would have one-pass flow.
- The figure names the sign as the deciding variable. It does **not** in this line fix which sign
  gives settling and which gives amplification. That direction is imported from world sources S2 and
  S3: strong, symmetric coupling produces stable phase locking; weaker or asymmetric coupling
  produces metastability with dwells and escapes. Mark `single-source` for the direction,
  `triangulated` for the structure (Fig 12 + Fig 13 :31-32).
- Fig 13 :31-32: "a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos, and the detail a
  crossing discards reappears above it as noise." This is the regime-B reading when the sign is
  amplifying.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** a closing, settling cycle whose sign is the amplifying one
  under a fixed, declared convention for sign. If that is shown, F3 of `01` fails.

### Q2. Partition or union, and what is the dependency order?

- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: "A case in two conditions is not a harder case of either." Fig 9 :54: "And when the split
  lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- The conditions are: (a) a reference that does not update; (b) a cycle sign that closes; (c) a
  composition that settles and is admitted; (d) a per-slot instrument that returns something; (e)
  if maintained, continued work. These overlap. For example, (a) and (d) are both needed for (c) to
  be observable: a settled term needs a ground that pays, and a read that resolves.
- Dependency order (Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance"):
  1. **Perception** (D4): does the instrument return a per-slot residual at all? Fig 3 :27: "A link with
     no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
  2. **Anchor** (D1): does the reference hold still when the network pushes on it? Fig 2 :33: "The
     anchor must not update."
  3. **Settlement** (D2): does a composed structure get admitted as an atom? Fig 12 :106: "Nothing
     composes over what was never admitted as a part."
  4. **Maintenance** (D3): if a seat is steering, is the work ongoing? Fig 11 :63: "Which is why a
     managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."
- A convergence claim made at step 4 when step 1 never returned a signal is "a reading of nothing"
  (Fig 3 :23). This is the order claim, and it is checkable without a measurement.
- **Falsifier:** a correct convergence verdict reached while step 1 (per-slot signal) is absent, or a
  case where step 3 binds before step 2. Either breaks the dependency order.

### Q3. What do the two time scales change?

- Fig 4 :3: "The same machine at two sizes, and the rule about what may pass between them." Fig 4 :21-22:
  "The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a
  filter with a direction." Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."
- Map the two scales onto the network: the fast scale is state change on a fixed topology; the slow
  scale is topological evolution. The transform between them is lossy, so the round trip carries a
  gap R_T (Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by
  dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked.").
- If the scales are strongly separated, the fast dynamics can be summarized for the slow dynamics.
  World source S1 states the constraint sharply: "in every scale-separated system there has to be a
  discontinuous transition in the fast dynamics in order to maintain an adaptive interplay in the
  long-term evolution of the system. Otherwise the fast dynamics is simply slaved to the slow
  dynamics." This is corroboration, not a figure line.
- If the scales coincide, information can be "stored in and read from the topology" (S1), and the
  loop's sign and the manufactured gradient (Fig 5 :46) become the dominant variables.
- The practical consequence: a convergence result on one scale does not transfer to the other. A
  method can cross up; a recording of one run cannot (Fig 4 :25: "A recording carried upward looks like
  knowledge and is a description of one occasion.").
- **Falsifier:** a demonstration that a convergence certificate at the fast scale is also a
  convergence certificate at the slow scale with R_T measured as zero. That would break the lossy
  transform claim.

### Q4. Are the settled and the reorganizing networks isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- If regime A and regime B are built from the same six-term set and differ only in the bond
  (the cycle sign, the reference relation), they are isomers in the figure's sense: same atoms,
  different arrangement, different behaviour. That is a `specified` structural claim, and it is the
  strongest form of the answer.
- If, instead, regime B requires an imported atom that regime A does not (a new node class, a new
  external driver), the two are not isomers and the union is a genuine compound that must be
  unbundled (Fig 9 :57-58: "becomes a specific compound").
- The test is therefore per-case: enumerate the atoms in each regime before calling them isomers.
- **Falsifier:** a pair of regimes where one requires an atom the other does not, yet they are claimed
  as isomers. The claim then falls to the functional-group reading instead.

## CONCLUSION

The adaptive network is a **cycle**, so the sign of its loop is the deciding variable (Fig 12 :121),
with the sign-to-regime mapping imported from world sources and marked `single-source`. The five
conditions are a **union**, not a partition, and must be read in dependency order: instrument,
anchor, settlement, maintenance (Fig 9 :55, Fig 3 :20). Two time scales are joined by a lossy
transform whose round-trip gap R_T must be measured, not assumed (Fig 4 :30). Regime A and regime B
may be **isomers** over the shared six-term set, but that is a per-case claim with a stated falsifier.

Nothing here needs a new primitive, an import that moves the wall, or a name for a missing atom. What
it needs, to move from `specified` to `grounded`, is an outside instrument that registers whether R
is still arriving against a reference that does not update.
