# Supporting derivation: structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit leaves two sub-questions: (1) what is the **structure** that makes two languages differ in a way that could reach perception, and (2) in what **order** does that structure act relative to perception and the objective? A third question is whether low-overlap analogues outside psycholinguistics change the answer or merely corroborate it.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

- Atoms held: states (space, time, energy) and structures (spectrum, gradient, network), plus the psycholinguistics frame's own terms (slot, term, bond, category, task). No new primitive is available without import.
- Bonds held: the seven plus negation. The ordered ones (sequence, production, subtraction, comparison) are the candidates for grammatical structure.
- Actors / currency / anchor: the speaker and the language frame (currency: what composes inside closure), the slot and the instrument (currency: residual, anchor: the measurement), the community (currency: contact and transmission).
- Question type: structure is a **partition** question (which bond holds), order is a **dependency** question (Fig 3), analogues are a **union** question (they share an evidence pool only if drawn from the same framework).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are two languages isomers, or just two inventories?

- Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two language communities with the same underlying perceptual atoms (focal colors, spatial layout, temporal succession) but different **obligatory bonds** (absolute vs relative reference frames, distance vs quantity metaphors for duration, classifier vs number-based counting) are candidates for Fig 12's isomer claim: same atoms, different arrangement, different downstream behaviour. As usually reported, however, the communities differ in both bonds and atoms and in ecology, so they are not clean isomers; they are members of the same **functional group** with different qualifiers. The isomer reading is the stronger, testable form: hold the atom set and the task fixed, change only the bond, and look for a non-linguistic difference.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two languages with the same atoms and the same obligatory bonds still show a non-linguistic perceptual difference, the isomer reading fails and the difference belongs to an unbundled condition (vocabulary, ecology, task framing). If two languages with different bonds and the same atoms show no non-linguistic difference, the bond claim fails.

### Q2 - What order does the effect have?

- Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- The bond table: "A then B; B needs A to have happened" (Fig 12 :71); "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes" (Fig 12 :73).

The order is perception (1) -> vocabulary (2) -> objective (3) -> planning (4) -> learn and carry (5). Language acts at link 2, before the objective is fixed. That ordering matters because an effect at link 2 propagates into how progress is defined at link 3 and into what is planned at link 4, while an effect claimed at link 1 would have to move a measurement that link 1 already tests. The ordered bond -> is the right carrier here: the language supplies a route ("A then B"), not just an ingredient.

**Falsifier:** demonstrate a language-dependent change in link-1 transduction with the instrument independently calibrated and link 2 held constant. That reverses the dependency order and breaks Q2.

### Q3 - Does a geodesy analogue change the verdict?

- The Wikipedia article on spatial reference systems states that "The goal of any spatial reference system is to create a common reference frame in which locations can be measured precisely and consistently as coordinates" and that a datum has "one or more anchor points or control points".
- Fig 2 :25: "pinned: it does not update on A or B"; Fig 2 :31-32: "Independence, between two frames, is how little they draw on the same evidence."

This is a low-overlap analogue from geodesy and engineering, not evidence about psycholinguistics. It corroborates one structural point: a coordinate frame is useless without anchor points, and two frames need a documented transformation between them. Applied here, a language that supplies a frame of reference still needs a ground that does not update on it. The analogue does not add an atom to the psycholinguistics derivation; it shows the same shape in a different field. Overlap with the framework is low, so its agreement carries a little more weight than an essay by the framework author, but it is still a secondary witness.

**Falsifier:** show a working spatial reference system with no anchor or control points, or one whose anchor moves when the observer changes frame. That would break the analogy, not the psycholinguistics verdict.

### Q4 - Does the metamerism analogue change the verdict?

- *The Hard Problem is About Source*: "If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information"; "The answer is unreached until a better instrument exists."
- Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."

The analogy strengthens F9 rather than dissolving it. A cross-linguistic task difference is a projection; several sources (grammar, vocabulary, ecology, instrument) can produce the same reading. A null result is likewise a projection: a spectrometer resolves green's source, but an eye reading green cannot. The analogue predicts that progress comes from a source-discriminating design, not from more between-group correlations.

**Falsifier:** a task reading whose source is uniquely determined by the language alone, with all other candidates excluded by design. That would make the metamerism analogue inapplicable to this case.

### Q5 - What does bilingual switching add?

- Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."
- Fig 8 :51: "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them"
- World source: "bilinguals can flexibly switch between different conceptual systems, depending on the language they are using."

A bilingual speaker is one habitat containing two frames. The wall can move because the second closure differs, and the within-subject switch is the closest available source gate. It is still a union compound: activation history, proficiency and task language are overlapping conditions, so the switch must be randomised and recorded.

**Falsifier:** a bilingual switch effect that disappears when proficiency and task language are matched, leaving only the cultural condition. That would move the case from import to union residue.

## CONCLUSION

The structure that matters is the **bond**, not the inventory. Two languages over the same atoms with different obligatory bonds are the clean isomer case; as normally studied they are a functional-group case with confounded qualifiers. The order is fixed by Fig 3: language at link 2, before the objective, never at link 1. Three analogues (geodesy anchors, metamerism, bilingual contact) corroborate the shape at low overlap with the framework and add no new atom. This rescues the checkable part of the Whorfian intuition without determinism: grammar changes the reach of a frame, the ground stays put, and the conditions are stated in advance.
