# Isomers, catalysts and order in a structural change

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

A redesign changes which actors carry the loop and which bonds join them. Two questions are owed: (1) when old and new structure are written over the same atom set, are they **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, and (2) which carriers are **catalysts** (they survive their own use) versus spent parts (they do not)? A third question follows: what is the dependency order of a change, given Fig 3?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the library and its terms, the six structures including network (Fig 13 :24), the seven bonds and negation, the ground and the seat. No new primitive is available without import. The redesign changes bonds and the assignment of atoms to slots. The three losses from the question are properties of this arrangement, not new atoms (F11 of `01`).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are old and new structure isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*
- Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*
- Fig 13 :124-125: *"the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."*

Write the redesign as a re-bonding over a fixed atom set: the same people, the same held terms, the same ground, the same seat, with reporting edges, contact edges and authority edges rearranged. Under Fig 12 the two structures are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. This is the strongest structural reading of the question, because it explains why an org chart can look "the same on paper" (the ingredient list is unchanged) while behaviour changes decisively (the bonds changed).

Isomerism is a **claim about the arrangement**, not a claim that nothing was lost. If the redesign removes an atom outright (a carrier leaves and the term goes with them because the arrangement was not recorded), the atom set is no longer fixed and the isomer form fails. The weaker, still-true reading is then a **functional group** claim: Fig 12 :124, *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Both old and new structure exhibit the substructure "a method crosses a boundary and a seat can refuse", with different members in the slots.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a redesign necessarily changes the atom set (a category of actor, term or ground disappears and cannot be reproduced by contact), the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group form stands. If both structures share every atom and every bond, they are the same substance and the claim that a change occurred is empty.

### Q2 - Which carriers are catalysts?

- Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*
- Fig 12 :107-110: *"the result carries forward and the deriving does not, so every lineage pays again."*

A mentor whose knowledge is regenerated by contact with each new cohort is a catalyst: the part survives its own use. A departure that takes an unrecorded arrangement with it is a spent part: the use consumed it. The difference is not in the person, it is in whether the bond that carried the knowledge is re-made by the habitat. Nothing in the organization's records necessarily says which it is, which is exactly the failure Fig 12 :119 names. The Bates et al. (2025) review is the low-overlap counterpart: after severe disruption, *" elephant societies can function at a basic level without elders"*, yet *"some of these 'cull-orphan' populations lack potentially significant forms of knowledge"*. Basic function survived; the catalytic part did not.

- **Falsifier:** if per-slot performance is identical whether a carrier's arrangement is re-made by contact or not, with the atom set held and the ground unchanged, the catalyst distinction is idle for this question.

### Q3 - What is the order of a change?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

The dependency order for a structural change is:

1. **Perception:** can the organization tell what contact currently carries the residual? If not, the map is the broken link and no redesign helps.
2. **Vocabulary:** are there terms for the bonds (who consults whom, who can refuse what)? A list of atoms has no terms for these, and "a vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one" (Fig 12 :111).
3. **Objective:** can the organization say what would count as retained, in effect terms, per slot? "A goal is a comparison" (Fig 12 :112-113); without it, autonomy pushes decisions to people who cannot score them.
4. **Planning:** is there a route from the old bond set to the new one that reproduces required contact?
5. **Learn and carry:** is the method, not the recording, carried up and sent down as a head start (Fig 4 :22)?

Fig 4 :27 warns that the transform between sizes loses information, so a method that worked for one team does not transfer to the organization without a measured round trip: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."*

- **Falsifier:** a redesign that delivers a durable per-slot gain when steps 2 and 3 are demonstrably absent and unmeasured. That would break the dependency order.

### Q4 - Is the union a compound with its own loop?

- Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

Knowledge loss and relationship loss overlap (an unrecorded arrangement often lived in a relationship), but overlap is not identity. A union has its own loop and its own ground (Fig 9 :54). A single lever, such as "document everything", clears the region where only the record condition holds and leaves the contact-and-refusal intersections standing, because the second condition still binds. What remains is not a harder documentation problem; it is a specific compound.

- **Falsifier:** a single instrument that settles both conditions at every slot with no interaction term and no residue. Then the union is a partition and can be searched as one question.

## CONCLUSION

Old and new structures over a fixed atom set are isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance (Fig 12 :117), which is why an unchanged ingredient list can coexist with changed behaviour. If the redesign removes an atom, the weaker functional-group form stands instead. Knowledge carriers divide into catalysts, whose knowledge is regenerated by contact and survives use, and spent parts, whose unrecorded arrangement is consumed by departure (Fig 12 :119). The change itself is ordered by dependency: perception before vocabulary, vocabulary before objective, objective before planning, and method transfer last, with a measured round trip between scales. The three losses form a union compound with its own ground, so no single lever clears it.

This framing keeps the structural content of the question without pretending the framework names a retention recipe. `metric = method` is not the claim; `old and new are isomers over a fixed atom set, and retention is a property of the bond and the record` is the checkable claim.
