# Monetary and fiscal as isomers, and the order of composition

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6). No multiplier, lag length or ratio is asserted.

## RESIDUAL

The audit refused a standing ranking and returned a per-slot separator. Two structural questions remain before the answer is complete:

1. Are monetary and fiscal policy two spellings of one thing, or two different substances over the same atoms? That is the isomer question.
2. If they compose, is the composition ordered, a cycle, a comparison, or a flat sum? That is the bond question. A one-occasion answer ("rate cuts worked in 2009") is a recording, not a method (Fig 4 :25-26).

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the stabilization frame: the policy rate, the central bank balance sheet, reserve requirements, taxes, government spending, transfers, debt issuance, credit conditions and expectations. All are already in Γ for this frame; none is a missing primitive. Bonds held: the seven in Fig 12 plus negation. The anchor is a measured, non-updating per-slot outcome. Money and fiscal authority are different closures (D1 and D2 of the audit), so their union may have a surplus, but only against a described residual (Fig 8 :19-21).

## Q1 — Are they isomers over a fixed atom set?

Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Over the fixed atom set `{policy rate, balance sheet, taxes, spending, transfers, debt, credit, expectations}`, write the two cores schematically:

```
monetary core:  (policy rate + balance sheet) --intertemporal-substitution--> (credit, expectations) --> (prices)
fiscal core:    (taxes + spending + transfers) --budget-constraint--> (disposable income, demand) --> (activity)
```

They share atoms. A rate change and a tax change both alter household intertemporal choices; both change demand; both interact with expectations; both are constrained by the government's intertemporal budget at some level. But the bond differs. The monetary core operates by changing the price of credit and the expected path of short rates. The fiscal core operates by changing the budget constraint and the direct claim on output. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. So over the full shared atom set they are **isomers** in Fig 12's sense.

If the atom set is narrowed to the two narrow instrument names (rate versus tax), they share no atom at all and the isomer claim fails; they become a **functional group** instead. Fig 12 :123:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Both belong to the functional group "demand-side stabilization instrument," which says what kind, while "rate" and "tax" say which member. The isomer claim is the stronger, checkable form; the functional-group claim is the weaker form that survives a narrow census.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one core can be written without an atom the other requires (for example, if a monetary core needs no expectations term, or a fiscal core needs no budget term), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group claim stands instead. This is the same test used in the exemplar's `06` Q3.

**Mode:** `specified`, ungrounded. **Status:** single-source on the figure definition (Fig 12), with no world source claiming the isomer mapping. It is a structural claim, not an empirical one.

## Q2 — What bond does the composition use?

Fig 12 :46: "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands." Fig 12 :111-112:

> **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**
> **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."**

- **`+` conjunction (both hold at once).** In normal conditions monetary and fiscal can both be active on different slots. Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term (Fig 12 :92: "Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing"), so there is no extra content in "fiscal and monetary" versus "monetary and fiscal." Any claimed ordering must come from another bond.
- **`→` sequence (A then B).** In a funding crisis, liquidity provision must precede effective demand support, because the transmission channel for the latter is broken until the former restores it. Fig 12 :71: "A then B; B needs A to have happened." The order is a property of the habitat, not of the instruments. In a different regime the order may reverse.
- **`⋛` comparison (threshold).** The objective on each slot is a threshold relation, not a state: Fig 12 :87, "a threshold relation between two quantities," and the Operators table :51, "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one." A target is a comparison; the policy action is the subtraction that closes the gap (Fig 12 :112).
- **`−` subtraction.** Progress on a slot is the reduction of its residual. This is why "how much did policy help" is only well defined slot by slot; a global subtraction over an aggregate residual erases the index (Fig 1 :22).
- **`¬` negation.** A refutation is a negation (Operators :58: "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"). The pre-registered refuter in `01` §5 is the unary operator that lets the held ranking claim be refused rather than merely accumulated.

The practical consequence: the flat phrase "policy mix" is a `+` spelling and carries no information about sequence, threshold or subtraction. Where the state of the habitat imposes an order, the mix is a `→` chain and the order is load bearing. This is the structural content behind F2 of the audit.

## Q3 — Is there a cycle, and what decides its sign?

Fig 12 :121:

> **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Fiscal policy has a genuine cycle: spending raises income, income raises receipts, receipts change the deficit, the deficit changes future spending capacity. The sign of that loop decides whether debt stabilizes. Monetary policy has a different loop: the rate changes expectations, expectations change current demand, demand changes inflation, inflation changes the real rate and thus the effective stance. Both are cycles in Fig 12's sense; a chain cannot close by construction. A model that writes either as a one-way chain (rate leads to output, full stop) has lost the closure and with it the sign question.

The two cycles can couple. In a liquidity trap, a fiscal expansion can raise inflation expectations, lowering the real rate and thereby increasing the effectiveness of monetary policy even when the nominal rate cannot fall. That is a cross-cycle bond, and it is a composition across two different closures (D1 and D2), which is where Fig 8 :45 places the surplus. It is not an import that adds an atom (Fig 6 :21).

**Falsifier:** if the fiscal loop's sign is independent of the state of the other cycle in every observed regime, the cross-cycle composition claim fails and the two instruments are separable, not coupled.

## Q4 — Credibility as catalyst, and the type question

Fig 12 :119:

> **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

Forward guidance and the credibility of the target behave like a catalyst: a small term changes the rate at which the rest of the composition works, it survives its own use, and it can be spent and regenerated across episodes. The chemistry source opened 2026-11-23 confirms the atom definition ("Catalysts are not consumed by the reaction and remain unchanged after the reaction"), but the mapping from credibility to that atom is a binding from the economics frame.

**Live type question, not hidden.** Fig 12 distinguishes a catalyst, which is regenerated, from a spent-and-regenerating part and from a sacrificial reagent that is consumed. Nothing in the figures decides which one policy credibility is. An instrument that discriminates "regenerated credibility" from "a recording of one successful episode replayed" is owed. Until it exists, the catalyst binding is `specified` and ungrounded. This is the live strain recorded in `01` §7 and carried into `09` and `11`.

## Q5 — The two currencies do not add

Fig 12 :125-127:

> **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
> **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
> **"The two readings come apart in both directions."**

A stabilization rule is cheap to describe (one line: "raise rates when inflation exceeds target") and expensive to settle (it takes quarters and a ground reading to learn whether it paid). A complex discretionary intervention can be expensive to describe and cheap to refute (one contradictory outcome). The budget decides how much of the space is reached; description cost and action cost must be read separately. Fig 12 :130: "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it." This is why the audit never converts "which tool is stronger" into a single score: that would add two non-additive currencies and let a filter hand the verdict (Fig 9 :60).

## CONCLUSION

Over the full shared atom set the two cores are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117); over a narrow atom set they are members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :123). The composition is not a flat sum where order matters: in a funding crisis it is a `→` sequence, the objectives are `⋛` comparisons, progress is a `−` subtraction on a per-slot residual, and the held ranking claim is refused by `¬`. Both cores are cycles, and their cross-coupling is where the union surplus can live. Credibility is bound to the catalyst atom with a live type question attached. Description cost and action cost stay in separate columns. None of this returns a ratio or a standing priority order; that remains the declared `gap` of F12.
