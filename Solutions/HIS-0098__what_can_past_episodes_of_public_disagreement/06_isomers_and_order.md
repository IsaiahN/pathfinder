# Isomers and order: two communication formats with the same ingredients

**Mode: `specified`, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a comparison of
actual audiences (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

Two public statements can contain the same facts, the same uncertainty number and the same two expert voices, yet
produce different public understanding. The question of this supporting derivation is: is that difference a difference
of ingredients or a difference of bond and order? If it is only a difference of ingredients, the lesson from past
episodes reduces to "add better facts". If it is a difference of bond and order, the lesson is structural and transfers.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: a claim C, an uncertainty estimate U, a supporting voice A, a dissenting voice B, and a ground G (an
instrument, a counter, an outcome). Bonds held: the seven, including `+` conjunction, `∥` disjunction, `→` sequence,
`−` subtraction, `⋛` comparison, plus negation `¬`. No new atom is introduced. The two formats differ only in how these
atoms are bonded and in the order of the bonds.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Same ingredients, different substance

Fig 12 :46-47: "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and
which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and
decisive in the behaviour."

Format A (balance plus information): `C + U + (A ∥ B)`, with G present in the room but not used as the thing each
voice ranges against. Format B (residual first): `(describe what would be seen → state residual U → name falsifier ¬
→ claim C)`, with A and B each separately ranged against G.

Both formats contain C, U, A, B and G. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different
substance. The difference is invisible if you record only the ingredient list (which is what an audit of "how many
facts and how many voices" does) and decisive in behaviour. So the historic lesson is not "more or better ingredients";
it is a bond change.

**Refuter, written before the run:** if a controlled presentation test finds that the two formats are indistinguishable
on a held-out ground measure while the ingredient list is held constant, the isomer claim fails. The formats are then
spellings of one term, and the structural lesson collapses.

### Q2 - The symmetric reading is the trap

Fig 12 :92: "Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term".

Under `+` or `∥`, A and B commute: `A ∥ B` and `B ∥ A` are one term. That is exactly the ideal of equal-time balance.
Fig 12 :23 in the source says of a symmetric bond that "this bond is symmetric, so the four are one term". The public
performance of fairness is a symmetric bond over voices.

The problem is not that symmetric bonds are invalid. Fig 2 :41 permits agreement to be "the product, not a corruption
of it" when the subject is a shared meaning. The problem is using a symmetric bond over voices to settle a
**factual** question. Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." A
symmetric bond over A and B has no term for G, so it cannot range either voice against the thing the claim is about.

**Refuter:** a symmetric format that includes G as a non-updating term for both voices and still produces the public
understanding claimed for the ordered format. Then `∥` can carry ground contact and the trap finding is wrong.

### Q3 - Sequence is not commutative, and that is the point

Fig 12 :74: "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes." This is the test for `→` sequence.

Swap the first two bonds of format B: `(state claim C → describe what would be seen)`. The artifact now asks the
audience to adopt a claim and then supply expectations, which invites the audience to fit the expectations to the
claim. Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit". The two orders are
different terms, not spellings. This is why "tell people the answer, then explain the uncertainty" and "describe what
you would expect if this were true, then give the claim" are different communications even with identical text.

Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one." A list of facts names a
state. The ordered sequence names a route. `THE_FORMULA.md` "Composition" makes the same point: "a plan is a sequence,
a goal is a comparison, progress is a subtraction."

**Refuter:** an order-swap experiment showing no difference on the ground measure, with content held constant. Then
`→` is doing no work here and the order finding is withdrawn.

### Q4 - Refusal precedes mint

Fig 5 :53: "a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so". `THE_FORMULA.md` step 2 routes REFUTED before
minting: the fifth kind sorts a gap by what is wrong and cannot be reached by the other four.

In the historic episodes, the move that manufactured doubt was not a new claim but an old regularity held as open:
"the science is not settled" long after the ground had settled it. The correct structural move is a refusal, a `¬`
term saying that the regularity "this remains an open question" is false for that slot. A vocabulary with `−`
(subtraction) but no `¬` can say what is missing from the story and cannot say that the story is wrong; it can only
accumulate. Fig 5 :65-66 requires making the sought shape an ordinary term so the preference is refusable.

**Refuter:** a case where no held term is false, the gap is genuinely a NOVEL gap (extend perception), and a refusal
would be out of order. Then this finding does not apply and should be narrowed to the settled-slot case.

### Q5 - Functional group, not universal identity

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the
qualifier says which member."

All defensible uncertainty communications share a functional group: `(ground, residual, falsifier)`. The qualifier
differs by domain: an instrument in a lab, a validated survey item for a public audience, a held-out test for a
communication study. As in the exemplar's parallel finding for GR and QM, two practices written over the same full atom
set are isomers; two practices written over small different atom sets are members of the same functional group rather
than isomers. Claiming universal identity across all domains would overstate what the figure licenses.

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level
and never about the world." Once a first-order claim has been settled by its ground, it can serve as an atom in a
second-order public discussion. Before that, the second-order room has nothing sound to compose over.

**Refuter:** a practice that reliably keeps contact without an explicit ground term or an explicit falsifier. Then the
functional group is not necessary and must be narrowed.

## CONCLUSION

The ingredients are not the difference. The bond and the order are. Balance plus information bonds the voices
symmetrically and leaves the ground as a bystander; residual-first bonding sequences description, residual, refusal,
claim, and ranges each voice against a non-updating ground. Under Fig 12 those are isomers, not spellings. The
structurally decisive move is `¬` before mint: refuse the false "still open" term for a slot the ground has settled.
The shared functional group across domains is `(ground, residual, falsifier)`, with the qualifier supplied by the
domain's instrument. Every claim here is `specified`, and the four refuters above are written before any comparison is
run.
