**Subject:** Check one claim about law in practice before you decide whether the framework is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I am writing with one specific claim I would like you to check against what you already know about law in practice. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`specified`, not `grounded`):** a written rule is a declared meaning, and a declared meaning does not ground itself. Where the statute book and everyday enforcement diverge, people's expectations and behaviour follow the signal whose channel to the ground stays reachable, while the written text functions as a convention rather than as a verdict about what happens. I am not claiming that written law is irrelevant. I am claiming that its effect at a slot depends on whether the enforcement channel carries an outcome the person can read.
>
> **What would refute the claim, written down before this message:**
> 1. Change the written text alone, hold the enforcement channel demonstrably constant, and show that expectations and behaviour at the named slot change with the text.
> 2. Show a case where expectations collapse while both the fidelity of the transmitted rule and the ground's pressure on the case measure high and are not declining.
> 3. Show an averaged institutional statistic that predicts prospectively, per named slot, whose expectation changes next after a specified enforcement shift.
> 4. Show that the same statutory text under two different enforcement bonds produces invariant expectations at the two slots.
>
> If any one holds, the shape is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that this settles how much behaviour changes, which particular rule will work, or that the framework is the only account of law in practice. It returns a shape and a set of falsifiers, not a number or a named rule. I am also not asking you to trust me. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the public sources are below.
> - Figures and formula, canonical extract: local file `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md` in the Project Pathfinder framework
> - Legal realism and the prediction thesis: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_realism
> - Sociology of law and Ehrlich's living law: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_law
> - New institutionalism and decoupling: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_institutionalism
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the eight-step formula is in the figures. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and label the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
