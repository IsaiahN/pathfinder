# Written law and enforcement as isomers, and the order of the causal chain

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The written rule and the enforcement record present the same statutory atoms but produce different expectations at different slots. Two questions are owed: (1) under Fig 12's definition, are "law in books" and "law in action" **isomers** of one atom set, or merely members of a functional group; and (2) in what order do perception, vocabulary, objective, planning and learning break down when the two diverge.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (before choosing a figure line)

Atoms held for this derivation: a declared text, an enforcement event, a person's belief b, a weighting w, a sanction or warning, a peer signal, and a record. Bonds held: the seven, including sequence `→`, comparison `⋛`, subtraction `−`, and the unary refusal `¬` (Operators table, Fig 12 amendment). No new primitive is available without import.

R by arity: three-place (person, text, event), indexed by slot and ordered by time. Symmetry: asymmetric, because text and event are not interchangeable. Scale: person, office, population, cohort.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are law-in-books and law-in-action isomers?

> Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
> Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
> Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**

Take a jurisdiction with a fixed statutory text. The atoms are {text, sanction, person, record}. Two readings of the same atoms are available:

- **Reading B (books):** text bonded to sanction by a declared rule, `text → sanction`, where the bond is the statute's own statement of what follows.
- **Reading A (action):** text bonded to a warning, a connection, or no event at all. The bond is the enforcement routine, not the declaration.

Same atoms, different bond, different expectation and behaviour. By Fig 12 these are **isomers**, not variants of one thing. The formula (the statute) cannot tell you which you have, which is exactly the *"difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour"* case.

**Weaker reading.** If the two jurisdictions also differ in atoms (one has a specialized court, the other does not), they are not isomers; they share a **functional group**, Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Both readings still have the form `declared rule → enforcement event`, so the group fixes the kind, and the qualifier fixes which member.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a pair of slots with identical statutory atoms and different enforcement bonds shows invariant expectations, the isomer claim fails and the two readings are merely two spellings of one substance. Named in `01` F6 refuter 5.

### Q2: Which bond is the formal/informal distinction?

The legal pluralism reference page reports that within US courthouses *"the formal ideology of law as it is written exists alongside the informal ideology of law as it is used"* (Wikipedia, Legal pluralism, fetched 2026-11-23). Under Fig 12, "formal" and "informal" are not atoms; they are bond types. The formal bond is a declared sequence (`text → sanction`). The informal bond is often a comparison (`⋛`, a threshold on who gets which outcome) or a subtraction (`−`, the sanction removed or absent for an insider).

> Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**

Which bond holds is not recoverable from reading the statute. An enforcement study is the instrument that distinguishes them. This is the metamerism point of `05` section 3 restated in bond terms: the behaviour is a projection whose source is lost unless the instrument resolves the bond.

### Q3: What order does the chain break in?

> Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
> Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
> Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

Order: 1 perception of the enforcement event → 2 vocabulary for what the rule affords → 3 objective, what counts as fair treatment here → 4 planning the action → 5 learning and carrying to the next cohort.

The middle link is where the instrument is usually missing. Legal-cultural surveys routinely measure perception (have you heard of the rule?) and planning (do you intend to comply?), but the vocabulary/objective link, what the rule affords at the person's slot and what would count as progress there, has no standard instrument. Fig 12 :115: **"having terms for what a situation affords was taken for a dictionary and it is a grammar."** A dictionary of legal terms is not a grammar for a person's situation.

**Falsifier:** a documented population whose perception, vocabulary and objective links all measure as working, and whose failure is located at planning. That breaks the "usually at the middle" claim, not the dependency ordering. Named in `01` F11 refuter.

### Q4: How does a rule change valence when the residual changes?

> Fig 11 :49: **"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."**
> Fig 11 :50: **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."**
> Fig 11 :51: **"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."**
> Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."**

A formal rule that protects a person in one enforcement habitat (a landlord-tenant rule enforced by a court that hears small claims) becomes an instrument of harassment in another (the same rule used to threaten a tenant who complains). Nothing about the text changes. A catalyst-like part regenerates across uses; a spent part does not, and nothing in the rule's description records which it is. There is therefore no standing list of "good laws" to transplant. The transplant literature's warning about *"a recognition of pluralism"* being treated as *"a roadblock to nation-building"* (Wikipedia, Legal pluralism) is the political version of the same valence flip.

**Falsifier:** a rule whose measured valence is invariant across a documented change of the residual at the slot. If no such change can be found, this claim weakens to `general`.

### Q5: Is the union a partition?

> Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**
> Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**

C1 (declared meaning grounded) and C2 (channel reachable) and C3 (a seat able to refuse) overlap. A case failing only C1 is a dead letter; a case failing only C2 is a person who has lost touch; a case failing C3 is a convention that has quietly become a verdict. A single lever, "clarify the statute" or "increase enforcement", clears one region and leaves the intersections standing. The union must be unbundled before a reform is chosen.

## CONCLUSION

Law-in-books and law-in-action are **isomers over a fixed statutory atom set**: same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference invisible in the text and decisive in behaviour. Where the atom sets differ, the relation weakens to a functional group. The chain breaks at the vocabulary/objective link, where a person can name the ingredients but has no grammar for what the rule affords at their slot. A rule's valence is relative to the residual, so no standing list of good laws survives a change of habitat. These are structural claims with stated falsifiers; none is `grounded`.
