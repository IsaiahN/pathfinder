## Q1. Adjudicative versus consensual: isomer or functional group?

The world source distinguishes two types: *"Adjudicative processes, such as litigation or arbitration, in which a judge, jury or arbitrator determines the outcome"* and *"Consensual processes, such as collaborative law, mediation, conciliation, or negotiation, in which the parties attempt to reach agreement."*

Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

- Read over the settlement molecule {parties, contested threshold, rule or evidence, third seat, ruling, record}, both types use the same atoms. What differs is the bond. Adjudicative binds the seat to the rule by production (⇒ a ruling is produced that did not exist before) and binds the parties to the outcome by comparison (⋛). Consensual binds the parties to each other by sequence (→ offer, counter, concession) and the seat by facilitation, which is a catalyst rather than a production source.
- On the atoms as written, they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. This is testable because the behaviours differ: adjudication produces a binding outcome authoritative even if a party dissents; consensual process can end without agreement and has no output if a party withdraws. The *Dispute resolution* source confirms the behavioural difference: *"The verdict of the court is binding, not advisory"*, while ADR *"generally depends on agreement by the parties to use ADR processes"*.

**The strain.** Consensual processes borrow coercive enforcement from the background court system: an agreement that is not honoured can be taken to a court. If coercion is an atom in the molecule, the two types no longer share an atom set and the isomer claim fails.

**Re-census (kept visible).** Coercion is a habitat condition, not a settlement atom. Fig 11 :44 requires the habitat to be enumerated, and Fig 13 :74-75 requires composition across kinds, never within one. Putting coercion inside the settlement molecule would mix a habitat actor into a settlement composition. On that re-census the isomer claim survives *at the settlement level*, and the habitat is declared separately. This is a level-relative verdict (Fig 12 :104: *"irreducible is a fact about a level"*), not a claim about the whole legal system.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one process can be shown to require an atom the other lacks *inside the settlement molecule* (for example, a ruling that exists only when one seat can compel attendance), the isomer claim fails and the weaker **functional-group** claim stands: both are `state → structure → state` style settlements with different qualifiers. Recorded here before any check was run.

**Status:** `single-source` for the figure definition (Fig 12), with the world-facing check unmeasured. Triangulated with the world source on the *behavioural* difference, not on the atom census.

## Q2. The dependency order of a mediation chain

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**

| link | what it is in mediation | figure |
|---|---|---|
| 1 perception | what happened, distinguished from what was there before | Fig 3 :4-5 |
| 2 vocabulary | what claims and remedies are nameable, what the situation affords | Fig 3 :6-8 |
| 3 objective | what would count as a settled dispute, in the institution's own terms | Fig 3 :9-11 |
| 4 planning | a route to that objective under incomplete information | Fig 3 :12-13 |
| 5 learn and carry | name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next dispute | Fig 3 :14-16 |

Fig 3 :25-27: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments"*; *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

The middle link (2 to 3) is the one that breaks. A list of grievances names a state and cannot name a route to one: Fig 12 :111, **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."** Fig 12 :112 makes the grammar explicit: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."*

So the order is not "hire a mediator, then decide what success means". It is: establish what happened, make the affordances nameable, **bind what counts as resolution as an ordinary term**, then plan, then carry. The world source corroborates the first move from a different direction: *"The first step in a dispute is usually de-escalation"*, and Glasl assigns moderation to the early stages, conciliation and mediation to the middle, and arbitration or power intervention to the late ones. That is a stage-ordering from clinical practice, low overlap with the figure's dependency argument.

**Falsifier:** a documented process that reliably plans a route to settlement before it has a working definition of what counts as settlement, and that still closes disputes at a higher rate than one that follows the dependency order. If found, Fig 3's order is being applied to the wrong chain.

## Q3. Escalation as the reverse loop

Escalation is not a larger instance of a dispute. It is the loop running the other way:

1. **Mutual update instead of ranging against an anchor.** Fig 2 :33: *"The anchor must not update."* The world source on conflict escalation: the *"escalation trap is a cycle where small conflicts or behaviors intensify because each party involved continuously increases their response"*, and systems theory models the process as *"positive feedback"*. That is Fig 2 panel 1 across scales.
2. **Referral to a like venue.** Fig 7 :12-13: *"same closure, handed along; agreement compounds, evidence does not"*. Fig 7 :36: *"A chain of mirrors is one long room. The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced."* A referral chain between institutions that read the same evidence is motion without progress.
3. **Aggregation of the residual.** Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."* "Community conflict" is the average; the live dispute is the slot.
4. **More process inside one closure.** Fig 8 :32: *"Closure is idempotent. Composing inside a closed set never leaves it, so no search, however long, adds an atom."* Adding another meeting, another form, or another committee inside the same frame adds no atom.

A third-order effect is the one that matters. Fig 11 :52-54 uses DDT and eagles: *"Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."* In a dispute, the third-order effect is the broader alignment that forms around the original grievance. Enumerating only the direct parties is how that effect is missed.

**Falsifier:** a case where escalation is demonstrably driven by a first-order factor alone, with no reciprocal updating, no like-venue referrals, no aggregation, and no off-habitat cascade. That would break the reverse-loop reading and require a new census.

## Q4. What crosses the membrane

Fig 4 :21: **"The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a filter with a direction."** Fig 4 :22: **"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."**

A method that can be reapplied is eligible to cross upward. A recording of one success is not: Fig 4 :25, *"A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion."*

This is why the UN Peacemaker practice note *"Engaging at the Local Level: Options for UN Mediators"* and its case studies series are the figure-consistent form of export: they are methods and comparative cases, not a claim that one local settlement should be copied. Ostrom's *"multiple layers of nested enterprises, with small, local CPRs at their bases"* is the institutional form of the same filter.

But the round trip is lossy. Fig 4 :28: *"the trip is not reversible"*; Fig 4 :30: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."* A higher-court ruling carried back down will not match the local one; the gap is a measurement, not an error to be assumed away.

**Falsifier:** a ruling carried up and back down that reproduces local settlements with no loss of fidelity across independent sites. That breaks Fig 4 :28 and the membrane reading.

## CONCLUSION

Adjudicative and consensual processes are a plausible **isomer pair at the settlement level** (same atoms, different bond, different behaviour), with coercion correctly placed in the habitat and a stated falsifier. The mediation chain is **ordered by dependency**, and its middle link, deciding what counts as resolution, is the one most often assumed without an instrument. Escalation is the **reverse loop** (mutual update, mirror referral, aggregation, more process in one closure) plus a third-order habitat cascade. What should cross the membrane upward is a **method**, and the round trip has a nonzero gap.

This framing rescues the world literature's stage models and institutional design principles without letting any of them name a winning institution. `metric becomes the rule` remains an unproven binding; `adjudicative and consensual are isomers over a shared settlement atom set` is a checkable structural claim with a falsifier.
