# Structure, order and analogues in the build/acquire decision

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The question is posed as a two-way choice. Two things are owed: (1) determine the structure of that choice, that is, whether build and acquire are two members of one kind, two arrangements of a shared atom set, or two levers on different quantities; and (2) determine the order in which the governing questions must be asked. A further analogue question is whether an internal venture and an acquired capability that look the same after the fact are isomers or a functional group.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting derivation)

Atoms held for this derivation: a firm's capability slots (states in the business sense), the closure of the firm, the closure of a target frame, an arrangement (the venture), a primitive (the capability), and a habitat (team, routines, incentives, contacts). Operators held: `→` sequence, `⇒` production, `−` subtraction, `⋛` comparison, `¬` negation. No new primitive is introduced.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are build and acquire symmetric alternatives?

- Fig 6 :21: "MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."
- Fig 6 :14: "the only operator that moves the wall"
- Operators table :59: "Symmetry decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."

A symmetric bond has the property that swapping operands leaves the term unchanged. Build and acquire do not have that property: swapping them changes which wall moves, what is debited, and which failure mode applies. They are therefore not two spellings of one lever. They are ordered, directed operators acting on different quantities. Fig 6 :31: "Neither answer tells you much about the other. Run them as two tracks, and never read one against the other."

**Result:** not symmetric. **Falsifier:** show a case where swapping build and acquire leaves reach, cost and failure mode unchanged, with the ground settling both.

### Q2 — What is the structure of the choice: partition, union, or chain?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."

Three overlapping conditions were named in `01` §1: reachable new arrangement (build region), primitive held elsewhere (acquire region), habitat not reproducible (compound). Because they overlap, the structure is a union, not a partition. A lever applied to a case in two conditions clears only the single-condition part and leaves a categorically different residue. This is not a claim that firms cannot choose one route; it is a claim about the structure of the residual the route must answer.

Dependency supplies the chain inside the union (Fig 3 :20): "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Verdict before lookup before narrowing (Fig 9 :6-8, :43).

**Falsifier:** a well-measured set of venture decisions in which one lever governs every case with a non-updating ground, collapsing the union to a partition.

### Q3 — What order do the questions run in?

The Formula's step 7 names three questions. The order is fixed, and it is not an arbitrary preference:

1. **SEARCHED:** is the capability in here and unfound? "Asked FIRST — it is cheapest and the other two assume its answer." More search at the same reach, with independent searchers (Fig 6 :49-50).
2. **INWARD / OUTWARD:** if not, two opposed tracks. "inward: is our representation adequate to hold it?" extends the instrument; "outward: does another frame already hold it?" casts the residual in effect terms. "never read one against the other" (Fig 6 :31).
3. **SETTLE then COMPOSE:** an imported atom must first pay at the ground, then be composable over (Fig 5 :69-72). "a library that grows is not a reach that grows."

Separately, refusal precedes mint: Formula step 2 binds the sought-for shape as an ordinary term so it can be refused before a candidate is built. In business terms, write down what the venture would have to look like and what would refute it before choosing a route.

**Result:** a fixed partial order, with two opposed middle tracks that must not be collapsed into one score. **Falsifier:** a case where acquisition is correctly chosen without first determining whether the capability was in the firm's closure and unfound.

### Q4 — Are an internal venture and an acquired capability isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Fig 8 :50-52: "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them"

Two readings:

- **Weak reading (functional group).** A built venture and an acquired unit share the substructure `capability → market ground`, but their atoms differ (home-grown atoms versus imported atoms). Under Fig 12 :124 they are members of one functional group, qualified by origin. This reading is always available.
- **Strong reading (isomer).** If both are described over a common atom set, for example `{capability, team, routines, incentives, market contact}`, and differ only in the bond that attaches the imported primitive (import versus mint), then they are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The behavior difference is exactly the one Fig 8 :50 describes: identical contents, different provenance and different follow-up failure modes.

The strong reading is the checkable one and the one worth testing. The weak reading is the safe fallback. **Falsifier, written before the run:** if one route requires an atom the other does not, for example an acquired capability whose team cannot be reproduced internally, the strong isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. That is likely to be common, so the strong claim is narrow by construction.

### Q5 — What about a "catalyst" or a "cycle" in the venture decision?

- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

A venture unit that becomes a method supplier to later ventures is Fig 12's catalyst: it survives its own use, and it matters whether it is spent or regenerating. A portfolio in which each acquisition feeds the next is a cycle, and its sign decides whether it closes. These are descriptive structures, not recommendations. They are included because the question asks when to build, and "build once, then supply methods to many" is a different structure from "build each time from scratch". **Falsifier:** show that the catalyst or cycle reading predicts nothing distinguishable from the plain functional-group reading on a measured portfolio.

### Q6 — Two currencies, not one score

- Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."
- Fig 12 :126: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."
- Fig 9 :60: "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."

The cost of describing the venture and the cost of testing whether it holds are in different units. A short plan over a costly capability is cheap to state and expensive to settle; a long plan over a cheap capability is expensive to state and may cost one action to refute. Summing them into a single "build versus buy score" is the named error. A budget filter may decide what gets tried first; it may not decide which lever is right.

**Falsifier:** a decision process in which a single additive score predicts the ground outcome as well as the two readings kept apart, across many ventures.

## CONCLUSION

Build and acquire are not symmetric members of one choice. They are an ordered pair of operators on different quantities (inside reach versus the wall). The residual is a union of overlapping conditions with a fixed dependency order: verdict, lookup, settle and compose. The post-deal relation between a built and an acquired capability is a functional group in the weak reading and an isomer in the strong reading over a shared atom set. The strong reading has a stated falsifier and is falsified whenever one route requires an atom the other does not. Two prices stay apart; a single score is a filter at best.

This framing keeps what is defensible in the everyday question without pretending the figures return a number or a target. What the figures return is structure, order and a shape.
