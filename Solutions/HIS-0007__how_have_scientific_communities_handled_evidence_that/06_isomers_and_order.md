## DERIVATION

### Q1: Union or partition? CORRECTION TO MY OWN §3 of `05`

- Fig 9 :54: "*And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again.*"
- Fig 9 :57: "*a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound.*"
- Fig 9 :55: "*A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones.*"

"Reliable but difficult to reproduce" is a case in two conditions: ground contact and within-frame reach. It is a **union compound** with its own loop and its own anchor, not one hard question and not two separable questions. The correction to `05` §3 is that the missing condition can be **named** when it is a held atom (binding), even though the compound's shape cannot be reduced to a name. ### Q2: What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: "*The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it.*"
- Fig 3 :26: "*having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments.*"
- Fig 3 :27: "*A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at.*"

Order: perception (does the reading register?) → vocabulary (does the frame have terms for the instrument and procedure?) → objective (what counts as reproduction, per slot?) → planning (which differing frame?) → learn and carry (transmit method). The break is most often at vocabulary/objective, link 2 to 3, because that link has no obvious failures and no obvious tests. A community that buys better statistical power (link 4) when it has no terms for the material batch (link 2) is spending above the break.

### Q3: Are two failed replications isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "*isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour.*"
- Fig 12 :124: "*functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member.*"
- Fig 12 :94: "*The arrangement is the substance.*"

Two attempts over the same habitat atoms (same reading, same anchor, same protocol family) but with a different instrument or a different frame arrangement are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different substance. That is exactly why a second attempt in a differing closure can decide what a second attempt in the same closure cannot. If the two attempts share the method `state → instrument → reading` but differ only in which material batch fills the slot, they are members of a **functional group** (Fig 12 :124): same kind, different member.

The stronger claim, that two differently arranged attempts are isomers, is `specified`, unmeasured. The weaker claim, that they share a functional group, is the safe form. **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either attempt requires an atom the other does not (e.g. one measures a different phenomenon, so the anchor is not shared), the isomer claim fails and the two are not variants of one thing at all.

### Q4: What crosses between a lab and a community?

- Fig 4 :19: "*The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked*" (the line continues "*whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change.*")
- Fig 4 :22: "*Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down.*"
- Fig 4 :30: "*So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked.*"

The loop is invariant across one lab and a community of labs; only the cast changes. What crosses up is a method (a reusable protocol), never the recording of one success; what comes down is a head start (a starting apparatus or calibration), never a replay. The round trip between lab and community is lossy, so a community-level summary of the lab's work will not match the lab's practice, and the gap must be measured rather than assumed zero.

### Q5: Which currency decides?

- Fig 12 :125: "*A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add.*"
- Fig 12 :126: "*Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds.*"
- Fig 9 :60: "*Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict.*"

A published protocol is cheap in bits (a description). Checking whether it actually works is expensive in actions (running the experiment). A community that counts papers, or counts replications, is counting the bits and will not notice the shortfall in actions. The two readings come apart in both directions (Fig 12 :128-129), so both must be read or the choice is made blind. Summing them is the error Fig 12 :130 names: "*Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it.*"

## CONCLUSION

The residual is a **union compound**, not a partition, so it needs its own loop (Q1). Work in dependency order, with the break most often at the vocabulary/objective link (Q2). Two differing replications are isomers if they share atoms and differ only in arrangement, and members of one functional group if they share the method and differ in a qualifier; the isomer version is a checkable `specified` claim with a stated falsifier (Q3). Across scales carry methods and head starts, never recordings, and measure the round-trip gap (Q4). Price description and action in separate currencies and let neither become a verdict (Q5).
