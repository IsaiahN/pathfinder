# Cultures as isomers: structure, order and the closed set

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

`01` F4 and F5 claim that a general theory can hold a closed universal set while admitting deep cultural variation as arrangements over that set, and that two such cultures stand as isomers. Three questions are owed: (1) does the isomer definition actually apply across the individual and cultural scales, (2) what is the order of the composition, and (3) what would falsify the claim?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Does "closed set, open arrangements" transfer from Fig 13 to cognition?

- Fig 13 :115 "The set is closed. The arrangements are not."
- Fig 13 :118 "infinite effort inside a finite reach."
- Fig 13 :74 "Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."

The transfer is of the **form**, not the members. This was forced by strain S1 in `01`: Fig 13's six members are physical substrate terms, and treating them as cognitive universals would mint a seventh meaning. What transfers is the structural law: a finite generative set plus unbounded arrangements over it. In cognition the set is whatever universal primitives the ground settles. The arrangements are cultural forms.

**Re-census result:** legal as a structural analogue, not as a name. Triangulated (Fig 13 :115 plus Fig 12 :103 on level-relative irreducibility). **Falsifier:** a cultural form that requires a new primitive not composable from the universal set.

### Q2 : Are two cultures isomers?

- Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- Fig 12 :117 "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :97-98: the figures cite the chemistry precedent: "silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation. Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; named isomerism by Berzelius, 1830."

Two cultures whose cognitive atoms are the same but whose bonds differ are isomers by this definition. The behaviour differs decisively; the ingredient list does not record the difference. This is a stronger claim than "cultures share a functional group", which would only require a common substructure (Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member.").

Independent corroboration from chemistry, opened 2026-11-22: "Isomers do not necessarily share similar chemical or physical properties" (Wikipedia, "Isomer"). This is a different evidence pool from the figures and counts as corroboration of the *definition*, not of its application to cognition.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two cultures require different atoms rather than different arrangements of shared atoms, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. If a shared atom can be shown to be absent from one culture, the atoms are not shared.

### Q3 : What is the order of the composition?

- Fig 4 :19-20 "The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked, whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."
- Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom".
- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces".

The dependency order is:

1. **Perception / vocabulary (Fig 3 links 1 to 2):** describe the residual in effect terms before naming any frame (Fig 9 :27-28). Done in `01` section 1.
2. **Objective (Fig 3 link 3):** bind what an answer would look like as an ordinary term, so the sought-for shape accrues a residual (Formula, "Direction"). Done in the README.
3. **Settle first-order molecules (Fig 12 :104):** the cognitive capacities at the individual level must be settled before they become atoms for a cultural composition. Where they are not settled, the cultural composition is premature.
4. **Refuse before mint (Formula step 2):** refuse the reduction term (culture equals individuals) before minting the accommodation. `01` F3 does this.
5. **Compose at second order:** only then compose cultural forms as arrangements of the settled atoms.

A link whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can (Fig 3 :22-23). So if the individual-level atoms are not settled, a claim about cultural isomers is a reading of nothing, not a weak reading.

**Falsifier:** if a cultural form can be composed before the corresponding individual-level atoms are settled and still be checked against the ground, the dependency order is wrong.

### Q4 : Is the level boundary lossy, and does that matter?

- Fig 4 :27 "The transform between scales loses information, and that is the point."
- Fig 4 :30 "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."
- Fig 10 :68 "Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between."

The round trip here is: individual-level finding carried up to a cultural claim, then brought back down to a prediction about individuals. The gap R_T is nonzero. Na et al.'s r = 0.04 versus r = 0.73 is precisely such a gap: the cultural-level correlation does not come back down as an individual-level correlation. Treating the transform as identity would set R_T to zero and would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

The round-trip gap R_T is carried into `11_audit.md`, where it is enumerated qualitatively. It is not measured numerically, because no single instrument here reads both levels at once.

## CONCLUSION

The closed-set/open-arrangement form transfers from Fig 13 (Q1). Cultures built from shared cognitive atoms with different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117 (Q2), with chemistry as an independent corroboration of the definition. The composition is ordered: settle individual-level atoms, refuse the reduction term, then compose cultural forms at second order (Q3). The individual-to-cultural transform is lossy, and its round-trip gap is the honest measure of what a general theory gives up when it generalises (Q4).

This rescues the defensible part of the question without naming a universal set and without reducing culture to individuals. What remains unproven is which atoms are actually universal; that is a ground question, not a figure question.
