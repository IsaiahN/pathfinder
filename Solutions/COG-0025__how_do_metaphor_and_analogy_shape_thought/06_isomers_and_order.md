# Are two cross-tradition metaphors isomers? Structure, order, and analogues

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claim, `general` on the bond grammar. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).**

## RESIDUAL

Two traditions can use apparently the same mapping. Is that "same mapping" an identity, an isomer, or just a shared functional description? And what order does the mapping itself have: is the source-to-target relation symmetric (a bond like identity) or directional (a bond like sequence)? These are two questions about structure, and they are downstream of the mechanism settled in `01` D1.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the six substrate terms and, for this question, the relational vocabulary of Fig 12 (seven bonds plus negation). Bonds held: `+ ∥ ≡ → ⇒ − ⋛`. No new primitive is available without import. The question is whether two traditions' mappings share atoms under a different arrangement (isomer), share only a substructure (functional group), or merely share a label.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. What makes two mappings the "same"?

Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable"

So two mappings that name the same source and target domains are not thereby the same mapping. The bond between them is the missing half of the description. Cross-linguistic work that reports "the same conceptual metaphor in two languages" has recorded the ingredients; it has not recorded the bond unless it has shown which relations transfer and which do not. This is the structural reason for F5's provenance requirement: the difference between convergence and adoption is a difference in the record, not in the ingredients.

### Q2. Isomer or functional group?

Fig 12 :117 "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :124 "A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Take two traditions, A and B, each with a mapping from a concrete source domain to an abstract target.

- **Strong claim (isomer):** both mappings are written over the **full shared atom set**, for example the six substrate terms plus a shared set of relations, and differ only in which bond mediates which state pair. Then, by Fig 12 :117, they are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The behavioural difference is exactly what makes them non-identical.
- **Weak claim (functional group):** the two mappings share only a substructure, for example "concrete source to abstract target," while their atoms differ. Then, by Fig 12 :124, they are two members of a functional group: same kind, different member. The group says what kind; the qualifier (which source domain, which tradition) says which member.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a cross-linguistic census shows that the two mappings share no atom at all, not even a relational one, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. If it shows they share atoms and the same bond, the "different substance" claim fails and they are one term under two names (Fig 12 :92 "Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term").

This is a correction to my own first draft (recorded in `01` §6 as R3). I initially wrote that two cross-tradition metaphors are isomers because "same atoms, different bond, different substance." That only follows if the atom set is shared. It is not given by the fact that both are called metaphors. The narrower claim, functional group, is the one that survives without extra evidence.

### Q3. What order does the mapping have?

Fig 12 :71-74 defines sequence as ordered: "A then B; B needs A to have happened" with the test "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes." Identity (`≡`) is symmetric: "two names, one referent," test "substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."

A source-to-target mapping is **ordered** in the strong sense: swapping base and target does not preserve systematicity. Gentner (1983) makes this concrete with the systematicity principle: a predicate belonging to a mappable system of interconnected relations is more likely to be "imported into the target" than an isolated predicate. The atom(solar system) to target(atom) direction carries the central-force system; the reverse carries different structure. So the mapping is a `→` sequence (or better, a `⇒` production when the target did not previously hold the relational structure), not an `≡` identity.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a mapping where swapping source and target preserves the full system of relations and produces no new inferential change. Then the ordered-bond reading fails and the mapping must be recorded as identity (`≡`), which would also collapse the import claim in F2, since identity adds no access.

### Q4. Recursion: a settled mapping becomes an atom

Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."

Once a mapping is settled in a tradition (used, taught, and available for further inference), it becomes an atom for further composition. This is why a metaphor can generate a family of expressions: the first mapping is settled, then variants compose over it. It also explains the historical layering the cross-cultural literature reports: a mapping whose evaluation changes across periods (Zibin and Solopova 2024 report the "migrants as liquid" metaphor shifting from favourable to unfavourable evaluations in the UK across three periods) is a settled molecule being re-used as an atom at a later level, not a new primitive.

Fig 12 :103 "Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible." So the question "is this metaphor basic?" is a question about a level, never about the world. A primary metaphor in one tradition can be a derived molecule in another.

**Falsifier:** show a metaphor that is used in further composition without being settled against anything (no use, no transmission, no behavioural trace). Then the recursion claim does not apply to it and it should be recorded as a one-off, not an atom.

### Q5. Is there an analogue that is not a bond?

The operators table :70-78 and Fig 12 :134-140 make negation a unary operator, not a bond: it joins nothing and does not enter the bond price. For this question the relevant analogue is **refusal**: a tradition can refuse a mapping (mark it as dead, inappropriate, or wrong) without composing a replacement. A vocabulary with `−` (subtraction) can say which relation is missing from a mapping; only `¬` can say that the mapping is wrong. This matters because the question asks how metaphors shape thought, and a refused mapping shapes the boundary of what may be thought as much as an accepted one. The framework's own refusal of "a metaphor for intelligence" (Fig 11 :38) is an instance.

This analogue is a claim about the operator set, not about linguistic data, and is marked `general`.

## CONCLUSION

Two cross-tradition mappings are **isomers only under a demonstrated shared atom set**; otherwise the defensible claim is that they are members of a **functional group**. The mapping itself is **ordered** (base to target), not an identity, because swapping the operands does not preserve the systematic relations that make the mapping import structure at all. Settled mappings become atoms for further composition, so what counts as "basic" is level-relative. Each claim has a falsifier above. None of this names which particular metaphor is universal; that remains the `gap` recorded in F7.
