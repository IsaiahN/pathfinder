## Two things in the wider literature that bear directly on this question

1. **The "gap" versus "abundance" dispute is a union, not a contradiction.** Matthias (2004) gives the classic gap: *"nobody has enough control over the machine's actions to be able to assume the responsibility for them."* Kiener (2025) argues the opposite: *"there is responsibility abundance, that is, a situation in which numerous agents are responsible for the harm caused by AI, and that the challenge comes from the difficulties of dealing with such abundance in practice."* Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) disaggregate the singular term into *"not one problem but a set of at least four interconnected problems."* Under Fig 9 :54, these are two conditions of one compound: gap means no candidate meets the conditions; abundance means many meet them and cannot be handled. Picking a side is a single lever that leaves the intersections standing. The conflict sends the reader back to the census, it does not flip the verdict.

2. **The external institution is the operative anchor in practice.** The SEP entry notes that enforcement requires the affected party to *"identify the responsible legal entity, prove the action, perhaps prove intent, find a court that declares itself competent"* and get enforcement. NIST places governance and oversight with senior leadership and the board. EU AI Act Article 14 puts the authority to disregard, override or reverse the system's output in named natural persons. All three locate the settlement outside the artifact and outside the immediate decision, which is the figure-derived anchor rule at Fig 2 :36-37.
