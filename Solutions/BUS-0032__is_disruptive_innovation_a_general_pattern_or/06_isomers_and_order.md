# Isomers, order, and analogues in the disruption question

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the bond typing, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The ordinary either/or treats "general pattern" and "selective label" as two opposite answers over one object. The figure census shows at least four objects (mechanism, instrument, name, convention). Two supporting questions are owed:

1. When disruption accounts share ingredients, do they share one thing arranged differently (isomers), or a kind with different members (functional group), or genuinely one claim?
2. What is the **order** of the relationship: identity (≡), sequence (→), or threshold comparison (⋛)? The answer decides whether a retrospective label can be equated with a prospective one.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available from the market habitat (Fig 11 :43 *"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."*): cheaper offering, low-end or new-market foothold, incumbent overshoot, incumbent's high-margin customers, value-network asymmetry, business model, scale economies, legacy costs, regulatory barrier, entrant count, time.

Bonds available: the seven (three symmetric, four ordered) plus negation (Operators table). No new atom is available without import, and imports need a source gate and shadow test (Fig 6, Fig 8).

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are competing disruption accounts isomers?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Write the ingredients as a set: {cheaper offering, foothold, overshoot, high-margin customers, value-network asymmetry, business model}. Two accounts that bind them differently are isomers, not variants:

- **Account C (causal foothold):** `foothold ⇒ incumbent ignores ⇒ overshoot matters ⇒ displacement`. Here the foothold is a **production** bond (⇒: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*).
- **Account K (correlate of a business model):** `business model + value-network asymmetry ⇒ foothold is attractive ⇒ incumbent ignores`. Here the foothold is a **consequence**, not the driver. Christensen's own late correction is recorded in the Wikipedia article: *"it isn't that being at the bottom of the market is the causal mechanism, but that it's correlated with a business model that is unattractive to its competitor."*

Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The behaviour differs: Account C predicts that any low-end foothold will work; Account K predicts that only a foothold unattractive to the incumbent will work. King and Baatartogtokh 2015 report that in 60 of 77 cases (78%) experts thought incumbents were **not** overshooting customer needs, which is evidence against Account C's bond and is silent about Account K's.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single bonding of the ingredient set covers every documented case without a second arrangement, the isomer claim fails and the accounts are one claim with sloppy wording.

**Verdict:** `specified`, single-source on the figure definition of isomer, corroborated by the divergent bonds in the world sources. The world does not decide; it shows where to look.

### Q2. Is "disruptive" an isomer, a functional group, or a standing kind?

Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Fig 11 :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*

- As a **functional group**, "disruptive" names a kind of relation: entrant from a differing closure, incumbent channel decayed, displacement over time. The qualifier ("the 1970s disk-drive case", "Steam versus sail") identifies the member. This is figure-legal.
- As a **standing kind** ("these are disruptive companies", a list to be used across residuals), it fails Fig 11 :51. Uber is the recorded test case: by one reading it is disruptive, by Christensen's own later reading it is not, because the foothold was not the cause. The valence flipped with the residual, not with the firm.
- As an **isomer over a fixed atom set**, two specific disruption stories (say low-end foothold and new-market foothold) are isomers if they share the full atom set and differ only in which bond mediates which pair. This is the strong claim and is checkable; it is not what the ordinary term means.

**Verdict:** `specified`, triangulated (Fig 11 valence and Fig 12 functional group, different figures, low overlap). Refuter: a standing list whose members keep their valence across a documented residual change.

### Q3. What is the order of the term's history: ≡ or →?

The Operators table defines ≡ as *"two names, one referent"* with the test *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."* It defines → as *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* with the test *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*

The recorded history is a sequence, not an identity:

1. 1995, Christensen and Bower introduce **disruptive technologies** (Wikipedia reference list and usage history).
2. 1997, *The Innovator's Dilemma* studies disk drives and excavators.
3. 2003, *The Innovator's Solution* replaces "disruptive technology" with **disruptive innovation** because *"most technologies are not intrinsically disruptive or sustaining in character; rather, it is the business model that identifies the crucial idea"*.
4. 2014, Lepore and King/Baatartogtokh publish the retrospective-label and 9% critiques.
5. After 2014, Christensen's Uber correction moves the causal arrow from foothold to business model.

Substitute "disruptive technology" for "disruptive innovation" and something is lost: the second names the arrangement, the first names an ingredient. So the terms are not ≡. They are →, and each step remakes the term (Fig 10 :68: *"a different ground, and a lossy transform between"*). This matters: a term whose referent changes cannot be a settled atom, and a definition issued after the cases cannot be used to select the cases.

**Mode:** `specified`. **Status:** triangulated (Operators table plus Fig 10 level transform). **Falsifier:** a stable referent across the 1995 and 2003 usages, shown by a substitution test on the original texts.

### Q4. What is ⋛ doing here?

Fig 12 :87 defines comparison as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*. "Disruptive" is a threshold relation between the entrant's offering and the incumbent's paying ground, not an identity and not a conjunction. The Operators table: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one."* So the label carries a **goal**: the entrant must cross the incumbent's threshold. That is why the term is normative in practice (a firm "should disrupt"), which is a second reason to keep it a convention and refuse it as a verdict about what happened.

**Type strain, named not hidden:** at the habitat scale the operands of ⋛ are quantities (price, performance, margin). When the comparison is lifted to "two theories" or "two business models" the operands are molecules-now-atoms (Fig 12 :104 *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it"*). Whether those count as quantities at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. `08` and `11` carry the same note.

### Q5. Dependency order (Fig 3)

Perception to vocabulary to objective to planning to learn-and-carry.

- **Perception (link 1):** the low-end foothold is visible; incumbents often see it (King and Baatartogtokh: *"good managers face a dilemma... by doing the very things they need to do to succeed... they run the risk of ignoring rivals"*). So the break is not at perception.
- **Vocabulary (link 2):** the field has "disruption", "sustaining innovation", "overshoot", "value network". Link 2 works.
- **Objective (link 3):** no instrument states what would count as progress ex ante. Fig 3 :26: *"so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."* Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- **Planning and learn-and-carry (links 4 and 5):** book-length case histories are recordings of one occasion (Fig 4 :25), so carrying them up produces knowledge-like text rather than a reapplicable method. Guo et al. 2019 is a published attempt to build the missing instrument; its abstract notes that *"most of the documented methods tend to be case specific."*

**Order verdict:** the chain stops at link 3. Effort spent on more case studies (link 5) arrives at a step that cannot use it (Fig 3 :19-20). Refuter: an ex ante scoring rule that states its objective and is tested on held-out cases.

## CONCLUSION

The accounts that share disruption ingredients are **isomers** when the atom set is fixed and the bond differs, and a **functional group** when "disruptive" names the relation rather than a standing list. The term's own history is a **sequence** (→), not an identity (≡), so it cannot be treated as a settled atom. The comparison ⋛ is a threshold against a paying ground, which makes the term normative and therefore a convention until a ground settles it. The dependency chain stops at the objective link: no ex ante instrument is held.

## FALSIFIER

If any of the following holds, this supporting derivation is withdrawn:

1. A substitution test shows "disruptive technology" and "disruptive innovation" have the same referent (kills Q3).
2. One bonding of the ingredient set covers every documented case without a second arrangement (kills Q1).
3. A standing list of disruptive actors keeps its valence across a documented residual change (kills Q2).
4. An ex ante scoring rule states its objective, is tested on held-out cases and beats baseline against an independent ground (kills Q4 and the gap).
5. The ⋛ operands at second order are shown to be quantities without an additional instrument (removes the type strain, does not flip the main verdict).
