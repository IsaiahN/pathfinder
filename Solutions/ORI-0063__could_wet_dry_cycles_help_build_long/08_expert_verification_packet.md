# Expert verification packet: wet–dry cycles and long polymers (ORI-0063)

**What this is:** a claim in a form a prebiotic chemist or astrobiologist can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `specified` on the per-domain claims, `general` on the structural shape, `gap` on functional sequences. Not `grounded`.** No measurement is claimed by this packet. It is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

The question is a union of four conditions with different anchors, not one question with a yes or no answer. Polymerisation is composition by typed bonds, and a chain is the degenerate molecule with one bond type. Cycling explores arrangements over a fixed monomer set and adds no new monomer kinds. A wet–dry cycle is a signed loop: length accumulates only if dry-phase bond formation exceeds wet-phase hydrolysis and if something varies between cycles. The valence of water and of the cycle is relative to the residual and to the habitat, so regulated rehydration helps and overhydration destroys. Length is not function, because the bond arrangement carries information the ingredient list does not. Wet–dry cycling is a checkable contributor to bond formation and length accumulation under the right habitat, and it is not a sufficient account of functional long polymers. Which sequences become functional is a declared `gap`.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1 - Structure and closure (framework-internal, checkable by reading Figures 8 and 12)

- **Statement:** a polymer is a bonded arrangement; a homopolymer chain is the one-bond-type degenerate case; cycling rearranges a fixed monomer alphabet and cannot add a monomer kind.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :48, "A molecule is a bonded arrangement. A chain is the degenerate case: one bond type, no branching. Nothing about a chain is a different kind of object." Fig 8 :32, "no search, however long, adds an atom." Fig 13 :115, "The set is closed. The arrangements are not."
- **What to check:** is a glycine oligomer chain a one-bond-type arrangement under this definition? Does any claim in the packet require a new monomer to appear from cycling alone? (It does not.)
- **Refuter:** show a polymer whose monomers are not joined by typed bonds, or demonstrate a new monomer species arising from a closed starting set under mass balance. Either breaks C1.

### C2 - Loop sign and habitat valence (chemistry-facing, checkable against experiment)

- **Statement:** cycling is cumulative only with a positive loop sign and differing rooms; the effect of water is residual- and habitat-relative, so regulated rehydration helps and overhydration flips the valence.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :121, "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Fig 7 :36, "The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced." Fig 11 :49, "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries." Fig 10 :24, "the ground does not decay, the channel does."
- **What to check:** does a matched static control at equal integrated time and temperature erase the cycling advantage? Does length track the sign when wet-phase hydrolysis is varied? Does deliquescent, self-regulated rehydration outperform added water at the same conditions? Published checks: Higgs 2016 (theory), Campbell et al. 2019 (16.0% versus 1.9% yield), Boigenzahn et al. 2023 (kinetic selectivity).
- **Refuter:** a monotonic length gain under zero or negative loop sign with mass balance verified; or a cycle whose yield increases with water activity for every monomer set with no trade-off. Either breaks C2.

### C3 - Sequence, isomers and the gap (mixed, checkable by a function assay)

- **Statement:** cycling produces sequence isomers invisible to bulk composition; whether any is functionally decisive cannot be read from a yield table, so a sequence-discriminating instrument is owed.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 12 :94, "The arrangement is the substance." Fig 12 :117, "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 9 :32, "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."
- **What to check:** does the assay distinguish sequence isomers at the same bulk composition? Forsythe et al. 2017 report hundreds of proto-peptide sequences in four days and sequence isomers indistinguishable by mass alone. If a bulk length measurement predicted function on held-out chains, the gap would close.
- **Refuter:** show that bulk length predicts functional output without sequence information on a held-out set, or that sequence isomers at identical composition are behaviourally identical on every assay. Either breaks C3.

## 5. What would settle it

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument (cf. the spectrometer example in `05` section 4):

1. **Loop-sign test:** mean chain length after N cycles versus matched static control and versus a long-wet-phase control. Fig 12 :121.
2. **Hydration test:** deliquescent self-regulated rehydration versus uncontrolled water addition at matched conditions. Fig 11 :49.
3. **Sequence test:** method separating sequence isomers at the same bulk composition plus a function assay per named chain. Fig 12 :117.

Standing refuters from `01`: no cycling advantage in a matched control; no accumulation with short hydrolysis or accumulation with dominant hydrolysis; residual-independent valence; new monomer from a closed set; figure re-census breaking the chain or cycle definitions.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 12 :48, :94, :117, :121 and Fig 11 :49, five short passages.
2. Check C1: is the chain definition faithful to what a laboratory oligomer is?
3. Check C2 against your own knowledge or data: does the loop sign track length in the systems you know? Do you know of a case where overhydration increased yield?
4. Check C3: does your assay distinguish sequence isomers, or is a discriminator still owed?
5. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
