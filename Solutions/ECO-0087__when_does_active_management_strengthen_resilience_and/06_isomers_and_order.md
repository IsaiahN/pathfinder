# Managed and unmaintained as isomers, and the order of the management chain

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the definitions, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

## RESIDUAL

The audit refuses "active management" as one dial with two signs. Two supporting questions remain:

1. Are a managed habitat and an unmaintained habitat different substances over the same actors, or do they have different actor sets? This decides whether the relation is an isomer (Fig 12) or a composition plus import (Fig 6).
2. In what order does the management chain actually break, given that intervention effort usually lands at the action step? This decides where a checker should look first.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are managed and unmaintained habitats isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."

Fig 12 :95: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

A species list written as a flat inventory cannot tell the two modes apart. Both contain the same species; what differs is the bond. Under the seat, relations are maintained by introduction and instrument choice (Fig 11 :58). Without a seat, relations are maintained by selection alone (Fig 11 :36, :56). Same atoms, different bond, different substance: an isomer pair under Fig 12's definition.

This is not an analogy imported from chemistry. The figure itself grounds the definition in isomerism (Fig 12 :97-98: Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; Berzelius 1830). The open essay *The Recursive Self-Improvement Trap* states the ecological instance directly: "Natural selection is what the loop looks like when there is no one maintaining the ground... The same code runs when there is a Ground Maintainer. It runs in cultivation... It runs in habitat formation, when beavers build dams and change the selection pressures."

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one mode requires an actor the other does not (a species present only because the seat introduced it, or present only because the seat excluded a predator), the pair is not an isomer. It becomes a composition plus import, with a source gate and a shadow test owed (Fig 6 :23). The isomer claim is therefore conditional on enumerating the actor set and finding it shared. Mark `specified`, not `grounded`, until that enumeration exists for a named site.

**Triangulation:** Fig 12 :117 (bond definition) and Fig 11 :56/:58 (the two modes with the same habitat) are low-overlap routes to the same shape. The chemistry precedent is a third, outside witness.

### Q2 - Is the relevant actor a catalyst or a spent part?

Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

A maintained disturbance regime, a grazing regime, or a keystone actor that regenerates itself is a catalyst: it survives its own use. A one-off planting, a single cull, or a pulsed subsidy is a spent part. The figure says nothing records which a term is, so the distinction has to be written into the record at the moment of intervention. This bears directly on the question: "active management strengthens resilience" is more likely when the seat's work is carried by regenerating parts than by spent parts that must be paid again every season (Fig 12 :107-110 on re-deriving arrangements where the record does not cross a boundary).

**Falsifier:** a spent part that behaves across uses exactly like a regenerating part, with no re-payment and no re-derivation, would collapse the distinction. Measure the second and third use, not the first.

### Q3 - Sign of the loop: does management close a cycle or a chain?

Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

A self-regulating ecosystem is a cycle: the output of a trophic interaction re-enters as input, and the sign of the feedback decides whether it closes. A chain cannot close by construction. Active management can do either: it can reinstall a negative feedback (a restored predator that regulates grazing) or install a positive feedback (engineered sediment retention that amplifies itself). The novel-ecosystem source opened this session reports that "engineering principles have also been increasingly applied to ecological restoration projects by designing to mimic the activity of habitat-forming organisms and thus create positive feedback loops", with doubled transplant survival. That is a real case of the sign mattering.

**Falsifier:** demonstrate a management intervention that changes no feedback sign, only adds a link, and still converts a chain into a closed self-regulating cycle. By Fig 12 :121 that is impossible for a chain; a positive result would refute the cycle definition at this scale.

### Q4 - "Active management" is a functional group, not a kind

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

"Active management" names a functional group: the presence of a seat that acts fixes the kind. The qualifier (which actor introduced, which cascade enumerated, which anchor read) says which member. Fig 11 :49-51 then forbids reading the group as a standing list of good members: "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries" and "No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."

This resolves an apparent contradiction in the question itself. "When does active management strengthen" asks for the group; "when does it prevent adapting" asks for a specific member under a specific residual. They are different scopes, so no single answer can cover both.

**Falsifier:** a member of the group whose valence is invariant across every residual in a habitat, with the same mechanism and a non-updating ground. That would turn the functional group into a standing kind and refute Fig 11 :51.

### Q5 - The order of the management chain

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."

Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

Fig 3 :25: "The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."

Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

The five links, applied to a management program:

1. **Perception.** Can the program tell what is in front of it and distinguish it from what was there before? Instrument: per-slot monitoring (Fig 1 :19).
2. **Vocabulary.** Does it have terms for what the situation affords, including cascades out to the third order? Instrument: the habitat enumeration (Fig 11 :44).
3. **Objective.** Can it say what would count as progress in its own terms, without the target deciding the outcome? Instrument: a non-updating anchor (Fig 2 :29, Fig 10 :47).
4. **Planning.** Is there a route to that objective under incomplete information? Instrument: intervention with a source gate and shadow test (Fig 6 :12-14).
5. **Learn and carry.** Is what worked named, verified and carried as a method? Instrument: the round trip and its gap (Fig 4 :30).

Most programs spend at link 4 (plant, cull, reintroduce) and some at link 5 (report success). The chain usually stops at link 2 or 3: there is no vocabulary for the cascade, or the success metric is produced by the same frame that runs the program. A reading taken at link 4 below a break at link 3 is not a weak reading of the program; by Fig 3 :22 it is "a reading of nothing."

Fig 3 :29: "Before asking whether a system is good, ask which link it currently stops at, and whether the answer was measured or assumed."

**Falsifier:** a program whose interventions reliably improve the ground while its vocabulary link and objective link are demonstrably absent and unmeasured. That would refute the dependency ordering for this domain.

### Q6 - Two currencies, not one effort number

Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."

Fig 12 :126: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."

A plan can be cheap to describe and expensive to settle (a short species list that needs a decade of monitoring), or expensive to describe and cheap to refute (a long cascade inventory with one decisive measurement). Fig 12 :130: "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it." So "amount of management effort" cannot be the variable in the original question. The variable is the bond and the sign, not the summed cost.

**Falsifier:** a case where bits and actions are commensurable, so a single effort scalar predicts both description cost and settlement cost across interventions. That would refute the two-currency claim.

### Q7 - Order of composition: refusal before mint

`THE_FORMULA.md` step 2 sorts a residual into TRANSFERRED, NOVEL, BROKEN rebinding, BROKEN mechanism and REFUTED. A refused term pays the same bargain as a minted one: "NOTHING IS EVER DELETED, and the reach falls because something was ADDED." Applied here: when a program holds a false term ("this species list is the reference condition" while the ground shows a regime shift), the first move is a refusal, not a new intervention. Negation is not a bond (Operators table :70-72), so it cannot be expressed inside the intervention vocabulary. A vocabulary with subtraction but no negation can say what is missing from a habitat and cannot say that the target is wrong.

**Falsifier:** a vocabulary that can only accumulate and still manage to correct a false target without adding a refusal. That would refute the operator asymmetry.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed actor set, a managed habitat and an unmaintained habitat are isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance (Fig 12 :117), with the caveat that a named site must be enumerated before the fixed-set condition is established. The seat's actors divide into catalysts and spent parts (Fig 12 :119). The sign of the feedback loop decides whether a chain closes (Fig 12 :121), and active management is a functional group whose members get their valence from the residual, not a standing list (Fig 12 :123, Fig 11 :51). The management chain is ordered by dependency (Fig 3 :20), and most programs act above the break at vocabulary or objective. Description cost and action cost are held apart (Fig 12 :125).

This framing rescues what is checkable in the question without pretending the figures return a verdict on intervention. The thing to test is the bond, the sign and the broken link, not the amount of activity.
