# Supporting derivation: structure, order and analogues for the MMT disagreement

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks "how much" of one disagreement belongs to mechanisms and how much to policy priorities and risk judgments. Two things are owed: (1) is the structure a partition or a union, and (2) what is the dependency order, so that a verdict is not composed over a link whose input never arrived.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: the six substrate terms (Fig 13), the seven bonds plus negation (Fig 12), the anchor rule (Fig 2), and the split rule (Fig 9). No new primitive is imported.

- D1 Mechanism claims: actors are models, data, estimators; currency is prediction minus observation; anchor is the empirical ground.
- D2 Policy priorities: actors are governments, central banks, voters; currency is preference and value; anchor is human judgment.
- D3 Risk judgments: a union of a probability claim (D1 currency) and an acceptability claim (D2 currency); no single anchor.
- D4 The proportion: no actors that can settle it, no currency, no anchor; not a domain.

Union, not partition (Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is the structure a partition or a union?

Fig 9 :4-5: *"Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."*

Fig 9 :54-58: unbundle before searching again, and *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*

D3 shares a probability component with D1 and a value component with D2, so the overlap is real. Partition would require disjoint sets. Verdict: **union**, with D3 as the overlap compound.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a census of actual disputes finds no overlap, D3 dissolves and the structure is a partition. The split claim survives either way; only the union claim is at risk.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 :20-23: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."* And: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can. Which is why a reading taken below the break is not a weak reading. It is a reading of nothing."*

The order is: describe the residual (Fig 9 :27-28), split the union (Fig 9 :4-5), name the anchor for each part (Fig 2 :36-38), settle the mechanism claims on their ground, then compose the policy verdict (Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it"*). Attempting to price D2 before naming D1's ground is effort above the break.

### Q3 - Are the two parts "isomers" of one another?

Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

The two kinds of dispute can share atoms and differ in bond. "Inflation risk" appears in both: as a prediction in D1 and as a tolerated tradeoff in D2. Same term, different bond, different substance. So the figure's isomer definition applies **at the level of a dispute term**, not at the level of the two schools. It explains why a shared vocabulary hides the difference and why a single proportion would be a flat-formula error (Fig 12 :6-8: the flat formula *"collapse 6"* records ingredients and not arrangement).

**Falsifier:** if a named dispute cannot be written with the same atoms under two different bonds, the isomer reading fails for that dispute and only the functional-group reading remains (Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*).

### Q4 - What is the outside analogue, and where does it strain?

The positive/normative distinction (Wikipedia, read this session) is an outside analogue: positive economics *"focuses on the description, quantification and explanation of economic phenomena"*, normative economics on *"prescriptions for what actions individuals or societies should or should not take."* Low overlap with the framework, so it is corroboration, not authority.

The strain is Caplan and Miller's claim that *"many policy disagreements often described as value judgments are simply disagreements about facts"*, and Friedman's that policy disagreement is often *"an inability to agree about the likely consequences of a piece of legislation."* This blurs the D1/D2 bond. Re-census: the analogue is about how disputes are *classified*, not about whether a single currency can price them. The blur is an instruction to classify empirically, not a reason to merge the domains or accept a proportion. Strain recorded, verdict unchanged.

### Q5 - Why is D4 not a domain?

Fig 4 :30: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."* A proportion would be the concretisation of an abstraction that throws away the anchor difference. The round-trip gap is the entire answer: what was lost is the distinction between an empirical residual and a value choice. There is no instrument to measure that gap as a number, so no anchor can be named for D4. Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence."*

## CONCLUSION

The structure is a union of two anchorable domains plus one mixed compound (D3), and the "how much" term is not a domain. Dependency order: describe, split, anchor, settle, compose. The isomer reading applies to shared dispute terms under different bonds, which explains why a common vocabulary hides the split. One strain remains live (Q4), recorded rather than smoothed.
