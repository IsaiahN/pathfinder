## 0. The claim under audit

> The disagreement between MMT and mainstream macroeconomics is one disagreement with two measurable parts: an economic-mechanisms part and a policy-priorities-and-risk-judgments part. A correct answer states what fraction belongs to each.

The audit asks whether the figures license that framing. The short result: they do not. They require the question to be split before it is answered, they give each part a different anchor, and they forbid adding the two into one number. What survives is a shape and a set of per-domain verdicts, not a proportion.

## 1. Figure census

Describe R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain with actors, currency and anchor. A domain with no nameable anchor is not a domain yet. Partition or union, and order by dependency.

**R, the residual.** The persisted MMT versus mainstream disagreement. Arity: at least two conditions (mechanism claims, policy priority and risk claims, with risk itself mixed). Symmetry: not symmetric, the two kinds of claim do not range against the same anchor. Scale: first order at the level of individual claims about the economy, second order at the level of the schools themselves. Fig 9 :4-5 supplies the first test: *"Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."* This is a **union compound**, not one hard question (Fig 9 :57-58: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*).

| domain | actors | currency | anchor | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 Mechanism claims | competing models of money, deficits, inflation, full employment; data and model comparison | prediction minus observation, `R` per slot | empirical ground: data, natural experiments, model comparison that pays | domain, anchor nameable |
| D2 Policy priorities | elected governments, central banks, voters, distributional stakeholders | preference and value, what ought to be done | human judgment, because the question is constitutively about people (Fig 2 :36-38) | domain, anchor nameable |
| D3 Risk judgments | the same actors plus modelers | probability estimates mixed with value weighting | no single anchor: empirical probability belongs to D1, acceptable risk belongs to D2 | compound, not a domain until split |
| D4 The "how much" score | the two schools, or an outside arbiter | a single number meant to price both currencies | none: no instrument measures a proportion across two incommensurable currencies | not a domain |

**Partition or union.** Union, not partition. D3 overlaps both D1 and D2; Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

**Dependency order.** Split before searching (Fig 9 :4-5); describe the residual before choosing a frame (Fig 9 :27-28); settle mechanism claims on their own ground before composing the policy verdict; refuse the single proportion before minting one (Formula step 2).

## 2. Findings

### F1 - The "how much" framing asks a partition question of a union (decisive)

Fig 9 :4-5: *"Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."*

Fig 9 :54-58: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* and *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*

The Library of Babel essay (read this session) says the same thing in the search register: *"Perhaps your problem was a bundle or conflation of several problems masquerading as one? You will need to search all of those problems individually to find their answers and then combine them in a satisfactory way."*

The MMT debate fits the diagnostic: it has not shrunk with decades of argument, and each side keeps working well on a different subset. Palley's *"MMT lacks a plausible theory of inflation"* is a mechanism claim; Mankiw's *"its most novel policy prescriptions do not follow cogently from its premises"* is a policy claim. They are not the same test. A proportion presumes one sum to divide. The figures prescribe a split into separately settled compounds.

**Refuter:** show that the disagreement does shrink with effort against a single ground, or exhibit a census of the actual disputes in which every one lives in a single condition. Either would make partition legitimate.

### F2 - The two parts have different anchors (decisive)

Fig 2 :29-30: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks. A measurement, an experiment, a counter, a market, a patient outcome."*

Fig 2 :36: *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."*

Fig 2 :37-38: *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people. It is only an instrument for is this code correct, and instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure."*

Mechanism claims are constitutively about the world, so their anchor is empirical: data, model comparison, a natural experiment, a measurement that pays. Policy priorities are constitutively about people, so human judgment is the right anchor and data is only an instrument there. Risk judgments split: the probability of inflation is a world question, the acceptability of that inflation is a people question. Fig 2 :45: *"Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."*

**Refuter:** show that a policy priority is constitutively about the world rather than about people, or produce an instrument whose reading settles an "ought" without importing a value. (Caplan and Miller's claim that many value judgments are really fact disputes is handled in F8; it does not flip this verdict, it sends particular disputes back to classification.)

### F3 - The two currencies do not add (decisive for "how much")

Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."*

Fig 12 :130: *"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it. A price in bits and a price in actions are two readings, held apart."*

Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."*

Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*

A percentage that says "X percent mechanisms, Y percent policy" adds a description cost and a choice cost, or an empirical residual and a value judgment, in one number. The figures name that sum as the error. Fig 1 :27: *"A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."* Any proportion produced by one of the disputing camps is a frame-internal score.

**Refuter:** exhibit a single currency that prices both a mechanism claim and a policy priority without losing information, and show the instrument that reads it. None is named in the figures or in the sources read.

### F4 - Risk judgments are a compound, not a third single domain

Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

The risk-perception source (Wikipedia, citing Starr, Slovic and colleagues) defines risk perception as *"the subjective judgement that people make about the characteristics and severity of a risk"* and notes that *"Risk perceptions often differ from statistical assessments of risk"*. So the empirical probability and the judged severity come apart. D3 therefore has no single anchor until it is split into a probability claim (D1 ground) and an acceptability claim (D2 ground). A domain with no nameable anchor is not a domain yet.

**Refuter:** show that risk judgments are purely statistical (then they fold into D1) or purely valuational (then they fold into D2). The source read supports neither reduction.

### F5 - The debate's persistence is a diagnostic, not a measurement

Fig 7 :12-13: *"same closure, handed along; agreement compounds, evidence does not"*

Fig 8 :39: *"A search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."*

The MMT article reports Carnevali and Fontana's finding that *"much of the debate over MMT has taken place outside conventional scholarly settings"*. That is consistent with a union that keeps being searched as one question. It is not itself a measurement of the proportion. Fig 9 :60: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."* A poll showing that few economists agree with MMT is a filter for attention, not a witness for the split.

**Refuter:** provide a time series in which the number of live mechanism disputes falls under one empirical protocol while the number of live value disputes stays flat. That would make the persistence measurable rather than merely diagnostic.

### F6 - What survives is a per-domain verdict with a named ground

Fig 2 :42: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."*

Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

The same term, "inflation risk", is a different substance depending on the bond: an empirical prediction in D1, a tolerated tradeoff in D2. Fig 13 :133: *"what it returns is a shape rather than a name"*. What the framework can return here is the shape of a correct answer: a split, an anchor per part, and a verdict per part kept separate. Fig 10 :52: *"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."*

**Refuter:** none within the framework. This is the prescribed output, not a claim about the world. An outside refuter can reject the shape by showing that the split cannot be made for a particular named dispute.

### F7 - The refusal is out of order in the question as asked

Formula step 2 sorts a residual by what is missing or what is wrong, and names refusal as a distinct branch: *"REFUTED: a term held is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation"*. The question holds the term "one disagreement with two measurable parts" and asks for an explanation of its proportions. The figures require the refusal first: the held term is false, so what is owed is a refusal, not an apportionment. Fig 5 :29-30: *"nothing new here"* and *"the machinery worked; the answer was already known"*. A more careful literature search cannot mint the missing anchor.

**Refuter:** show that the held term is not false, that is, that a single measurable disagreement really exists. Then the refusal is withdrawn and the proportion becomes owed.

### F8 - A genuine conflict from the world, sent back to the census (honest strain)

The Wikipedia article on positive and normative economics reports Caplan and Miller's claim that *"many policy disagreements often described as value judgments are simply disagreements about facts"*, and Friedman's view that *"disagreements about economic policy are primarily due to an inability to agree about the likely consequences of a piece of legislation"*.

This conflicts with a clean D1/D2 partition at the level of particular disputes: some disputes filed as "policy priority" may be mechanism disagreements in disguise. Per the standing rule, a conflict sends the work back to the census and never flips the verdict. Re-census result: the conflict **strengthens F1**. It says the classification of individual disputes is empirical work, not a stipulated split. It does not license a single proportion, because a dispute that turns out to be a fact dispute simply moves to D1 and is settled there. Strain recorded, not hidden; verdict unchanged.

**Refuter:** if it were shown that every apparently normative dispute reduces to a fact dispute, D2 would have no anchorless residue and the question would become a one-domain empirical census. The source does not claim that.

### F9 - Some mechanism disputes really do have a live ground

Palley's *"MMT lacks a plausible theory of inflation"* and the 2024 model comparison by Liu, Minford and Ou, which found that the MMT policy mix *"would substantially destabilize output, undermine overall macroeconomic stability, and reduce household welfare"*, are mechanism claims with a possible ground. By contrast Mankiw's *"MMT contains some kernels of truth, but its most novel policy prescriptions do not follow cogently from its premises"* separates a mechanism concession from a policy verdict.

This matters for honesty: the answer is not "the framework says nothing about mechanisms." It says D1 has a landable ground and D2 does not have an empirical one. Fig 2 :36-38 governs the distinction. Verdict: D1 is a domain with a nameable anchor; D2 is a domain with a different anchor; D3 is a compound; D4 is not a domain.

**Refuter:** exhibit a policy-priority dispute that an empirical instrument settles without first importing a value. Caplan and Miller's reduction claim would be the candidate, but it converts the dispute into a fact dispute rather than giving D2 an empirical anchor.

## 3. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per Seat Map :287: *"And a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."*

The shape claim is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. The MMT versus mainstream disagreement is shown to shrink with effort against one ground, so Fig 9 :4-5's split test fails and it is one question after all.
2. A single currency is exhibited that prices mechanism claims and policy priorities without loss, so Fig 12 :125-130 fails and a proportion becomes well formed.
3. Policy priorities are shown to be constitutively about the world rather than about people, so the D2 anchor rule in Fig 2 :36-38 fails.
4. A census of the actual disputes shows every one lives in a single condition, so the union reading in Fig 9 :55 fails.
5. An instrument is produced that settles an "ought" claim without importing a value, so D2 acquires the empirical anchor the audit says it lacks.

If any fires, the shape is withdrawn. Until one is run, nothing here is labelled `grounded`.

## 4. Recommendation

1. Do not answer with a percentage. Answer with a split and a per-domain verdict (F1, F3).
2. Name the anchor for each part before naming the dispute (F2). Mechanism disputes go to data; policy disputes go to deliberation about values; risk disputes are separated into probability and acceptability (F4).
3. Treat the persistence of the debate as a diagnostic for a union, not as evidence for a proportion (F5).
4. Refuse the held term before trying to mint a number (F7).
5. Classify particular disputes empirically, because the Caplan and Miller conflict shows the D1/D2 boundary is not always where it is first drawn (F8).
6. Send the split method and the refuter, not the conclusion of either school (Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."*).

### Seven-question recursive derivation (summary)

1. **Is there a fact here?** No single fact. Fig 9 :4-5: the disagreement does not shrink and each side works on a different subset, so split.
2. **Partition or union?** Union. D3 overlaps D1 and D2 (Fig 9 :55).
3. **What anchors D1?** The empirical ground (Fig 2 :29-30, :36-38). Settled by what pays.
4. **What anchors D2?** Human judgment, because the question is constitutively about people (Fig 2 :37-38). Data is only an instrument there.
5. **Can D4 be minted?** No. No support, no reach, no novelty in a single currency (Formula step 3); the refusal is owed first (Formula step 2).
6. **Settlement?** None. This is a `specified` shape. Fig 2 :45 frames range against the ground; no ground reading was taken here.
7. **Route?** SEARCHED first (more literature cannot add the missing anchor, Fig 6 :23); INWARD would sharpen our own terms; OUTWARD would import a frame that already predicts the shape. The shape needed here is achieved by splitting, not importing.

### Updated verdict

The question as asked is refuted. The disagreement is a union compound, the mechanism part and the policy part have different anchors, and their currencies do not add. What survives is a shape: split first, settle each part on its own ground, keep the verdicts separate, and never report a proportion as though it were a measurement. Strains after re-census: one, the Caplan and Miller reduction claim in F8, recorded and not hidden.
