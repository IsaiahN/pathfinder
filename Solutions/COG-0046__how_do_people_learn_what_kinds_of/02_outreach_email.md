**Subject:** Check one claim about how desires become "acceptable" before you decide whether the framework is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I am working on a narrow question: how people learn which desires and ambitions count as acceptable or admirable. I have one structural claim I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree. If the claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the learning is indexed per kind of desire, not averaged into one "well adjusted" score. The figures put it plainly: "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated" (Fig 1 :19) and "The gap is measured per object, not per step. A system that averages across objects has thrown away the only signal that says where to look" (Fig 1 :28). Two further constraints follow. Capability belongs to the person-and-habitat, not to the person alone: "A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves" (Fig 11 :40). And what crosses generations is a way of doing, not a recording of one success: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down" (Fig 4 :22).
>
> I also unbundle the question. "Acceptable" is a threshold and "admirable" is an ordering; they are two overlapping conditions, not one hard question. Fig 9 says to split a union before searching it. The figures do **not** say which desire, which weight, or which age. They return a shape, not a name: "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself" (Fig 9 :32).
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that I have discovered the mechanism, that the six-term substrate settles a developmental question, or that this is `grounded`. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a measurement. The specific answer is a recorded `gap`, not an omission I am hiding.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim (written before this run):**
> 1. evaluative learning is well modeled as a single global scalar with no per-desire structure, so Fig 1 :19 does not apply;
> 2. a learner's capability is improved in isolation with no change in contact, which Fig 11 :40 rules out;
> 3. "acceptable" and "admirable" reduce to one scalar, so the union split is wrong;
> 4. a culture is found in which only a recording, not a method or head start, crosses generations.
>
> If any holds, I will withdraw or narrow the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Canonical figures README (usage rule and "unreachable is a verdict"): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md` (local copy of the public `general-agent` branch document).
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers: `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`.
> - The moral-learning corpus this question sits in: Haidt, Koller and Dias (1993), "Affect, culture, and morality, or is it wrong to eat your dog?", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 65(4), 613-628. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8229648/
> - The transmission frame: Richerson and Boyd, "Culture Is Essential", excerpt from *Not by Genes Alone* (2005), University of Chicago Press. https://press.uchicago.edu/Misc/Chicago/712842.html
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures folder. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. The framework's own rule is that a frame cannot check the direction of its own question, and the method essay warns that outputs could be "contrived to fit the formula" unless a ground settles them. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
