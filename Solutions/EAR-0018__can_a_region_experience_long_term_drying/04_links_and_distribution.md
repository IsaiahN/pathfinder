## What the wider literature adds, and what it does not

1. **It supplies the physical frame that the global mean cannot supply.** The atmospheric energy budget constrains the global mean to roughly 1-3% per K, while atmospheric water vapour rises around 7% per K under Clausius-Clapeyron; the difference is closed by a weaker circulation and by moisture transport (Held and Soden 2006; IPCC AR6 WG1 Ch8).
2. **It names the break in scale.** Dagan and Stier 2020 put the transition near 3000 km; Dagan, Stier and Watson-Parris 2019 put a water-budget closure scale near 3500-4000 km. Above it, precipitation change approaches the small global mean value; below it, divergence terms permit much larger changes of either sign. This is the physical counterpart of the framework's cross-scale round trip (Fig 4), not a figure-derived number.
3. **It refuses the easy slogan.** IPCC AR6 WG2 Ch4 calls "wet gets wetter, dry gets drier" too simplistic; Wills et al. show the thermodynamic scaling gets the wrong sign over a large fraction of the globe. A regional dry trend is therefore not a paradox even in the naive sense: the aggregate rule was never a local rule.
4. **A low-overlap witness.** Simpson's Paradox (SEP) is a mathematics/statistics result about association reversal under partitioning. It corroborates the figure's per-slot rule (Fig 1 :19, :22) from a field with no connection to the framework or to climate science. Agreement here is meaningful because the evidence pools do not overlap; it is still corroboration only, never the verdict.
5. **It does not supply the verdict.** None of these sources is used to overrule a figure line. Where a source could be read as saying "the global mean determines regional outcomes," the conflict rule sends the reading back to the census, not to a revised verdict.
