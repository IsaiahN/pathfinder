# Bonds, isomers, and order: a supporting derivation for knowledge exchange

**Mode: `general` on the structural claims, `specified` on the analogues. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading** (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

`01_figure_grounded_audit.md` establishes that influence is import, gated by provenance and a shadow test. Two questions remain for the supporting derivation:

1. When an atom crosses between knowledge systems, **what kind of bond** carries it, and does the bond change the substance? Ingredient lists cannot answer this.
2. Are two knowledge systems that hold the same ingredients arranged differently **isomers**, or merely variants of one thing? And does the order of a transfer matter?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: any term a system can use as an operand (an observation, a category, an instrument reading, a practice). Bonds held: the seven typed relations plus negation (Operators table). No new primitive is available from the figures themselves. Currency: composition inside a closure and settlement against the ground. Anchor: the ground, not the similarity of the two descriptions.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - An import is a bonded arrangement, not an ingredient list

Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."** Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."** Fig 12 :95: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing."**

Consequence. Saying "system B borrowed concept X from system A" names an ingredient and records half of the event. The bond matters: was X *joined* to B's existing terms (conjunction), did it *produce* a new term in B (production), was it placed *before* them (sequence), or did it *refuse* one of them (negation, which is not a bond at all)? Two frames can hold the same atom under different bonds and thereby hold different substances. This is why a translation that preserves the vocabulary can still change the knowledge: it re-bonds the atoms. Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**

**Refuter:** show that the bond between an imported atom and its host terms is recoverable from the two terms alone. If it is, the arrangement carries no information and Fig 12 fails at this scale.

### Q2 - Two systems with the same ingredients are isomers, not degrees of one another

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Apply to two knowledge systems that hold the same set of ingredients, say a category for a plant, a rule for harvesting it, and a seasonal observation.

- If both systems hold the same atoms but bond them differently (harvest rule *produces* the seasonal category in one, is merely *joined* to it in the other), they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different substance. They are not "the same knowledge at different levels of development".
- If the systems share only a substructure (both have a harvesting rule, but the other atoms differ), the correct claim is the weaker **functional group** one: *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

This matters for the question's "unequal" half. A common way to flatten a knowledge system is to record it as an ingredient list: "they had the same observations, they just lacked the theory." That phrasing makes two isomers look like one substance at two stages, which the figure forbids. The difference is *"decisive in the behaviour"*, which is checkable at the ground.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two systems sharing a full ingredient set can be shown to behave identically under every test their own ground can run, the isomer claim fails for that pair and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3 - Order is not always a difference

Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** Operators table :59: **"Symmetry decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** Under a symmetric bond (+, ∥, ≡), k operands have k! spellings and one term; under an ordered bond (→, ⇒, -, ⋛), different orders are different terms.

Consequence for history. A report that system B's terms appear in a different order from system A's may be reporting nothing (if the bond is symmetric) or a different substance (if the bond is ordered). The figures do not let you assume which. The bond must be identified, and its test is per the Operators table: swap the operands and ask whether the meaning changes. For a knowledge system this is an empirical check on its own practices, not a rhetorical preference.

### Q4 - The round trip when a concept travels

Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

1. **Sent up (x):** the concrete practice in the source system, with its bonds and its ground.
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** an effect description, because *"The description travels. Fails by being sharpened into terms only we can read. Cast it in effect terms"* (Fig 6 :30).
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** the practice reinstalled in the receiving system, re-bonded to that system's terms and checked against that system's ground.

**R_T is nonzero in general**, because the transform is lossy (Fig 4 :28-29). What survives cleanly is the effect description. What can fail to survive is the arrangement: the same ingredient list, re-bonded, yields a different substance. Fig 12 :107: **"Settling compresses the description and not what it cost to establish."** Where the record of the arrangement does not cross, the receiving lineage pays the establishment cost again, or silently substitutes a different substance. This is the structural form of "what you failed to reproduce, and what you brought with you" (Fig 11 :28-30).

## CONCLUSION

An import is a bonded arrangement, not an ingredient. Two knowledge systems sharing a full ingredient set are isomers if their bonds differ, and members of one functional group if only a substructure is shared. Order matters only under ordered bonds, which must be identified by their tests rather than assumed. A concept that travels across systems incurs a nonzero round-trip gap R_T, consisting of what the coarser effect description cannot hold, chiefly the arrangement. This sharpens F2 and F5 of the main audit without adding any new primitive.

## FALSIFIER (standing)

If any of the following holds, this supporting derivation is withdrawn:

1. The bond between an imported atom and its host terms is recoverable from the terms alone (breaks Q1 and Fig 12 :46).
2. Two isomer systems with the same atoms and different bonds behave identically under every ground test available to them (breaks Q2).
3. A transferred effect description reinstates the original arrangement with R_T = 0, with no lossy transform and no re-payment of the establishment cost (breaks Q4 and Fig 4 :28-30).
