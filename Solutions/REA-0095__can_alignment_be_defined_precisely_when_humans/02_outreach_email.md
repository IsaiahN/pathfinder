**Subject:** One checkable claim about defining alignment under disagreement

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The residual, in effect terms.** We need to say what "aligned" means while the people served by a system disagree about values, goals, and acceptable risk. Two things can be meant by "defined precisely": a shared convention that coordinates people, and a ground-settled verdict about what the system should do. These have different anchors and different failure modes.
>
> **The output (`general` on the shape, `specified` on the split, not `grounded`).** The framework's own alignment figure says *"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you"* (Fig 2 :35). An anchor is *"whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks"* (Fig 2 :29). From that and the per-slot rule (*"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated,"* Fig 1 :19), the following shape follows:
>
> 1. Precision is available as a **convention**: a shared meaning that makes the disagreement statable and can be violated. It is not a verdict. Fig 2 :41 calls agreement about a shared meaning *"efficient for a convention,"* and Fig 10 :47 says *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one."*
> 2. Precision is **not** available as a single verdict over contested values by aggregation or negotiation. Arrow's theorem blocks the aggregation under its axioms; the figure blocks the negotiation because a mirror cannot correct you and a moving target cannot be an anchor.
> 3. The question is a **union**, not one hard question. Values and acceptable risk have different anchors and must be unbundled before searching (Fig 9 :54).
> 4. The remaining disagreement is kept **per slot**. Aggregating it into one score is how the live signal disappears into an average (Fig 1 :22).
> 5. A precise local shape per deployment is possible against a non-updating ground with instruments. The framework returns a **shape**, not a name for the correct value set (Fig 9 :32).
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that this settles which values a deployment should serve; that averaging preferences gives the answer; that a constitution or reward model is grounded merely because it is precise; that human judgment is the right anchor for an instrument question such as an acceptable failure rate. Human judgment is the right anchor only where the question is constitutively about people (Fig 2 :37).
>
> **What would refute the output.** I wrote these before writing the conclusion:
> 1. an aggregation procedure satisfying the axioms that is ground-confirmed on a contested real case;
> 2. an anchor that updates on what frames think and still settles the question, confirmed by a separate instrument;
> 3. one lever that closes both the value and risk conditions with no residual in their intersection;
> 4. a global alignment score from which all per-slot disagreement can be reconstructed losslessly;
> 5. a specific value ordering derived from the figures alone with no import and no ground.
>
> If any holds, I will retract the corresponding claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Figures and formula for direct download: https://github.com/IsaiahN/tether/tree/general-agent/figures
> - Worked alignment essay by the framework author: https://adventuresinml.substack.com/p/the-recursive-self-improvement-trap
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it. The framework's own limit is that a loop cannot check the direction of its own question, so an unwritten prediction is invisible to every check it runs. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
