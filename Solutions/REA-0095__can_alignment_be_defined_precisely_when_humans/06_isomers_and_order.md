# Isomers, unions, and the order of operations

**Mode: `specified` on the structure and the order, `general` on the isomer reading. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).**

## RESIDUAL

The main audit split "defined precisely" into a convention sense and a verdict sense. Two supporting questions remain: (1) are those two the same thing under different names, or genuinely different substances? (2) in what order must a definer operate, so that precision does not get spent before the ground is reachable?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting)

Atoms held for both senses: a statement of desired behaviour, a statement of agreement among parties, a record, and a check. Bonds available: the seven typed bonds plus negation (Fig 12, Operators table). The distinguishing bond is the one each sense has to the **ground**: a convention is bonded to shared meaning; a verdict is bonded to an outcome that does not move. No new primitive is needed for either.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are a convention and a verdict isomers?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*
Fig 8 :50: *"But shared atoms are not the signature. Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence."*

A written alignment rule and an experimentally settled alignment claim can contain exactly the same sentences, the same signatories, and the same test suite. Ingredients match. What differs is the bond to the ground:

- Convention: the bond is to shared meaning. Fig 2 :41: *"efficient for a convention: agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it."* Change the parties' agreement and the convention changes legitimately.
- Verdict: the bond is to an outcome. Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* Change the parties' agreement and the verdict must not change.

So under Fig 12 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference invisible in the published text and decisive in behaviour. The failure mode named in Fig 2 :43 and Fig 10 :47, *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one,"* is exactly the moment the isomer is mislabelled: the bond was swapped while the ingredients stayed the same.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a precise rule can be shown to be both a convention and a verdict with no change in what may move it, that is, if swapping the bond to the ground changes no behaviour and no obligation, the isomer reading fails and the two senses are one thing under two names. A single documented case would suffice.

### Q2: What is the order of operations?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

The definer's chain, in dependency order:

1. **Perception (Fig 3 :4 to :5).** State each contested item per slot. Instrument: the per-slot residual list (Fig 1 :19). If this link is missing, no later step can see where the disagreement lives.
2. **Vocabulary (Fig 3 :7).** Have terms for what each frame affords, including a refusal term. Fig 12 :112: *"A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."* A vocabulary that cannot say "this rule is wrong" can only accumulate rules.
3. **The objective (Fig 3 :10).** Say what would count as progress without moving the anchor. This is the break in this question. The usual failure is to define the objective as "reach agreement," which makes the anchor the frames themselves and triggers Fig 2 :34 coherent drift.
4. **Planning (Fig 3 :12).** Choose a route under incomplete information: split the union, then handle each condition with its own anchor.
5. **Learn and carry (Fig 3 :15).** Name what worked, verify it, carry the method, never the recording (Fig 4 :22).

**Ordering constraint from the formula.** Formula step 2 says a refusal may be owed before a mint. Applied here: if the current definition contains a term that keeps producing a flat, non-declining residual, refuse it before composing a new definition over it. Building a more precise version of a wrong definition is work above the break.

**Ordering constraint from Fig 9.** Fig 9 :45: *"Describing the residual is the expensive part. Leaving, looking up and recognising are one step each, and cheap only in that order."* Describe the disagreement, then look for the frame or instrument, then recognise the fit. Choosing the frame first is the inversion named as apophenia in other solutions.

### Q3: Why the union cannot be treated as a harder single question

Fig 9 :56: *"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*
Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
Fig 9 :58: *"It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."*

The value-plurality condition (D1 plus D2) has no internal anchor and is bounded by the aggregation proof. The acceptable-risk condition (D3) has a possible ground in measured outcomes. Their intersection is a case where people disagree about which risks count *and* the instrument is contested. That intersection is the specific compound that needs its own loop; it is not solved by getting more precise about either side alone.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a deployment is exhibited where one precise rule closes both conditions and no residual remains in the intersection, with a single non-updating anchor, the union claim fails for that class of cases.

### Q4: Does the recursion change anything?

Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."* (truncated at the sentence boundary; the SVG line continues with "and there is no bottom").

A convention that has settled a coordination question becomes an atom for the next question: a standard, a test suite, a reporting format. It may then be composed over. But Fig 5 amendment 2 warns that settling is necessary and not sufficient: *"a library that grows is not a reach that grows."* Adding more precise conventions does not by itself make the value question reachable. That still needs a ground or a provenanced import.

## CONCLUSION

The convention sense and the verdict sense of "defined precisely" are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: identical ingredients, different bond to the ground, different substance. The order of operations is fixed by dependency: state the residual per slot, acquire a refusal term, then set the objective against a non-updating anchor, then plan, then carry methods. The value and risk conditions form a union whose intersection needs its own loop. Nothing in this derivation names a value set; that remains the gap recorded in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`.
