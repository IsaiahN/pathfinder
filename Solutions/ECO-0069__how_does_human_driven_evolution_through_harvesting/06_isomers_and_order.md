# Isomers, catalysts, cycles and order in ecosystem relationships

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

`01` showed that human-driven evolution alters the *relation* rather than necessarily the actor list, and that the three pressures form a union. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. If the species list is unchanged, what exactly is different between the before and after states? Is this the figure's **isomer** case?
2. What is the dependency order of the derivation, and can the framework write a route to the changed state rather than only its ingredients?

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are two ecosystem states with the same species list isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 11 :50: **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."**
- A before state `{Species A, Species B, ...}` with relation `A benefits B` and an after state with the same species set and relation `A harms B` differ only in the bond. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, not variants of one thing.
- Their species list is the formula. The formula cannot tell them apart; the bond does. This is exactly the case Fig 12 :94 names: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two ecosystem states can differ in relationships while keeping all actors, all bonds and all conditions fixed, the isomer claim fails; only abundance or state values changed, and the isomer frame is the wrong frame.

**Status:** triangulated for the structural claim (Fig 12 definition plus Fig 11 valence rule); single-source and unmeasured for any particular ecosystem.

### Q2: Which of the three pressures is a catalyst?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- A contaminant that persists and biomagnifies is the clearest catalyst case: it is not consumed by producing its effect, and the same molecule acts again at a later step. The figure's own DDT example (Fig 11 :52-53) has this shape. The published record corroborates: "The bald eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus) population in North America declined greatly after World War II due primarily to the eggshell thinning effects of p,p'-DDE, a biodegradation product of DDT." (Bowerman et al. 1995, *Environmental Health Perspectives*, opened 2026-11-23)
- A size-selective harvest regime is also catalyst-like in a different sense: the *regime* survives each harvest event, while the individuals it acts on do not. What persists is the operator, not the operand.
- **Falsifier:** a pressure whose effect is strictly one-shot and whose actor is spent in producing it, with no re-entry into the system. Then the catalyst label is wrong and the pressure is a single event bond.

**Status:** single-source for the structural mapping (Fig 12), with the DDT case corroborated by a low-overlap world source.

### Q3: Is there a cycle, and what decides its sign?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- Harvest removes large individuals, which selects for earlier maturation, which changes the size structure, which changes which individuals the same gear removes. The output re-enters as input. This is an eco-evolutionary feedback, not a one-way chain. The world literature opened for `01` describes the same structure: a decade-long size-dependent harvest experiment found "heritable changes towards early maturation and high fecundity" (Diaz Pauli et al. 2026, *Evolutionary Applications*), and the paper explicitly notes that the ecological consequences of such life-history change "at the population and ecosystem levels" are still largely unknown.
- The sign decides whether the loop closes destructively or stabilises. The framework does not decide the sign in advance; the ground does.
- **Falsifier:** a documented harvest or pollution response in which the changed trait has no effect on subsequent exposure, so the loop cannot re-enter.

**Status:** triangulated for the cycle form (Fig 12 :121 plus Fig 7 :35, the ground votes between rooms); single-source and unmeasured for the sign in any named system.

### Q4: Are the three pressures a functional group?

- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- All three pressures share the substructure `a seat acts on a habitat and moves the residual`. That fixes the kind: human-driven evolution in the maintained mode (Fig 11 :58). The qualifier, harvesting versus pollution versus habitat change, says which member.
- This is a different claim from isomerism. The three members share a functional group; two *states* of one system with the same atoms and different bonds are isomers. Both can be true at once, and neither licenses averaging the three members.
- **Falsifier:** a pressure that does not act through a seat on a habitat at all, which would fall outside the functional group and require a separate domain.

**Status:** triangulated (Fig 12 functional group plus Fig 9 :54-57 union rule).

### Q5: What is the order of the union, and can the framework write a route?

- Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**
- A plan is a sequence. Operators table :31: **"A then B; B needs A to have happened."** The order that matters here is dependency, not chronology: Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- Ordered derivation for this question:
  1. enumerate the habitat outward until the cascade stops mattering (Fig 11 :44);
  2. identify the live slot, not the global average (Fig 1 :19);
  3. describe the residual in effect terms before naming a frame (Fig 9 :27-28);
  4. separate the management convention from the ground (Fig 10 :47);
  5. select the next observation against the residual the last one surfaced (Fig 7 :36);
  6. only then consider a lever.
- Steps 1 and 2 have no instrument in many systems. Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** Planning (step 6) cannot rescue that.
- **Falsifier:** a case in which a lever reliably settles the compound without steps 1 to 5 having been performed, and no failure appears later. That would show the dependency order was assumed rather than real.

**Status:** `general` for the dependency order, `specified` for the six steps. Single-source for the order claim (Fig 3), corroborated by Fig 12 :111 at a different level.

### Q6: The two currencies, held apart

- Fig 12 :126-127: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"**
- Saying "harvesting, pollution and habitat change are three pressures" is cheap in bits. Measuring which one moved a given interaction in a given river is expensive in actions. A model that reports the first as if it were the second is counting the wrong currency.
- Fig 12 :128-129: "A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."
- This is why the outreach protocol asks for a refuter first: a refutation is one action that can settle a cheaply stated shape.

## CONCLUSION

Two ecosystem states with the same species list but different relations are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117, and this is the precise structural form of "human-driven evolution alters ecosystem relationships." Persistent pressures are **catalysts** (Fig 12 :119); the feedback between trait change and subsequent exposure is a **cycle** whose sign the ground decides (Fig 12 :121); the three named pressures are members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :123), not three effects to be added. The derivation has an order, set by dependency rather than importance, and most systems break it at the instrument link (Fig 3 :27). The structural claims survive. Which isomer a given system has moved to, and the sign of its cycle, are not returned by the framework and are recorded as owed in `07` and `09`.
