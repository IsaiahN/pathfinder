**Subject:** A checkable claim about how a brain could estimate its own uncertainty

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The residual, in effect terms.** A brain holds a belief, acts, observes, and forms a prediction error per object or slot. The question is how the brain reads the reliability of that error, when the same machinery that makes the decision also produces the apparent confidence in it. A confidence readout produced by the machinery it scores is not evidence; it is a self-score. And a small prediction error has two causes that look the same from inside: the prediction met its ground, or the channel stopped carrying a discriminating observation.
>
> **The shape claim (`general` on the structure, `specified` on the determinants, not `grounded`).** A usable uncertainty estimate must be (1) per slot, never one global number, because a global error near zero with one live slot is a legal state; (2) typed by the kind of gap, not graded only by magnitude, because "held", "new", "mis-attached", "broken" and "refuted" route differently; (3) second order, a read of the reader; (4) channel aware, distinguishing a met ground from a decayed channel; and (5) ground ranged and refusable, able to say that a held belief is false and able to end in an explicit "unreached" instead of manufacturing a number. This is a shape a candidate mechanism must have, not a named circuit and not a measure I am claiming is correct.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** Not that this settles how the brain actually does metacognition. Not that any named region or frequency band is the estimator; nothing I am using names one, and the neuroscience literature itself has no consensus implementation. Not that meta-d′, or any single confidence score, is the answer: those are useful summary measures but they cannot tell you where the belief is fragile or which kind of gap is open. Not that the estimate can certify its own completeness: a second-order estimate is itself noisy.
>
> **The refuter, written before this email.** The shape claim is wrong if any of the following holds:
> 1. a global confidence scalar preserves the where-to-look signal for every live slot, including the case where the global residual is near zero and one slot is live;
> 2. a confidence signal produced by the decision machinery is shown to be independent of what it scores, by an intervention that moves one and not the other;
> 3. a decaying channel and a met ground are distinguishable from inside by the first-order readout alone;
> 4. one uncertainty magnitude determines the correct routing among all five gap kinds in every slot;
> 5. accurate uncertainty about a false belief is demonstrated in a system with no operation that can refuse that belief;
> 6. a metacognitive estimate certifies its own completeness and an independent ground confirms it with no further frame;
> 7. a single instrument settles per-slot metacognitive accuracy across preparations.
>
> If any holds, I retract the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **What I would value from you:** what would you count as a failure of this shape? Which of the five properties is, in your preparation, the one that could actually be measured now? And where would you expect the second-order read to break down? Please answer those before looking at the sources below, so your verdict is not shaped by them.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figure text, not my summary of it, is the authority.
> - Local verbatim figure text with line numbers: `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md` (this is the primary text; I can supply the extract).
> - Fleming and Lau, "How to measure metacognition", Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2014: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4097944/
> - Fleming, "Towards an integrative neuroscience of metacognition", Nature Reviews Neuroscience 2026: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41583-026-01081-x
> - Behrens et al., "Learning the value of information in an uncertain world", Nature Neuroscience 2007: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17676057
>
> **If you want to reuse the machinery:** the formula is in the local figure set. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and label the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> Thanks,
> [name]
