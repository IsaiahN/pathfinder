# Expert verification packet — the Tether on permafrost infrastructure

**What this is:** a claim in a form a permafrost engineer, cryosphere scientist or infrastructure-risk researcher can check. Mode, derivation and refuters are stated before the conclusion, so a reader knows what to look for and what would settle it.

**Mode: `general` on the shape, `specified` on the refuter, not `grounded`.** No measurement at any site is claimed here.

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

"Faster than expected" is a comparison between two rates at a named asset slot and site. The observed term is what the ground is doing to a foundation, roadbed or pipeline, measured at the ground. The expected term is a design assumption, hazard map or cost model, and it is a frame rather than an anchor: it moves when the infrastructure inventory beneath it is corrected. The residual between them is indexed per object slot, so aggregating all "northern infrastructure" into one percentage hides which slot is live. The comparison is ordered, the term to refuse when the residual persists is the expected model, and the harm that matters to a community sits several steps down a contact cascade from the first ground-temperature reading. The figures provide this shape and its falsifiers; they do not provide a rate.

## 2. Three checkable sub-claims

### C1 — framework-internal and empirical; checkable by reading Figure 1 and any per-asset cost table

- **Statement:** the residual must be tracked per asset class and per site. A single global risk percentage is not a residual measurement.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 1 :19 — "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." Also Fig 1 :22 — "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." The Symbols table adds that R is measured per slot, never as a single global number.
- **What to check:** in Melvin et al. (PNAS, open access), Table 2, do buildings and roads fail for different reasons and at different rates? If about 90 percent of building damage comes from thaw while roughly 75 percent of road damage comes from flooding, is a single percentage a meaningful comparison?
- **Refuter:** show that the asset classes share one mechanism and one anchor, so aggregation discards no signal. Then this claim fails and a global percentage is admissible.

### C2 — the anchor test; checkable against any corrected inventory

- **Statement:** the ground is the anchor; the expected rate is a frame that moves under correction.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 2 :29 — "An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks. A measurement, an experiment, a counter, a market, a patient outcome." Fig 2 :36 — "An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it." Fig 10 :24 — "the ground does not decay, the channel does".
- **What to check:** Manos, Witharana and Liljedahl 2025, *Communications Earth and Environment* (open access), abstract and "Estimated infrastructure damage costs". Does adding buildings missed by earlier maps roughly double the loss estimate while reusing the Streletskiy hazard product? If so, the expected term moved and the ground did not. Also check Hjort et al. 2018, where the authors say infrastructure "could experience thaw settlements and failure before the thaw of near-surface permafrost", i.e. the expected threshold trails the event.
- **Refuter:** produce an expected-rate term that does not move when the inventory and design assumptions are corrected. That would make it an anchor and break this claim.

### C3 — framework-internal derivation of the method; checkable by reading Figures 9 and 11

- **Statement:** the question is a union compound, and the harm sits at least three steps down a contact cascade. Unbundle before searching, and enumerate outward.
- **Source, verbatim:** Fig 9 :55 — "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 11 :44 — "List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering." Fig 11 :52 to :53 — "Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."
- **What to check:** is there a documented case in the cited literature where a first-order ground signal did not coincide with the reported harm, for example a buried water-pipe break leaving a clinic without water, or pavement deformation distinct from the mapped thaw extent? If the cascade terminates at first order for every asset, this claim fails.
- **Refuter:** show that one lever clears the whole region (so the problem is a partition, not a union), or that second and third-order enumeration adds nothing.

## 3. What is explicitly not claimed

- Not that thaw is destabilizing infrastructure faster than expected at any particular site. No measurement was taken.
- Not that 70 percent, 80 percent, $37B or $51B is the correct number. Those are world-source figures with stated scopes and inventories, and the 2025 study argues earlier ones were too low.
- Not that the framework derives permafrost physics or supplies a damage function.
- Not a six-term substrate mapping onto permafrost. That reading was retracted as a strain in `01` F9.
- Not that agreement among the cited papers is corroboration. They share authors, inventories, hazard functions and data pools (Fig 2 :40).
- Not that this simulated derivation can settle the question it reports on.

## 5. What would settle it

A measurement, not an argument:

1. For a named asset class at a named site, an InSAR, levelling or borehole time series showing whether the destabilization rate exceeds the design-expected rate over a declared interval.
2. A corrected inventory showing whether the expected damage estimate moves, with the change attributed to the inventory rather than to a new hazard model.
3. A documented third-order harm with its position in the cascade, to test whether first-order scoping is sufficient.
4. The standing refuters in `01` section 3, including a figure line that directly asserts a rate, which would make the empirical content figure-derived.

## 6. What an expert can check in twenty minutes

1. Read Fig 1 :19, Fig 1 :22, Fig 2 :29, Fig 9 :55 and Fig 11 :44, :52 to :53. Six short passages.
2. Open Manos et al. 2025 and read the abstract and the "Estimated infrastructure damage costs" section. Did the corrected inventory move the estimate?
3. Open Hjort et al. 2018 and find the sentence about structures failing before the mapped thaw threshold. Is the expected term conservative?
4. Name one instrument at one site that would measure observed rate against design-expected rate, or say why none exists there.
5. Send the refutation. A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
