## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What is the order, and which bond carries it?

- Fig 12 :112: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."**
- Fig 12 :71-72: sequence (`→`) is **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"**. Generational transmission is ordered: the offspring distribution needs the parent distribution to have happened. `→` is the correct bond for the handoff.
- Fig 12 :86-87: comparison (`⋛`) is **"a threshold relation between two quantities"**. The residual itself is a comparison of the population's mean trait against the current optimum, and Fig 7 :39 calls `S` **"the ground's pressure on the room"**. `⋛` is the correct bond for the distance-to-optimum term.
- Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."** A list of heritability and selection values names a state; only the bonds give the per-generation route.
- So the minimal route is: `current trait distribution → (selection over variance) → next distribution`, with `mean ⋛ optimum` as the comparison that defines the residual being closed. The product law is not a new bond; it prices how much of the comparison survives the sequence.

### Q2 - How does a generation become substrate for the next?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"**
- A trait distribution settled in generation `t` is an atom available to generation `t+1`. Recursion needs no new variance at every step; it needs the variance to persist as a composable term.
- This is the figure-level reason the per-generation product compounds. It does not say the compounding is linear: *How Intelligence Actually Works* notes that "Evolution pays the bill in bodies and lots of time" and that lineage chains are not tidy routes. Mode `general`, single-source for the recursion rule.

### Q3 - Are two adaptive routes isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- If two populations (or the same population at two epochs) are written over the same atom set {variance, selection, inheritance, habitat contacts} but arrange them differently, for example one adapting by a shift in the mean and the other by a change in the variance structure, they satisfy the figure's isomer definition: same atoms, different bond arrangement, different behavioural outcome. The scalar `R` would be blind to the difference, which is exactly the "invisible in the formula" case.
- If they share only the bare triple {variance, selection, inheritance} with different atoms in the slots, they are not isomers by Fig 12's definition; they are members of one **functional group**, the group "variance → selection → inheritance", with different qualifiers. This is the weaker and safer claim.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two routes over the full shared atom set, with different bond arrangements, produce the same per-trait response in the same habitat, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands alone. Conversely, if a route can be written without the inheritance atom and still yield a heritable response, the functional group itself is wrong.

### Q4 - Does the habitat enter the order as an atom or as a condition?

- Fig 11 :48: **"You do not invent the list. You read it off the world, and what you cannot perceive or measure yet is the residual."**
- The habitat's actors (predators, competitors, conditions) are enumerated around the residual, not composed into the rate formula as extra atoms. They are what sets `S` for a slot, which is why F5 of `01` places `S` at the habitat/ground seam. Adding them as atoms would be a category mistake: they are the source of pressure, not the transmission machinery.
- A strain was tested here and rejected: treating the habitat as a seventh atom would conflict with Fig 13's minimal-set rule. Re-census resolves it because the habitat is what the residual is *about*, not a primitive the loop composes. No strain remains.

## CONCLUSION

The order has two bonds: `→` for the generational handoff and `⋛` for the comparison of mean trait to optimum. A settled distribution becomes an atom for the next generation under Fig 12's recursion rule. Two adaptive routes over the same full atom set are a real isomer claim under Fig 12 :117, with a written falsifier; over only the shared triple they are a functional group (Fig 12 :123). The habitat is enumerated around the residual, not added as an atom. Nothing here requires a new primitive, and nothing here gives a number of generations.
