# Are the two causal stories isomers? Order, bond type and the loop

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks to distinguish "institutions that cause prosperity" from "institutions that arise as a consequence of it." Both stories can be written over the same vocabulary: an institution, a prosperity measure, a time index, a policy choice, a stock of human capital, an external condition. What differs is the bond and its order. Two questions are owed: (1) are the two stories isomers under Fig 12's definition, and (2) which ordered bond does each claim, sequence or production?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the two stories isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."
- Fig 12 :46: "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."

Write the two stories over the shared atom set `{institution, prosperity, human capital, geography, time}`:

```
I ⇒ P     (institution produces prosperity)
P ⇒ I     (prosperity produces institutions)
C ⇒ I + C ⇒ P   (a common cause, e.g. disease environment or geography, produces both)
```

Every story uses the same atoms. What differs is the direction of the bond and whether a third atom is introduced as a common source. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. They are not variants of one claim with a coefficient attached.

- The shallow form, I + P (conjunction), records only that both hold. Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." A pooled regression that reports a positive coefficient is the flat formula: it records the ingredients and not the arrangement (Fig 12 :13: "three different changes, one written formula" is the analogous chemistry case).
- Under the symmetric bond `+`, k operands have k! spellings and one term (Fig 12 :92-93). Both orders are the same term, which is exactly why the direction question is invisible in the regression.
- Under the ordered bond `→` or `⇒`, different orders are different terms. Fig 12 :74 / Operators :33: "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."

**Conclusion Q1:** yes, the two stories are isomers over a fixed atom set. The experiment that distinguishes them is not a better measurement of the ingredients, it is a test of which bond holds. This is the isomeric form of F1 in `01`.

### Q2 - Sequence or production?

- Operators :29-34 define `→` sequence: "A then B; B needs A to have happened" and the test "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."
- Operators :35-40 define `⇒` production: "A produces B; B did not exist before" and the test "is there a B before A fires? No."

A finding that a reform was enacted before income rose is a **sequence** claim. It orders the operands but does not create the second one. A claim that the reform produced the income is a **production** claim: the second operand was absent until the first fired. AJR's settler-mortality instrument is an attempt to supply production, not mere sequence: disease environment affects settlement, settlement affects early institutions, early institutions affect current institutions, current institutions affect performance. Glaeser et al.'s counter-claim (human capital first, good policies under dictators, then institutional improvement) is a competing production chain over overlapping atoms, with education taking the earlier slot.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a well-measured case shows B present in the same form before A fires and unchanged after A fires, the production claim fails and the sequence claim is all that remains. A temporal-ordering study alone cannot close this; it needs a counterfactual contrast (Fig 6 :23, a differing closure).

**Conclusion Q2:** the distinction between "precedes" and "produces" is a bond-type distinction, not a degree-of-evidence distinction. Most observational institutional work establishes at best sequence; the direction claim needs production.

### Q3 - The feedback case is a cycle, not a chain

- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

An institution that persists after the prosperity shock that created it (AJR: "These institutions persisted to the present") behaves like a catalyst: it survives its own use. A single regression cannot tell a spent part from a regenerating one, because "nothing records which a term is." If both directions operate, the composition is a cycle; the output of prosperity re-enters as input to institutions, and the sign of the loop decides whether it settles or runs away. A chain cannot close by construction, so a linear regression of P on I is the wrong notation for the feedback case before the question of which loop sign holds is even asked.

The practical consequence: studies that estimate a single coefficient are structurally unable to represent the third option (both directions). This is another reason the question is a union, not a partition.

### Q4 - Functional group versus isomer at different scopes

- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

If the two stories are written narrowly (I ⇒ P with only `{institution, income}` versus P ⇒ I with only `{income, institution}`), they share both atoms and differ only in bond direction, so they are isomers. If they are written broadly with different atoms in the mechanism slots (property rights plus courts versus education plus policy), they share only a functional group: both are "`state-of-rules` bonded to `material-outcome`", with different members in each slot. The group says what kind of thing; the qualifier says which member. Both readings are legitimate at their own scope, and the scope must be declared or the isomer claim is ambiguous. This is the same lesson as Fig 4's round trip: the level decides what is irreducible.

### Q5 - What order the investigation runs in

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." The order is:

1. perceive I and P per slot (Fig 1 :19);
2. have terms for what each institution affords (Fig 3 :7);
3. state what counts as resolving direction (the discriminator);
4. choose an identification frame;
5. carry the method, not the recording, to the next case (Fig 4 :22).

Reversing 3 and 4 is the apophenia the canonical usage note forbids: pick the instrument first and any frame will seem to fit (Fig 9 :27-28).

## CONCLUSION

The two causal stories are **isomers over a fixed atom set**, differing in bond direction, and the bond is the half of the information a pooled regression does not record. "Precedes" is sequence; "produces" is production; the two are separated by a bond-type test, not by a larger sample. The feedback case is a cycle with a sign, which a chain cannot represent, and a persistent institution is catalyst-like in that its survival across uses is not recorded by a single cross-section. At broader scope the two stories share only a functional group, so the scope of the isomer claim must be stated. No figure line selects which institution is the binding in the production bond; that remains the `gap` recorded in `01` F10.
