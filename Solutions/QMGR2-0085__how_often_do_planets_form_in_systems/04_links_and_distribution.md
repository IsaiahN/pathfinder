## What the wider literature actually bears on

1. **Definition before number.** Zhu and Dong (2021) show that "the same term can carry different meanings" across studies and that deriving the fraction of stars with planets "can rely on assumptions of these unknowns." This is the cleanest external match to Fig 9 :27-28, describe the residual before you go looking. Do not ask "how often" until you have said which frequency you mean.
2. **The number is survivor-shaped.** Yang et al. (2023) find multiplicity drops from about 3 to about 1.8 between stars younger than 1 Gyr and stars about 8 Gyr while the host fraction stays near 50 percent, concluding that systems "generally become dynamically hotter with fewer planets as they age." That is the observed, surviving population. Marzari (2024) says a "significant fraction" of systems on the verge of instability "underwent dynamical instabilities in the past." The two statements are one evidence pool only if both rely on the same Kepler catalogue; the forward-modelling step in Yang et al. makes them partly independent, but the overlap must be declared.
3. **The mechanism is chaotic, not clockwork.** Cepelewicz (2023) reports the mathematical result that instability inevitably arises in a three-planet model and notes that real changes may act on hundred-million-year timescales. This corroborates Fig 13 :31-32 at the ground, not at the level of a universal rate.
4. **The survivorship shape is not astronomy-specific.** Budd and Mann (2018) derive the same "conditioning on survival creates apparent early rates" structure in paleobiology, and the Wikipedia survivorship-bias entry records the Wald aircraft case. These are low-overlap witnesses for the shape, not for the frequency.
