# Identity, isomers and level order in the gauge-symmetry question

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The binary "fundamental or emergent" assumes F and E are two different substances occupying one slot. Two questions are owed:

1. Are F and E **isomers** under Fig 12's definition (same atoms, different bond, different substance), or are they the same term read at two levels?
2. What is the **order** between them: a sequence, an identity, a production, or a level crossing?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are F and E isomers?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Write the two readings over the held atoms:

- F reads the symmetry as an **atom at the bottom level**: it sits in `atoms(Γ)` and is not derived.
- E reads the symmetry as a **molecule at a higher level**: it is composed from held atoms and settles at that level.

At a **single fixed level** they are not isomers. They disagree about set membership, not about which bond joins a fixed set of atoms. Isomerism requires the same atoms under a different bond; here one reading adds an atom and the other does not. That is the F4 result in `01`: composition never adds an atom, so E cannot be promoted into F by rearranging bonds.

Read across **two levels**, the relation changes. Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"*. E at level n settles, then serves as an atom at level n+1. At that upper level the symmetry is F. So F and E are not two substances; they are the **same term under a level crossing**, where the crossing changes what counts as irreducible. The relation between them is not a bond in the seven. It is the demotion and promotion named in Fig 12 :104.

This is the answer to Q1: **not isomers at one level; the same term at two levels.** The question's binary smuggles in a single-level frame, and that is why it looks contradictory.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if F and E can be written over the same atom set at one level with only a different bond between them, the isomer claim becomes true and the level-crossing claim fails. If a level can be found at which the symmetry is both an atom and a derived molecule under the same record, Fig 12 :104 fails outright and the whole derivation falls.

### Q2 — What is the order between them?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*

The order is:

1. Settle what counts as an atom in D1 (Fig 13 :124-125).
2. Identify equivalence of descriptions in D2 (Fig 12 :67, :69).
3. Apply the level-crossing rule in D3 (Fig 12 :104).
4. Only then ask for an instrument in D4 (Fig 2 :29-30).

`→` is **sequence**: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :30). I considered writing the derivation as `F → E`, which would imply that fundamentality temporally precedes emergence. That is wrong. The figure that licenses the relation is `≡` identity at a level plus a transform across levels, not `→`. So the order is **dependency**, not chronology. Writing it as a sequence would be the same error the exemplar corrected in its own chain.

`⇒` production would be wrong too: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Fig 12 :76). E does not produce F in time; a settled molecule becomes an atom *for whatever composes over it*, which is a change of role, not a birth.

### Q3 — The functional group of gauge theories

Across the Standard Model, different gauge groups share a substructure: a local symmetry, a connection, matter fields and a covariant derivative. Fig 12 :124: *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The shared substructure is the functional group; SU(3), SU(2) and U(1) are qualifiers selecting members.

This matters for the original question because it relocates it. "Fundamental or emergent" asks about the **kind** (is this substructure primitive or derived?). Fig 12 :124 says the kind is fixed by the substructure's presence, and Fig 13 :124-125 says symmetry as such is derived. So the kind is derived; the members are bindings. An answer that names a group has answered the member question, not the kind question.

Bass (2021, opened 2026-11-23) supplies the world-side analogue: the carpet that looks translationally invariant from a distance and loses that invariance up close. This is a `specified` corroboration of level-relative symmetry, not a figure verdict.

### Q4 — The pricing analogue of gauge overcounting

Fig 12 :92-93: *"Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term, so a code that charges for the sequence overcharges by log₂(k!) while the bond term undercharges. Both wrong, in opposite directions, and only one is usually noticed."*

A gauge orbit is a set of representatives that are one physical state. A description that charges for each representative overcharges by the group volume, which is the same shape of error: one term, k! spellings, a code that counts spellings. This is why the overcount is a **pricing** fact, not an existence claim about extra states. It also gives the refuter in F2 its force: if an observable distinguished two representatives, they would be two terms and the identity reading would break.

Fig 12 :42 states the price: *"(k+1)·log₂(|atoms|+1) + (k−1)·log₂(|bonds|)"*. The group volume is not recorded in the figure, so I do not claim the framework computes it. The analogue is structural, `general`, and the physics quantity is a binding. This is a named limit, not a derivation of the group volume.

## CONCLUSION

F and E are not isomers at one level. They are the same term at two levels under the settled-molecule rule (Fig 12 :104), and the binary question is a level error. The relation is identity plus level crossing, not sequence and not production. The shared substructure of gauge theories is a functional group (Fig 12 :124); the particular gauge groups are members, and naming one does not answer the kind question. The gauge overcount is a pricing analogue (Fig 12 :92-93), not evidence for new states.

This framing rescues what is defensible in both options without adding a primitive: E is composition at a level, F is atom-hood for the next level, and no import is needed to hold the two together. What remains unclosed is F6 in `01`: no gauge-invariant instrument discriminates them at one level.
