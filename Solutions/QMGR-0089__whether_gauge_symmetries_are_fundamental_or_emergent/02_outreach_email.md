**Subject:** Check one output before you decide whether gauge symmetry is fundamental or emergent

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** a gauge symmetry is an identity relation among alternative descriptions of one physical state, not a new substance. Its status as primitive or derived is a fact about the level of description, not about the world. So "fundamental or emergent" is not one binary question. A symmetry can be emergent relative to a constituent level and be an atom for whatever composes over it at the effective level, without contradiction. The framework's own rule is *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"* (Fig 12 :104). There is no bottom and no top, so neither an absolute "fundamental" nor an absolute "emergent" is available.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that any particular gauge group is the basic one; that gauge fields are unreal; that the Higgs mechanism settles the question; that a philosophical analysis can replace a measurement. The framework returns a shape, not a name. The figure that calls symmetry a derivative says *"Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member"* (Fig 13 :124-125). That is a claim about the vocabulary's floor, not about the reality of gauge degrees of freedom.
>
> **What I am not claiming either:** that emergent gauge symmetry is merely a useful fiction. In many-body systems it is an experimental fact that a gauge description dissolves above a scale. I accept that as ground-level evidence from your field, and I keep it separate from the figure-internal shape.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. Derive a symmetry as a seventh primitive, or derive one of the six substrate terms from the other five in a way that promotes symmetry into the set.
> 2. Show that two gauge-related configurations produce a gauge-invariant observational difference, or that the overcount is not the orbit volume.
> 3. Exhibit an instrument whose gauge-invariant reading differs between a primitive gauge field and an effective one at the same level. That would close what I currently record as a gap.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, I will narrow or withdraw the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - The figures and formula, with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Gauge Theories in Physics, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2025: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/gauge-theories/
> - Emergent gauge symmetries, Steven D. Bass, arXiv, 2021: https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00241
> - Symmetry and Symmetry Breaking, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/symmetry-breaking/
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
