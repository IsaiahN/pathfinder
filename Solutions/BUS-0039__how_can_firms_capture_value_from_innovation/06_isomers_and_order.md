# Isomers, bonds and order in value capture

## RESIDUAL

1. Are the four mechanisms named in the strategy literature (patents, secrecy, lead time, complementary assets) **isomers** under Fig 12, or are they different kinds of thing?
2. In what dependency order must the conditions be checked, and where is the chain most likely to break?

## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  Are the four mechanisms isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**

Write the four mechanisms as molecules over the held atom set:

| mechanism | atoms | bond | substance |
|---|---|---|---|
| patent | disclosure + legal right + date + jurisdiction | `⇒` production of a temporary exclusion from a record | a time-bounded, jurisdiction-relative right |
| secrecy | knowledge + non-disclosure + enclave | `¬` refusal of disclosure | a substituted habitat with invisible loss modes (Fig 11 :66-70) |
| lead time | knowledge + speed + successive releases | `→` sequence between rooms | a handoff advantage, not a static asset |
| complementary assets | knowledge + manufacturing + distribution + brand + learning | `+` conjunction across kinds, priced in actions | an arrangement the imitator cannot copy from the artefact |

They do **not** share the same atoms, so under Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**. They are four members of a **functional group**, the group "isolating mechanism": each fixes the outcome in the same broad way (slows or redirects the transfer of private return), while the qualifier says which member. This matches the definition in Fig 12 :124, *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

**The stronger isomer claim** would require rewriting all four over the full shared atom set, differing only in arrangement. Under that rewrite, a patent and a brand are different bonds over the same atoms (a right produced by a record versus a reputation accumulated over time), and the isomer claim becomes real and checkable. That is the version worth testing, not the list as usually written.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one mechanism requires an atom that none of the others can be written over (for example, a legal right with no counterpart in the other three), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands permanently. On the atom set above, no such atom appears, so the functional-group reading is the one that survives this census.

### Q2  -  Union, not partition

- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

The four mechanisms overlap: a patent creates the record that a lead time exploits; a lead time funded by complementary assets funds the next room; secrecy substitutes a habitat that may remove the complementors a brand needs. Because of the overlap, they are C1 through C4 of F8, not four boxes in a menu. A firm failing only C3 and a firm failing only C1 are not different degrees of one failure.

**Falsifier:** a single intervention that restores all four conditions in all four failure types and leaves no categorically different residue. That would collapse the union into a partition and F8 would fail.

### Q3  -  Dependency order and where the chain breaks

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- Fig 3 :29: **"Before asking whether a system is good, ask which link it currently stops at, and whether the answer was measured or assumed."**

Order derived from the residual, not from the mechanism list:

1. **Perception:** can the firm read which returns are arriving, and to whom? Instrument: per-innovation private versus social return accounting.
2. **Vocabulary:** does Γ have terms for what the innovation affords, that is, which parts codify, which stay tacit, which complements bind? Instrument: an explicit atom-and-bond map of the innovation, built before choosing a mechanism.
3. **Objective:** can it say what would count as capture, per innovation, in its own terms? Instrument: a declared threshold ⋛ against imitators and a declared time window.
4. **Planning:** choose a mechanism, or a bundle, against the residual.
5. **Learn and carry:** name what worked, verify it at the ground, and carry the **method**, not the recording, to the next innovation (Fig 4 :22).

The break is most often at link 2 or 3. Links 1 and 4 have mature instruments (market data, strategy tools); the middle link has few. A firm can measure its R&D spend precisely and still have no term for what its innovation affords in the habitat. Fig 12 :112-113: *"A system holding only ingredients can say what a situation contains and cannot say what would count as getting somewhere from it."*

**Falsifier:** a documented firm whose perception, vocabulary and objective links all measure as working, and whose failure is located at planning. That would weaken the "usually at the middle" claim, though not the dependency ordering.

### Q4  -  The two-currency defect

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"**
- Fig 12 :130: **"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."**

Disclosure prices in bits, complement construction prices in actions. A firm that reports an "appropriability score" by adding patent counts to R&D spend is summing two currencies. It will propose arrangements it cannot afford to test, and the budget will decide how much of the space is reached rather than the ground.

**Falsifier:** a prospective metric that predicts capture across both currencies without loss, validated on named innovations before outcomes are known. That would collapse the two readings.

### Q5  -  Negation and the order of refusal

- Operators table, :57: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*
- Fig 12 :139: **"A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."**

The claim "value sits in the knowledge, so protect the knowledge" is a term held in the business frame. The derivation refuses it (F1, F2) before minting the bond claim. Formula step 2 puts refusal before mint: a term held that is false owes a refusal, not an explanation. Had the derivation minted first, it would have built on a term it had not refused. This is why `01` section 6 records the patent-as-proof reading as re-mapped rather than silently dropped.

## CONCLUSION

The four mechanisms are **not isomers** as usually written; they are a **functional group** of isolating mechanisms. The stronger isomer reading (all four over one shared atom set, differing only in bond) is a real checkable claim with a stated falsifier. The four conditions form a **union**, so they must be unbundled before a lever is chosen. Dependency order runs perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry, with the break usually at vocabulary or objective. Two currencies must be held apart. Refusal of the knowledge-as-moat term precedes the bond claim.

This framing rescues what is checkable without pretending the framework selects a mechanism: the group, the union, the order and the two currencies need no new primitive and no unprovenanced import. Which member of the group wins in a given industry remains a `gap` until a ground measurement pays.
