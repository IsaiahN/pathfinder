# Is two webs with the same connectance the same web? Bonds, order, and union

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two ecological networks can have the same species set and the same degree sequence yet behave differently under the same perturbation. The framework must say what carries the difference. Three questions are owed: (1) are such webs **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, (2) what **order** of contact carries the decisive residual, and (3) is the problem a partition or a union. A fourth corrects my own earlier overstatement of the shape-not-a-name rule.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the six structures and states (Space, Time, Energy, Spectrum, Gradient, Network) plus the ecological actors read off the habitat (species, conditions, relations: pollination, predation, competition, dependency). Bonds held: the seven typed bonds, including ⋛ comparison and → sequence. No new primitive is available without import. The residual is indexed per species slot. The web is a union of overlapping conditions because one hub occupies several relations at once.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Same atoms, different arrangement: are the two webs isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 12 :46-47: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**
- A degree sequence records the ingredients and not the arrangement. Under Fig 12 that is the flat formula: "the parts are not the thing" (Fig 12 :17).
- Two webs over the **same full species set**, differing only in which typed relation connects which pair, are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. Their different persistence under perturbation is exactly what the figure predicts.
- Two webs that differ in which species are present are not isomers. They share a **functional group** (for example, `resource -> consumer -> predator`, a state-to-structure-to-state motif) with different members. Fig 12 :124: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two webs with identical species sets but different typed arrangements show identical dynamics under the same perturbation, the isomer claim fails and the flat degree reading stands. Written here before the conclusion.

### Q2 - What order is the cascade?

- Fig 11 :52-54: **"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."**
- First order: the hub's direct partners. Second order: the partners of those partners. Third order: the residual that actually decided persistence in the figure's own example.
- Fig 1 :13: **"a global R near zero with one live slot residual is a legal state"**. A network-level average can look stable while one slot three steps out is already failing.
- Therefore the order of the decisive contact is not the order of the degree count. Degree is a first-order statistic; the sign of a hub's contribution is a second- or third-order result. Any answer that stops at direct links has taken a reading below the break (Fig 3).
- **Falsifier:** a direct-link-only model that predicts collapse outcomes as well as a cascade-enumerating model across webs breaks this order claim.

### Q3 - Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :54-58: **"when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
- Fig 9 :57-58: **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."**
- A hub is in several conditions at once (it consumes, is consumed, competes, facilitates). That is a union. A single lever, protect the hub or remove it, clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds.
- So the right move is to unbundle the hub's relations into separate loops (one per relation), then recombine at the intersection. Averaging them into one "connectance" number is the bundle the figure forbids.
- **Falsifier:** show that a hub's relations can be cleanly partitioned into independent single-condition cases with no interaction at their intersections. Then the union reading is wrong.

### Q4 - What does a settled molecule license at the next level?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- A food web whose modules (modules of strongly coupled species) are settled against the ground can serve as atoms for a larger web. The comparison of two such webs is a second-order composition, and needs no new primitive.
- This is the recursion the framework names, not an import. It also explains why a hub can be stabilizing at one level (inside a module) and destabilizing at another (as the bridge between modules): valence is relative to the residual at that level (Fig 11 :49-51), and irreducibility is a fact about a level.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a settled module that cannot serve as an atom for a larger web, or a hub whose sign is identical at every level and every residual.

### Q5 - Correction to my own section 3 and 4 of `05_what_shape_means.md`

- The README: the phrase is "what a **missing** thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)". The subject is a primitive not held in Γ.
- Network, correlation and overlap are already held (Fig 13, Fig 2, Fig 8). So using them is composition, not the invention of a seventh term.
- ⇒ Naming a hub as stabilizing in a specific web is **not** forbidden by the shape-not-a-name rule. What is forbidden is claiming the binding moves the wall (Fig 6: "IMPORT adds an atom", this one adds none) and reading a source off a projection without a discriminating instrument (the metamerism point in `05` section 4).
- **I overstated this earlier.** Retracted as applied to held atoms; retained as applied to missing discriminators. Fig 12 :92-93: **"Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term"** and **"Under an ordered bond, different orders are different terms, and charging for the sequence is correct."** The question's bare "connected" is the symmetric spelling; typed relations are the ordered terms.

## CONCLUSION

The two webs are **isomers** when they share the full species set and differ only in typed arrangement; they are members of the same **functional group** when they share a motif but differ in species. The decisive residual is second or third order, not the first-order degree count. The problem is a **union** of overlapping conditions and must be unbundled before it is searched. A settled module becomes an atom at the next level, which is why a hub's sign can differ by level. What survives from the naive connectance claim is only the functional-group motif: some structure must mediate between resource and consumer states. What fails is the inference from a degree count to a sign.

The falsifiers (isomer dynamics, cascade order, union unbundling, level relativity) are stated before the run. If any fires, the corresponding part of the supporting derivation is withdrawn.
