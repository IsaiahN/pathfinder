## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What is the bond structure of an extinction sequence?

An extinction is a subtraction. Fig 12 :82 gives the identifying test: *"is the second operand's absence the point? Yes."* The same row names the bond *"A with B removed or absent"*. Subtraction is ordered: the Operands table marks it `no` for symmetry, and the test asks specifically about the second operand's absence.

A sequence of k extinctions is therefore k applications of an ordered bond over a contact structure. Fig 12 :72 defines sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*. Where the second removal is enabled or disabled by the first (a cascade, a dependency collapse, a compensation by survivors), the two removals are joined by `→` or `⇒`, not by `+`. Fig 12 :76: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*.

Where there is no such coupling, the two removals are a symmetric conjunction: Fig 12 :59: *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."* The whole question reduces to which bond holds.

**Answer Q1:** an extinction sequence is a chain of ordered subtractions when dependency or cascade coupling is live, and a symmetric conjunction when it is not. The two cases have different verdicts and are never averaged.

### Q2 — When are two orders isomerous?

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Take two communities that end with the same remaining species set R and the same removed set S. Their atom sets are identical. Apply the figures:

- Under an ordered bond, the removal routes are different arrangements, hence different terms. Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."* If their measured behaviour differs, they are isomers.
- Under a symmetric bond, Fig 12 :92: *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."* The k! spellings are one term. They are not isomers; they are the same entry written k! ways.

Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."* This is the converse: different routes need not produce different substances. So the isomer claim is not guaranteed by a difference of route. It must be tested by measuring behaviour.

**Answer Q2:** two loss orders are isomers exactly when (i) the final atom set is the same, (ii) the bond joining the removals is ordered, and (iii) measured behaviour differs. Condition (iii) is empirical and is where the claim can fail.

### Q3 — What does the chemistry analogue add, and what does it not add?

Fig 12 :97-98 reports the chemistry case: *"This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation. Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; named isomerism by Berzelius, 1830."*

The figure itself says "not an analogy", but it is speaking about the relation between composition and structure, not about ecosystems. For ecology, chemistry is a **low-overlap corroborating frame**, not a source of ecological facts. Wikipedia "Isomer", opened 2026-11-23: *"Structural isomers have the same number of atoms of each element (hence the same molecular formula), but the atoms are connected in distinct ways."* And: *"Isomers do not necessarily share similar chemical or physical properties."*

What it adds: a second, independent field in which identical ingredient lists yield different substances by arrangement. What it does not add: any claim about which ecological species are interchangeable or which order is worse. Fig 8 :50-52: *"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them"*; the provenance of the analogue is recorded here.

### Q4 — Priority effects: analogue or identity?

Wikipedia "Priority effect", opened 2026-11-23: *"In ecology, a priority effect refers to the impact that a particular species can have on community development as a result of its prior arrival at a site."* The same page reports Robert May: *"If there is a unique stable state, historical accidents are unimportant; if there are many alternative locally stable states, historical accidents can be of overriding significance."*

Arrival order and removal order are both ordered bonds on the same contact network. The sign is reversed: arrival installs a relation, removal subtracts one. Because Fig 12 :39 distinguishes `A produces B` from :82 `A with B removed or absent`, they are different bonds, not the same term. So:

- **Legitimate:** priority effects corroborate the *shape* that order can be a live variable on a community's contact structure.
- **Not legitimate:** treating a priority-effect result as evidence for a specific extinction order. That would be an adopted import with no shadow test (Fig 8 :50-52).

**Answer Q4:** analogue only, with a sign reversal, and labelled as such. It is kept because it is low overlap with the biodiversity-ecosystem-functioning pool.

### Q5 — Is there a threshold in k?

No figure line returns a number of removals at which order starts or stops mattering. Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."* Fig 13 :135: *"It cannot say which primitive is missing, because naming it would mean already holding it."* Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence"*.

What can be said is structural: a threshold would be a `⋛` comparison, and Fig 12 :87 defines that as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*. To state a threshold in k one would need two quantities, a measured function and a removal count, and an instrument that shows where the comparison fires. None is in the figure library. This is recorded as `gap`, not filled with a plausible number.

## FALSIFIER (written before the conclusion)

The isomer-and-order derivation is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. Two orders over the same final set, in a dependency-structured community, give the same measured function and stability at every intermediate state, not just at the endpoint. That collapses `→` to `+` for that community.
2. Two communities with identical final atom sets and different recorded routes show identical behaviour on every measured function. That kills the isomer claim (Q2, condition iii).
3. A final species list plus final interaction strengths predicts all trajectories with zero route residual. That kills the lineage claim shared with `01` F5.
4. It is shown that arrival order and removal order require different structures with no shared contact network, so the priority-effect analogue in Q4 is withdrawn.
5. A figure line is produced that returns a threshold in k. That closes the Q5 gap.

If any fires, the affected answer is withdrawn and the retraction is logged in `01` section 6.

## CONCLUSION

An extinction sequence is a chain of ordered subtractions where dependency or cascade coupling is live, and a symmetric conjunction where it is not. Two orders over the same final set are isomers only when the bond is ordered and behaviour differs; otherwise they are one term written several ways. The chemistry case is a low-overlap corroborator of the arrangement principle, not a source of ecological orders. Priority effects corroborate the shape under a sign reversal, not a specific ranking. No threshold in k is available; that is a declared `gap`.
