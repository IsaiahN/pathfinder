# Are the two inequalities isomers? Structure, order and the type of the comparison

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

`01` establishes that opportunity is upstream of outcome (F2) and that the two form a union with an intersection (F4), but it leaves two structural questions open:

1. Are "inequality of opportunity" and "inequality of outcomes" two arrangements of the same atoms (Fig 12 isomers), or is one a part of the other?
2. Is "matters more" a legal second-order comparison, and if so why is it still not a verdict?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What bond joins the two measures?

Fig 12 :71: "A then B; B needs A to have happened" (`→`, sequence, ordered).
Fig 12 :81: "A with B removed or absent" (`−`, subtraction, ordered).

Two readings are available:

- **Part-whole reading.** Under Roemer's decomposition (world source, `10_source_ledger.csv`), total outcome inequality is separated into a component due to circumstances and a residual due to effort. The opportunity component is then a **subtraction** from the outcome total: `EOut − effort component`. The bond is `−`.
- **Isomer reading.** Write two full distributions over the *same* atom set, one varying circumstances and holding effort at its distribution, the other varying effort and holding circumstances. Same atoms, different attribution bond, different substance. Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." The figure's own example: "This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced" structure into the notation.

The two readings are not rivals at the same level. The part-whole reading is about the decomposition of one distribution; the isomer reading is about two distributions constructed over one atom set. Both are needed to answer the question as asked.

### Q2 — What is the order of the union?

Fig 3 orders links by dependency, not importance. The opportunity field precedes the outcome readout: circumstances are read before action, the outcome is observed after. So the union has an internal order `C → O`, an ordered bond, not a symmetric join. Swapping the two changes the claim, which is why "outcomes produce opportunities" is a different (and generally false) statement.

**Re-census note.** Fig 13 :75 forbids two *structures* joining directly with no intervening state. That rule does not apply here, because C is a habitat (Fig 11) and O is a readout, not two structures in the six-term set. This is the re-census recorded in `01` §7. No figure was softened to make this fit.

### Q3 — Isomer or functional group?

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

- If both distributions are written over the **full shared atom set** (slots, outcome variable, circumstance partition, attribution rule), differing only in which condition is varied, they are **isomers** in Fig 12's sense.
- If one distribution lacks an atom the other requires, for example a circumstance partition used for the opportunity measure but not for the outcome measure, then they are not isomers. They are two members of a **functional group** (both are inequality-over-slots measures) with different atoms in the slots.

The isomer claim is the stronger, checkable one. The part-whole reading from Q1 is what makes the overlap asymmetric: the opportunity component sits inside the outcome total, so reducing the former reduces the latter, while reducing the latter through the effort component can leave the former unchanged (F5 in `01`).

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a decomposition is exhibited in which circumstances and effort interact non-separably, so that neither component can be varied while holding the other fixed, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading with a single compound stands instead.

### Q4 — Is "matters more" a legal comparison?

Fig 12 :44, the Operators table and the Symbols table define comparison (`⋛`) as "a threshold relation between two quantities". Each distribution is a settled, measured quantity, so at that level the type check passes. Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." Two settled distributions can each serve as an atom for a second-order comparison.

But a type check is not a ground check. The comparison needs a common currency and an anchor that does not move when either distribution's advocates push on it. That anchor is exactly what F1 in `01` reports as `gap`. So:

- **well-typed:** yes, `⋛` over two indices;
- **grounded:** no, because no invariant anchor is available;
- **status:** the comparison is `specified` at best, `gap` as a verdict.

The operators table also says "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one". Naming "matters more" as a comparison exposes it as a goal, that is, a preference, rather than a measurement. That is a result, not a defect: it tells us what would have to be imported to make it a verdict.

## CONCLUSION

The two inequality measures are related by two bonds at two levels: a `−` part-whole relation inside one distribution (Roemer decomposition), and a possible `→` sequence plus isomer relation across two distributions over a shared atom set. The union is ordered, opportunity before outcome, by dependency (Fig 3), not by moral weight. The comparison "matters more" is well-typed but ungrounded because its anchor is missing. This rescues what is checkable in the question without inventing a priority verdict.
