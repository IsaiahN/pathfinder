# Contract bonds, isomers and dependency order

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The compensation literature lists ingredients (salary, annual bonus, stock options, restricted stock, performance shares, pension) and the governance literature lists controls (independence, say-on-pay, clawback, disclosure). Two questions are owed: (1) is a compensation package a molecule under Fig 12, so that the arrangement rather than the ingredient list decides its behavior, and (2) in what dependency order do perception, vocabulary, objective, planning and carry sit for an incentive contract?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the compensation frame: cash pay, equity, options, targets, benchmarks, vesting time, discretion, disclosure, assurance. Bonds available under Fig 12: conjunction (+), disjunction (∥), identity (≡), sequence (→), production (⇒), subtraction (−), comparison (⋛), plus negation (¬) as a unary operator outside the bond set. No new atom is available without import. The missing instrument (does the reading move without the ground?) is not in the library; it is reported as a shape, not named.

## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  Does "shape, not a name" forbid selecting a metric? CORRECTION TO MY OWN §3 of `05`

- The canonical figures README: *"what a **missing** thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)."* The subject is a primitive not held in Γ.
- Compensation metrics are already in the governance library. They are held atoms, not missing ones.
- Fig 13: *"It can say the answer is out of reach with the library as it stands... It cannot say which primitive is missing, because naming it would mean already holding it."* This governs missing primitives.
- ⇒ Selecting a metric is a **binding** from the compensation frame onto a held atom. It is allowed, but it must be provenanced: who chose it, for which residual, with what shadow test. What is forbidden is treating the binding as a derivation, or as an import that moves the wall (Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom."*).
- **I overstated this earlier.** "The framework returns a shape, not a name" governs the missing discriminator, not the choice among metrics already held. Retracted as applied to metric selection. Triangulated (README + Fig 13).

### Q2  -  Is the contract a molecule?

- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
- A package `{cash, bonus, options, restricted stock}` under different bonds is a different substance:
  - `options → immediate vesting ⋛ single-year EPS target` (sequence plus comparison on a short horizon);
  - `restricted stock → five-year vesting − any clawback right` (sequence plus subtraction of a refusal-like control, where − is absence of a term, not negation of a claim);
  - `bonus ∥ relative TSR` (disjunction: either suffices, which is a weaker bond than conjunction).
- The operators table distinguishes them: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*; progress is a subtraction; a plan is a sequence. A disclosure that lists only the four ingredients has Fig 12's flat-formula failure: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not."*
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two packages with identical ingredients and different bonds behave identically on realized long-term outcomes over a full cycle, the molecule claim fails and the ingredient-list reading stands.

### Q3  -  Are two packages isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Two packages with the same atoms (cash, equity, target, benchmark, vesting time, discretion) and different bonds (hurdle basis, vesting order, clawback presence, comparison group) are **isomers** under Fig 12. Their proxy statements can list the same ingredients while the behaviors diverge.
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** All packages with `"long horizon → measurable hurdle → deferred pay"` share a functional group; the qualifier (which hurdle, which deferral) decides the member.
- **Falsifier:** if the bond differences are shown to be notation only, with no difference in behavior at the ground, the isomer claim fails. If the packages share no atoms at all, they are not isomers but different families, and the functional-group claim fails too.
- Note on negation: a clawback is not Fig 12's `¬`. It is closer to `−` (a term with an operand absent or removed) plus a sequence that fires on restatement. `¬` refuses a claim's standing; `−` removes an operand. The Operators table: *"Subtraction is the nearest bond and is not it."* A contract vocabulary with only `−` can remove a payout and cannot say that the reported figure was wrong. That is the refutation-shaped gap named in the table.

### Q4  -  What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
- Applied to an incentive contract:
  1. **perception:** can the board distinguish durable demand from a pull-forward in the reported number?
  2. **vocabulary:** does the contract have terms for long-term progress and for metric-only movement? (This is the link with no instrument, Fig 3 :25-27.)
  3. **objective:** does the contract say what would count as progress, in its own terms, rather than restating a metric?
  4. **planning:** the payout rule chosen under incomplete information.
  5. **learn and carry:** deferred pay, clawback, and the successor contract, which is where Fig 12 :104 applies: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."*
- Most compensation reform lands at step 4 (change the payout rule) while step 2 remains unmeasured. Fig 3 :29-30: *"Most improvement effort in practice lands above the break, because that is where the measurements already exist."*
- **Falsifier:** show a board instrument at step 2 that is read, that measures what would count as long-term progress, and whose introduction changes step 3 outcomes. If it cannot be read, Fig 3 :27 applies: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

### Q5  -  Two incommensurate prices

- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- Describing a relative-TSR hurdle is cheap in bits. Establishing that the peer group was not selected to make the hurdle easy, and that the TSR movement was not market-wide, costs actions (data, restatements, independent reconstruction). A system holding only the bits accounting will propose arrangements it cannot afford to test.
- Fig 12 :128-129: *"A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."* A one-line "adjusted EPS target" is cheap to state and expensive to settle. Summing the two prices into one score is the error Fig 12 names, not the fix.

## CONCLUSION

Compensation packages are molecules under Fig 12, and packages with the same ingredients under different bonds are isomers whose behavior diverges. The dependency order puts the missing instrument at the vocabulary-to-objective link, so adding payout machinery at step 4 cannot rescue a break at step 2. The molecule and isomer claims are `specified`, not settled; the falsifiers above are written in advance. What survives re-census is the shape: price the arrangement, locate the break, and do not confuse a held metric with a missing discriminator.
