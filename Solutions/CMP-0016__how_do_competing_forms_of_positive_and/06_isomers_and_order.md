# Supporting derivation: cycle structure, bond order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading** (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The shape claim in `01` says a feedback process is a signed cycle and that the three fates form a union compound. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) what structural difference makes two feedback arrangements with the same components different regimes, and (2) does the order of the bonds matter for whether a loop closes at all?

Atoms held: the six terms (Space, Time, Energy states; Spectrum, Gradient, Network structures) plus the loop-level terms cycle, catalyst and comparison from Fig 12. Bonds held: the seven, including → sequence (ordered) and + conjunction (symmetric), and ⋛ comparison (ordered threshold). No new primitive is available without import. This is a union domain: both loops bind, so the case is a compound (Fig 9 :57). Order by dependency: establish the cycle, then its sign, then the bond order, then the threshold comparison, then settle at the ground.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Cycle or chain?

Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

A chain has an input and an output; nothing returns. It cannot close by construction, so a chain cannot self-correct or self-amplify. A cycle re-enters. The sign of the re-entry, not the gain magnitude alone, decides whether the relation closes into a stable loop or runs. This is a structural distinction available before any measurement, so it is `general`.

**Falsifier:** a chain that closes without an output-to-input re-entry. If one exists, this line does not govern the domain.

### Q2 — Are two feedback networks with the same components isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 11 :50 "The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."

Take two networks with identical actors and identical interaction strengths. In one, A amplifies B and B damps A. In the other, both amplify each other. Same atoms, different bond signs, different regime. Under Fig 12's definition these are **isomers**: the ingredient list is identical and the behaviour differs because the bond differs. The ecological analogue makes the point without framework vocabulary: mutualism and parasitism are relations over the same actors, not kinds of actor.

This is a real checkable claim, not a metaphor. Its falsifier, written before the run: if two networks with identical atoms and identical bond signs but different arrangements produce different regimes, the isomer reading is incomplete and a further structural variable is owed. If they produce the same regime, the bond-sign claim stands but the arrangement claim fails. **Status: `specified`, unmeasured.**

### Q3 — Does bond order matter?

Fig 12 :73 "A then B; B needs A to have happened" defines → sequence, ordered. Fig 12 :59 "swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No." defines + conjunction, symmetric.

A closed feedback relation needs an ordered return path: A acts, B responds, the response re-enters as input to A. Under →, swapping the operands gives a different term. Under +, the two coexist and no return path is recorded. So writing two feedbacks as A + B records that both are present and cannot record that they close. Writing them as A → B → A records a cycle. The order is not decoration; it is the thing that closes the loop.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a closed feedback relation whose closure is unchanged when the operand order is swapped under a symmetric bond. That would collapse → into + for this domain and break the ordering claim.

### Q4 — The threshold comparison, and an open strain

Fig 12 :87 "a threshold relation between two quantities" defines ⋛ comparison. The dominance of one loop over the other is naturally written as a ⋛ relation: when the amplification exceeds the damping by a threshold, the pattern crosses into a different regime. World corroboration: May 1972 reports stability up to a critical connectance and then a sudden instability, a threshold rather than a dial.

**Open strain, named not hidden.** At the loop level the operands are not bare quantities but settled cycles now serving as atoms. Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world" permits the demotion. Whether a settled cycle counts as a quantity at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. This is the same strain D recorded in `01` section 5.

### Q5 — Catalyst and autocatalysis

Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

Growth is not just a large gain. In an autocatalytic loop, the part that produces more of the pattern survives its own use and is available to produce again. A spent part does not. Because the formula records ingredients and not which parts regenerate, two systems with the same ingredient list can differ decisively in whether they grow. This is a second route to the isomer conclusion and it is triangulated at low overlap with Fig 12 :117.

**Falsifier:** a system whose producing part is consumed exactly as fast as it produces, yet whose pattern grows without a regenerating part. That breaks the catalyst reading.

### Q6 — Analogues across fields, used as corroboration only

- **Biology, stabilization.** Homeostasis: sensors detect a deviation, controllers coordinate a response, effectors counter it. A negative feedback loop with a set point that can move. Corroborates the damping reading in F3.
- **Physics and network science, propagation.** Watts and Strogatz 1998: small-world coupling displays enhanced signal-propagation speed and synchronizability, and "infectious diseases spread more easily in small-world networks than in regular lattices." Corroborates Fig 12 :117: same nodes, different arrangement, different regime.
- **Mathematical ecology, threshold.** May 1972: sudden instability past a critical connectance. Corroborates the ⋛ reading in Q4.
- **Dynamical systems, third regime.** Edge of chaos is described as "a transition space between order and disorder" and a region of bounded instability, with the explicit caveat that its generality has been questioned. Use as an analogue for the third fate, not as a settled result.
- **Terminology, valence relativity.** The Feedback article notes that the same example can be called positive or negative depending on reference. Corroborates Fig 11 :49.

## CONCLUSION

Two feedback networks over the same atoms but with different bond signs are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same components, different substance. Their closure requires an ordered bond (→), not a symmetric coexistence (+), per Fig 12 :73 and :59. Their dominance relation is written as a ⋛ comparison, corroborated by a threshold result in ecology, with one open strain about the operand type at the loop level. Autocatalysis gives a second, independent structural route to growth (Fig 12 :119). All of this is `general` or `specified` structure. None of it names the regime of a particular system; that is `gap` and is settled at the ground.

The falsifier bundle, written before this conclusion: break the chain/cycle distinction, break the isomer claim, break the order dependence, settle the ⋛ operand type against an instrument, or show growth with no regenerating part. Any one of these sends the derivation back to the census.
