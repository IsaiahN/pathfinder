## Q1 - Are two firms with the same governance mix isomers?

> Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
> Fig 12 :124 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
> Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Two firms with the same list of governance modes (say, both employ developers, contract out payroll and automate invoicing) have the same atoms. If their bonds differ, that is, if one composes the atoms as a repeated internal sequence and the other as a reconfigurable network with imported head starts, they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with the difference invisible in the org chart. This is the figure-grounded version of "it is not who you hire, it is how the work composes", but stronger, because the arrangement is itself a substance carrying information the ingredient list does not.

If the firms share only a pattern (both use `work → governance mode → market delivery`) while differing in which atoms occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: members of one **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), the qualifier saying which member. The presence of the pattern fixes the kind, not the outcome.

The distinction is testable. A governance-mix comparison that finds no difference between two firms cannot distinguish isomer from functional-group; the bond has to be recorded. Fig 12 :92 "Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two firms with identical atoms and identical bonds nevertheless diverge in per-slot R with no difference in ground contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D2), which moves the explanation out of D3.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated for the legality of the reading (Fig 12 and Fig 11 :50, different figures), single-source for the firm application. The world corroboration is Gordon 2015's finding that task allocation is a live distributed process rather than persistent specialization, but that is an ant-colony analogue, not a firm measurement (Fig 4 :20 requires the anchor to be re-named at the new scale).

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
> Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
> Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

Order for a firm facing a changed market:

1. **Perception.** Can it read which work the market now pays for, per slot? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests.
2. **Vocabulary.** Does Γ have terms for what the changed work affords, and can it say what would count as done? This is the middle link. Fig 3 :26: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."*
3. **Objective.** Can it state a comparison that counts as progress per slot? Fig 12 :112 "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction." A vocabulary with only governance labels cannot state a goal.
4. **Planning.** Allocate each slot to a mode.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, import what it did not have (Fig 3 :15, Fig 6 :23).

The four conditions map onto the chain: C1 (contact) is mostly a failure of links 1 and 2; C2 (reach) is mostly a failure of link 5, because the firm names what worked but never imports a new atom; C3 (channel) is a failure of link 1's percepts and link 2's pooled vocabulary; C4 (generator) is a failure at link 5, where a recording is mistaken for a method. That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear all four (Fig 9 :55).

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Link 1 has accounting and vendor data; link 5 has almost none. Budget naturally piles up at links 1 and 4 while the break sits at 2 and 5. Bainbridge 1983 corroborates at a different scale: the operator is left with the tasks the designer could not automate, and *"physical skills deteriorate when they are not used"*. The "automate the easy part" move buys link 4 efficiency and silently breaks link 5.

**Falsifier:** a firm whose chain demonstrably works at 1, 2 and 3 and fails only at 4. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim of F8-style diagnosis, though not the dependency ordering itself.

## Q4 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the make-or-buy emphasis on a boundary, as the seam between closure and import (D1/D2);
- the real-options emphasis on reversibility, as C3 and Fig 12 :126;
- the automation literature's warning about takeover skills, as channel decay (Fig 10 :26) and generator/recording (Fig 4 :25);
- the social-insect emphasis on live allocation, as valence relative to the residual (Fig 11 :49-51);
- the OR emphasis on volatility-dependent thresholds, as the action cost distinct from the description cost (Fig 12 :125-128).

It cannot generate the *name* of the specific vendor, tool or ratio that will work next. That is the `gap` recorded in F11 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence," and Fig 8 :39 apply. The essay's source rule states why: an instrument that reads a result *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists."*

## Q5 - The bond table applied to the four modes

Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands." Reading the governance modes as bonds rather than kinds:

- **Employee, internal composition** maps to `+` conjunction (both operands hold at once, symmetric) and to `→` sequence when the work is a route. The kernel is stable across size (Fig 4 :20).
- **Contractor, imported head start** maps to `⇒` production in the receiving closure: an operand that was absent before the import fires (Operators table :35-40, "is there a B before A fires? No.").
- **Supplier, settled molecule** maps to identity `≡` at the next level: a composed result becomes an atom for whatever composes over it (Fig 12 :104). Two names, one referent, after settlement.
- **Automation as generator** is a re applicable method crossing up (Fig 4 :22). **Automation as recording** is a replay that carries no new atom (Fig 4 :25). The difference is not in the artefact's ingredient list.
- **The "should" in the question** is `⋛` comparison, a threshold relation between two quantities (Fig 12 table, Operators table :48), applied to per-slot R against the market's payment, never to a global mix.
- **A refusal of a current allocation** is `¬` negation, not subtraction: a refutation is the refusal of a claim (Operators table :53-58), and a vocabulary without it can only accumulate. This is why the pre-registered refuter is part of the design, not an appendix.

This mapping is a `specified` structural reading. Its falsifier: if a relation between two work operands fails all six bond tests, it is not a missing bond but a claim not yet made (Fig 12 :90-91); the mapping must then be withdrawn rather than stretched.

## CONCLUSION

Two firms with the same governance atoms but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117; two firms sharing only a `work → mode → market` pattern are members of one functional group under Fig 12 :124. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the middle link (vocabulary and objective) and the carry link the usual breaks, which is why C1 to C4 need different instruments. The standard management and OR explanations are mostly bindings of one evidence pool and are generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim has a written falsifier; the order claim has one; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
