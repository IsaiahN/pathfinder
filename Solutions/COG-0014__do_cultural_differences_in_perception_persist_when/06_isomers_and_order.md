# Isomers and order: two observers over one atom set, and the re-entrant history loop

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two observers from different developmental habitats perform task T under the same immediate conditions. The between-group difference is asserted to be cultural. Two questions are owed:

1. Over what atom set are the two observers the same, and over what arrangement do they differ?
2. In what order does history enter the percept, and can that loop be closed by the experiment?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the loop's vocabulary: a state (the stimulus at the slot), a structure (the ordering or gradient the task presents), the belief `b`, the weighting `w`, the library Γ, the action `a`, the observation `o'`, and the habitat conditions. Bonds held: identity `≡`, comparison `⋛`, sequence `→`, production `⇒`, subtraction `−`, and the unary negation `¬`. No new primitive is available without import, and "culture" is not one.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the two observers isomers over a shared atom set?

Fig 12 :117, "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 12 :92, "Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."

If both observers are given the same stimulus, the same apparatus and the same instruction, then the *atom list* is the same: state, structure, action, observation, habitat conditions. What differs is the arrangement: which prior in Γ is bound to the stimulus, and how `w` weights private history against inherited prior. Under Fig 12, that difference is not a difference of ingredients. It is a difference of bond, therefore a difference of substance, and the flat description "same task, same conditions" cannot see it because it records only the ingredients.

So the two percepts are **isomers** over the shared atom set. This is not an analogy imported from chemistry; Fig 12 makes it a claim about notation: "the parts are not the thing" (Fig 12 :17) and "the arrangement is the substance" (Fig 12 :94).

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the two observers can be written with genuinely different atom sets (one has a prior the other literally does not have in Γ, or the stimulus is not the same physical object), the isomer claim fails and the difference is a compound with import, not an isomer. The distinction matters because an import needs a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 6 :23, Fig 8 :50), while an isomer needs only a bond census.

### Q2 — Are they instead a functional group?

Fig 12 :124, "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

If the atom sets are *not* held fixed (different languages, different visual environments, different response scales), the observers share only a functional group: a human perceptual system performing a comparison task. The group says "human perceiving under T"; the qualifier says which history. This is the weaker and safer claim. Amir and Firestone 2025 argue that for the Müller-Lyer case even the qualifier does no work, because the effect appears across species, modalities and congenital blindness. That is a world claim about the specific slot, not a figure claim; it bears on which reading (isomer versus functional group) the ground licenses for that slot.

**Status:** `specified`, ungrounded. The isomer reading is single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12), corroborated structurally by Fig 9 :30 (describe the effect, not the cause); the functional-group fallback is triangulated across Fig 12 and the animal-behaviour world source, which report the same effect under different "members".

### Q3 — Where does history enter, and is the loop a chain?

Fig 12 :121, "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Fig 3 :15, "name what worked, verify it, and carry it to the next problem."

History is not a sixth link appended to perception. It is the fifth link whose output re-enters the first: percept, learning, named-and-carried history, belief `b` and weighting `w`, next prediction, next percept. That is a cycle, not a chain. A chain cannot close by construction, so an experiment that models the task as a one-shot stimulus-response chain has already removed the thing it wants to measure.

The sign of the loop decides the result:

- Reinforcing sign: a prior bias is confirmed by what is attended to, the same slot stays live, and the difference persists and can grow.
- Correcting sign: exposure in the new habitat updates `w` and the percept converges.

Which sign holds for a given task is a ground question, not a figure verdict. What the figures do license is that persistence and washout are two signs of the same loop, not two different phenomena, and that a one-shot harness cannot decide between them (Fig 11 :70, "A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability.").

**Falsifier:** a task in which the percept has no re-entrant path to belief (no learning, no carry) and a between-group difference still persists. Then the cycle model is wrong for that slot and the carrier must be relocated.

### Q4 — What order is the derivation, and can a flat formula express it?

Fig 3 :20, "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." The order is perception (1), vocabulary (2), objective (3), planning (4), learn and carry (5). Culture enters at 5 and feeds back to 1. Fig 12 :111, "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one." Operators table :34, "the only bond a flat chain can express, so every term today is a sequence whether or not it should be".

A between-groups design records the state (group A mean, group B mean) and cannot name the route (which link carried the difference). Treating the difference as a sequence, "culture causes percept", imposes an ordered bond the flat measurement does not license. The correct order is a cycle with a sign, which requires an instrument measuring the feedback, not a larger sample at the endpoint.

Fig 13 :75, "Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state." This governs the substrate primitives, not the semantic content of a learned prior. Applying it to "culture causes perception" would be a strain, so it is **declined as a governing line** here and recorded in `01` section 9 and `11` as S1. The relation of culture to `w` is a binding from the social-science frame, marked `specified`, ungrounded, provenance required.

## CONCLUSION

The two observers are isomers over a shared atom set when the stimulus, apparatus and instruction are genuinely shared: same atoms, different bond, different substance, invisible in the flat formula. When the atom sets are not shared, the weaker and safer claim is a functional group with different qualifiers. History enters as the fifth link of Fig 3 re-entering the first, forming a cycle rather than a chain (Fig 12 :121), and the sign of that loop decides persistence versus washout. The sign is a ground question. What survives without any new primitive is the bond census and the cycle shape; what does not survive is the flat causal sentence "culture causes the difference".
