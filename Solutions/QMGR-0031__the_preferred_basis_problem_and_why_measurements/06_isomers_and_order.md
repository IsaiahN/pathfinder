# Bases, isomers and order: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`, not `grounded`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Three secondary questions are owed by the main derivation:

1. Are two different bases of one state **isomers** (Fig 12's "same atoms, different bond, different substance"), or something else?
2. What **order** does the measurement chain have, and which link is dependency-first?
3. What **analogue** from the figure vocabulary makes the basis concrete without naming one?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in this derivation: a state, a set of observables, a coupling, an environment, an outcome reading. Bonds held: ≡ identity, → sequence, ⇒ production, ⋛ comparison, ¬ negation. No new primitive is available without import. Anchor: the recorded outcome (Fig 2 :29). Question type: a union of three sub-questions; the answer to (1) constrains the wording of (2) and (3).

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are different bases isomers? No. They are identities.

- Operators table :25: "two names, one referent". Operators table :27: "substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."
- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Two basis expansions of one state have the same referent (the state) and the same bond structure (the representation). What changes is the coordinate naming. Under ≡ they are one term with k! spellings, not two substances. So they are **not** isomers. Calling them isomers would import a distinction the notation does not carry.

- **Consequence for the main question:** the formalism's "all bases equivalent" is the identity bond doing its job. It says nothing about which observable is physically coupled. The preferred basis is not a selection among spellings; it is the selection of a bond to an external frame.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two basis descriptions of one state yield different physical predictions under an identical coupling, then they are different substances (isomers), ≡ is the wrong bond, and this finding falls.

### Q2. What is the real isomer comparison?

The genuine isomer move in this neighbourhood is between two *different* physical arrangements over the same atoms, not between two descriptions of one state. Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

Applied carefully, this says: take two systems with the same physical ingredients (system, apparatus, environment, coupling) but different couplings, and you get different pointer substances (different preferred observables). That is an isomer claim, and it is checkable against the physics. It is not a claim that position and momentum are isomers of one state.

- **Mode:** `specified` for the application; the definition itself is `general`.
- **Falsifier:** two different couplings over the same atoms yielding the same pointer observable in all regimes would collapse the isomer reading to identity.

### Q3. What order does the measurement chain have?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

The dependency order for this question is:

1. **Perception (Fig 3 :4-5):** can the apparatus distinguish outcomes at all? This is the instrument link.
2. **Vocabulary (Fig 3 :6-8):** are there terms for what the coupling affords, namely stable pointer states?
3. **Objective (Fig 3 :9-11):** what counts as progress here, namely a definite recorded outcome?
4. **Planning (Fig 3 :12-13):** a route via environment-induced selection.
5. **Learn and carry (Fig 3 :14-16):** name what worked and carry it.

The common inversion is to begin at step 4 ("decoherence selects a basis") when step 3 is unmeasured. Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." In the present literature, the link with the fewest instruments is step 3, the move from suppressed interference to a definite outcome. The SEP decoherence entry says exactly this: decoherence "does not explain why we do observe measurement results in the first place."

- **Falsifier:** exhibit an instrument at step 3 that resolves outcomes without an interpretation, collapse or added record. Then the chain is complete above the break and this ordering claim is withdrawn.

### Q4. The pose analogue

Symbols table :81: F is "the frame transform", "Cannot be run without a pose." It is "the map between allocentric and egocentric at one scale" (Symbols table :80-82).

A basis functions like a pose: it is required to map between the state description and the outcome description at one scale. Without fixing a pose, the map is undefined, not merely unknown. This is an analogue, not a proof, and it is kept distinct from the physics. It explains why asking "which basis" before fixing a coupling is a category error, the same error as asking for an allocentric-to-egocentric map with no pose.

- **Falsifier:** a basis-free map from state to outcome that is fully defined without a pose would break the analogue. The analogue is corroborating only; it never decides the verdict (Fig 2 :35).

### Q5. Why "position" is quasi-preferred, without naming it

Fig 11 :39: "capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone." Fig 11 :40: "An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."

The reason localised observables are often the stable ones is a habitat fact: the actual interactions are characterised by potentials that are functions of position. That is a physical, contact-dependent fact about the environment. It is not a decree from the formalism and not a seventh primitive. This is why the shape answer can explain the quasi-preference of position without naming it as the framework's answer. Naming remains an import with a source gate and shadow test (Fig 6, Fig 8).

- **Falsifier:** a system whose environment monitors a non-local observable as the dominant stable record, with all position coupling absent, would show the quasi-preference is not general and narrow the claim to "depends on coupling", which the shape already says.

## CONCLUSION

Different bases of one state are **identities**, not isomers; the real isomer claim compares different couplings over the same physical atoms. The measurement chain is ordered by dependency, and the least-instrumented link is the move from suppressed interference to a definite outcome, so that link cannot be skipped by starting at decoherence. The pose analogue explains why a basis is required for the state-to-outcome map. "Position" is quasi-preferred for a habitat reason, not by decree, and is recorded as an import rather than a figure-derived name. The falsifiers above were written before the run and are recorded in `01` section 4 and `09`.
