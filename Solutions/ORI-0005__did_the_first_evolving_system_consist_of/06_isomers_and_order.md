## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are molecules, networks and compartments isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Apply the test. Isomers need a shared atom set with a different bond. The three candidates do not share one:

- an individual molecule is a persistent operand, a bonded arrangement in Fig 12 :104's sense;
- a chemical network is a relation over many operands, the structure named "network" in Fig 13 :24, "that entities relate, and influence travels";
- a compartment is a spatial bound, Fig 13 :10's "near, far, between, contained."

They are not the same atoms under different bonds, so **they are not isomers**. Nor are they a functional group in Fig 12 :124's sense: there is no single substructure whose presence fixes the outcome and whose qualifier selects a member. They are *different roles that must compose*, governed by Fig 13 :74: "Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit a single atom set from the six such that all three candidates are arrangements of it under different bonds, with the behaviour difference carried entirely by the bond. If that works, the isomer reading stands and this finding is retracted.

### Q2: What order is the union?

Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

The three conditions form a loop: molecules catalyse the network that produces molecules; a boundary concentrates the network that maintains the boundary; the network supplies the components and the gradient that sustain the boundary. Fig 12 :121 says a chain cannot close by construction, so a cycle has no first link. Fig 3's dependency ordering applies to chains, not loops. Applying it here is the category error that generates the question's "first."

Fig 12 :104 permits the recursion in which a settled molecule becomes an atom for the next level. That is the legitimate sense in which one thing can be *prior*: not first in time, but composed-over at a higher level. It does not licence a temporal claim about origins.

- **Falsifier:** show the three conditions in an acyclic dependency order where each is independently sufficient to start evolution. That would make "first" well defined and break this finding.

### Q3: What does the world literature say about order?

The chemoton model requires metabolism, self-replication and a membrane together (Chemoton, Wikipedia, fetched 2026-11-23). The Lincoln and Joyce result shows individual RNA enzymes forming a cross-catalytic pair that then evolves under competition (Lincoln and Joyce, Science 2009). The protocell page reports that a functional evolving protocell has not yet been achieved in the laboratory. None of these supplies a temporal order; they supply instances of the compound at different levels of organisation. They corroborate the shape, not a chronology.

- **Falsifier:** a ground result that establishes a unique temporal order among the three conditions and shows the others were absent at the start. That would move the `gap` to `grounded`.

### Q4: Is there any legitimate "first"?

One narrow sense survives. At any *given* origin event, the ground may show that one condition crossed a threshold before the others in that particular setting. That is a historical finding about a case, not a figure-derived rank among kinds. Fig 13 :4 to :5 forbids the rank: "There is no order of rank here. The two kinds are in lockstep, a crossing is a change of role rather than a step upward, and no member of the pair is senior to the other."

So the answer to Q4 is: **not as a structural claim**. As a measured historical claim, it is possible and is exactly the `gap` recorded in `01` F7.

## CONCLUSION

The three candidates are neither isomers nor a functional group. They are different roles (operand, closing loop, spatial bound) that must compose into one system. Their enabling relations form a cycle, so "first" is a chain assumption misapplied. The one legitimate priority is composition priority under Fig 12 :104, which is not temporal priority. A historical first remains possible in principle and is owed to the ground; the figures do not name it.
