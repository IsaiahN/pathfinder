# Isomers, cycles, and the order of the discrimination search

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed by `01`: (1) in what structural sense are a bounce model and an inflationary model the "same" or "different", given that they fit the same data; and (2) in what order must the search for a discriminant be run, so that a budget filter is never mistaken for a verdict.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the two model families isomers?

Fig 12 :117 *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

- The **weak** isomer claim: both families are built over the same *observable* atom set (a nearly scale-invariant scalar spectrum, adiabatic modes, a hot recombination history). Their difference is in the *history* that produced those atoms (accelerated expansion in the past versus contraction then bounce). Under Fig 12 they share atoms and differ in arrangement, so they are **isomers at the level of the phenomenological formula**: *"the difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour"*, where "behaviour" here means the predictions at the *other* slots (tensors, bispectrum, background).
- The **strong** isomer claim would require both families to be written over the same *theoretical* atom set, differing only in bond arrangement. That is not established by the cited sources: inflation uses an inflaton and slow roll; ekpyrotic models use a contracting scalar with a steep negative potential; LQC uses quantum-gravity modifications. These are different atom sets. The strong claim is therefore **not made**.
- Fig 12 :123 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The shared scalar spectrum is a functional group: it says "successful early-universe model" and does not say which member. That is the precise structural reason the scalar spectrum cannot discriminate.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either family can be written using an observable the other cannot in principle produce, the isomer claim collapses to a plain difference of predictions and the shared-functional-group reading is the only one left. If, conversely, both families are shown to have identical theoretical atoms and identical bonds, the isomer claim fails by identity. Both directions are refused in advance if unsupported.

Verdict: weak isomer claim `specified` and triangulated (Fig 12 isomer plus Fig 13 :121 *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*); strong isomer claim **not asserted**.

### Q2 — Does the Fig 12 "cycle" bond settle the cosmological "cyclic" question?

Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*

This is a **composition structure**, a bond that makes a molecule recur. A cosmological cyclic model is a **physical history**: expansion, contraction, bounce, repeat. The two share a word and a shape but are not the same object. Re-census (required by the canonical README's rule that a contradiction means a misidentified domain): Fig 12 governs how a composition re-enters; the model literature governs what the universe does. No figure line settles whether the universe is cyclic. Using the Fig 12 line as if it did would be echo without shadow, the inversion Fig 9 :27 to :28 warns about: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"*.

**Falsifier:** if a reader can show that the Fig 12 cycle bond *entails* a physical bounce, the re-census is wrong and the line does govern cosmology. No such entailment is present in the verbatim text. Verdict: analogy only, named as such. No strain after re-census.

### Q3 — Sample space versus partition: what kind of question is this?

Fig 9 :4 to :5: *"First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."*

The bounce-versus-inflation disagreement does not shrink on the scalar-spectrum subset: both frames keep working well there. It is therefore not one hard question. It is a **union of at least six well-formed condition questions** (C-a to C-f in `01`). A single lever aimed at the scalar spectrum clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing. Fig 9 :57 *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*.

At the same time, the two frames are a **partition of model space**: a given universe had one history or the other (or neither). Partition of models and union of tests are different objects; keeping them apart is what prevents the category error of averaging the tests into one model verdict.

**Falsifier:** show that a single observable separates the families on every subset, so the union collapses to a partition of tests. Then the unbundling requirement is unnecessary and should be withdrawn. Verdict: `general`, triangulated (Fig 9 split rule plus Fig 1 per-slot rule).

### Q4 — What is the order of the search?

Formula step 7 names three questions; Fig 6 adds the instrument condition. The order is fixed by cost, not by preference:

1. **SEARCHED** (cheapest; both others assume its answer). Fig 6 :49 *"more search at the same reach: is it in here and unfound? The closure does not change. Same atoms, same wall, more of the space actually visited."* Applied: the scalar spectrum has been searched to high precision; more of the same fitting adds no new atom. Fig 8 :32 *"Closure is idempotent. Composing inside a closed set never leaves it, so no search, however long, adds an atom."*
2. **INWARD** (extend an instrument already returning something). Fig 6 :29 *"inward: is our representation adequate to hold it? The instrument extends access. Fails by refining forever on an axis that cannot carry it. Sharpen in our own terms."* Applied: better B-mode foreground separation, larger PTA baselines, lower bispectrum noise.
3. **OUTWARD** (import a frame whose closure predicts the shape). Fig 6 :30 *"outward: does another frame already hold it? The description travels. Fails by being sharpened into terms only we can read. Cast it in effect terms."* Applied: a primordial interpretation of a stochastic background, imported with a source gate and a shadow test, debited against independence.
4. **Never read INWARD against OUTWARD.** Fig 6 :31 *"Neither answer tells you much about the other. Run them as two tracks, and never read one against the other."*

**Falsifier:** demonstrate that SEARCHED is not cheaper than INWARD or OUTWARD in this domain (for example, that an instrument extension costs less than re-analysing existing data). Then the ordering should be revised. Verdict: `general`, framework-internal, single-source for the ordering (Formula step 7 plus Fig 6, one evidence pool).

### Q5 — Which bonds are admissible for comparing the two predictions?

"The two models both fit the data" is a `+` conjunction (both hold), which is symmetric and carries no ordering. "The models differ at slot s" is `−` subtraction or `⋛` comparison, both ordered. Operators table :46 *"progress is a subtraction, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot say how far along it is"*; :49 *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*.

Using `+` for "both fit" and then calling that a discrimination is the flat-formula error of Fig 12 :14 to :16: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not... nothing downstream can tell them apart, and nothing signals that anything was lost."* The arrangement (which bond connects the two predictions at which slot) is the substance.

**Falsifier:** show that a symmetric conjunction of predictions can itself yield a difference signal. It cannot, by the symmetry test. Verdict: `general`, triangulated (Fig 12 plus Operators table).

### Q6 — Refusal before mint

The claim "there is one decisive observation" is a held preference, not a bound term. Fig 5 :62 to :63: *"A frame searching for an answer of a particular shape holds that shape as a PREFERENCE rather than as a term. It is not bound, it predicts nothing, and it accrues no residual, so nothing here can see it."* Fig 5 :65 *"make the sought-for shape an ORDINARY TERM."* The refusal is written before the run in `01` section 4. A refusal is a negation (¬), not a bond; Fig 12 :135 *"Negation joins nothing, so its symmetry entry is empty rather than no."*

**Falsifier:** show that the "one decisive observation" preference already predicts a value and accrues a residual, in which case it is already a term and needs no special discipline. It does not. Verdict: framework-internal, `general`.

## CONCLUSION

The two families are **weak isomers at the phenomenological level**: shared observable atoms, different histories, and the difference shows up only at other slots. The Fig 12 "cycle" governs composition, not cosmological history, and is used as an analogy only. The question is a **union of tests over a partition of models**, so it must be unbundled and answered per slot. The search order is SEARCHED, then INWARD, then OUTWARD, with INWARD and OUTWARD kept apart. The comparison bond is subtraction or threshold comparison, never conjunction. The "decisive observation" preference is refused before the run. None of this names a winner, and none of it is `grounded`.
