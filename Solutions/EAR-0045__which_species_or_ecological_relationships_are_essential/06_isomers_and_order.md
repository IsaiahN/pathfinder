# Bonds, isomers and order in an ecological community

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What is a community made of: ingredients or arrangements?

Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Fig 12 :94 "The arrangement is the substance." Fig 11 :47 "relations: pollination, predation, competition, dependency."

A species list is an ingredient set. The relations between the species are the bonds. Two communities with the same species list but a different interaction network are not variants of one thing; they are different substances whose ingredient notation cannot tell them apart. The ecology literature corroborates this at the world level (Paine's removal experiment showed that the identity of the top predator changes the whole community; Jones, Lawton and Shachak 1994 showed that a habitat modifier acts by changing the physical state others experience), but the figure verdict does not depend on those sources.

**Falsifier:** if two communities with identical species lists and identical interaction bonds nevertheless show identical functions under identical conditions across several habitats, the arrangement claim is refuted and the ingredient list is sufficient.

### Q2 - Are two communities isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Over a fixed species set, a grassland dominated by a nitrogen-fixing legume and one dominated by a mycorrhizal network, with the same species present in both but different interaction bonds, are isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance. Under the insurance hypothesis (Yachi and Loreau 1999), two communities with the same species but different response asynchrony can have the same temporal mean and very different temporal variance, which is exactly "different substance" at the function level. That is world corroboration from a low-overlap frame, not an extra figure witness.

**Falsifier:** if a fixed species set always produces the same function regardless of bond arrangement, the isomer reading fails and the functional-group reading below stands instead.

### Q3 - What is a functional group?

Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

A pollinator guild, a nitrogen-fixing module, or a top-predator slot is a functional group: the group fixes what kind of effect is present (pollination, nitrogen input, top-down control), and the species identity is the qualifier that says which member. This reconciles the ecology literature's "functional group" language with the figure's refusal of a standing list. The group is a structure claim; the member is a binding with provenance. A conservation-ecology paper linking keystone species to functional groups (found in the search) is world corroboration that the group-and-member distinction is a real move in this field, but it is secondary.

**Falsifier:** if removing a whole functional group leaves every measured function unchanged across habitats, the group does not fix the outcome and the claim fails.

### Q4 - What is a catalyst here?

Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

A pollinator, a nitrogen-fixing microbe, or an ecosystem engineer such as a beaver is catalyst-like: it persists through the function it performs and is regenerated. A one-off disturbance or a depleted soil seed bank is a spent part. The two behave differently across repeated seasons, and a species list records neither property. Paine's starfish and Jones et al.'s engineers are the world instances; the figure point is that "spent versus regenerating" is information the bond must carry or it is lost.

**Falsifier:** if a part's effect is identical on first and subsequent uses, there is no catalyst/spent distinction in that case and this reading does not apply to it.

### Q5 - What about cycles?

Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

Nutrient cycling is the obvious instance: the output of decomposition re-enters as input to primary production. A chain (a one-way species list) cannot close a cycle by construction. Whether a cycle closes depends on the sign of the loop (a negative feedback closes; a positive one runs away). The insurance literature's feedbacks and May's complexity-stability threshold are world corroboration that loop sign, not ingredient count, governs stability. An undirected union of species with no described residual is mute (Fig 8 :19-21); a described cycle has a sign and a direction.

**Falsifier:** if a nutrient pathway can be written as a closed chain with no re-entrant loop and still be stable, the cycle reading is unnecessary.

### Q6 - Which bond and which order does "essential" commit to?

Operators table: subtraction is "A with B removed or absent", test "is the second operand's absence the point? Yes", and "progress is a subtraction, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot say how far along it is." Comparison is "a threshold relation between two quantities", test "is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes", and "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one." Sequence is "A then B; B needs A to have happened" with the test "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes." Conjunction is "both hold at once", symmetric: "swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."

A presence-function correlation is a conjunction, symmetric. "Essential" is a subtraction (removal is the point) followed by a comparison (below-threshold is failure). Where the cascade has an order, sequence applies and swapping operands changes the thing: removing a pollinator before a host plant flowering is not the same as after. The ordered bonds are what a vocabulary of ingredients lacks; Fig 12 :112 "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."

**Falsifier:** if a symmetric conjunction can carry the full meaning of "essential" without a removal and a threshold, this ordered reading collapses and F6 of `01` is refuted. This is the same falsifier as in `01` section 4, deliberately repeated.

### Q7 - A strain, named

Fig 12 :125-127 "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add. Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds." In ecology a species list can be cheap to state (a short checklist) and very expensive to settle (years of removal experiments), while a compact interaction module can be costly to describe and cheap to refute with one knock-out. The two currencies do not add, so a claim that a community is "complex and therefore fragile" conflates description cost with action cost. This is a live type question, not a correction: the figures do not return a single complexity number. Recorded as a strain, not smoothed.

## CONCLUSION

A community is a bonded arrangement, not an ingredient list. Over a fixed species set, different interaction bonds make isomers; a functional group fixes the kind of outcome while the species identity is the qualifier; catalysts and spent parts behave differently across uses; nutrient cycles have a sign a chain cannot carry. "Essential" commits to the ordered bonds of subtraction and comparison (removal plus threshold), and, where the cascade is ordered, to sequence. A presence-function correlation is a symmetric conjunction and cannot carry the meaning. No figure line selects a species name, so the literal list remains the declared `gap` of `01` F10.
