# Identity, isomers and order in ecological redundancy

**Mode: `general` on the structural claims, `specified` on the ecology-facing ones. Not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The flat reading says two species in one functional group are interchangeable. The bonded reading says they may be isomers. Four questions are owed:

1. What is the difference between identity (≡), functional-group membership, and isomerism here?
2. Does the order of the ecological relations matter, and which bonds are symmetric?
3. Does the order of the test matter (substitution then condition change, or condition change then substitution)?
4. What is the analogue in a domain that is not ecology, and does it corroborate or merely repeat?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: species, conditions, relations, functions, slots. Bonds held: the seven, of which identity ≡, sequence →, production ⇒ and comparison ⋛ carry this question; negation ¬ is available for refusals. No new primitive is imported. The functional-group label is a flat formula over the species atoms; it records ingredients and not arrangement.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Identity, functional group, isomer

- Operators table :24-27: identity ≡, *"two names, one referent"*; the test is *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."* Applied here: the bond is confirmed by substitution with a reading, not by comparing trait lists.
- Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."* Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*
- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- So there are three distinct cases:
  1. **Identity for the function under test.** Substitution leaves no residual at the measured slots. This is ≡, settled by the ground.
  2. **Functional-group membership.** Same ingredient list, no bond recorded. This is the flat formula, Fig 12's collapse 6: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not"* (Fig 12 :14).
  3. **Isomer.** Same traits, different relations or positions, different behaviour under substitution. The group notation cannot tell cases 1 and 3 apart, which is precisely why the label misleads.
- **Falsifier written before the run:** if two species with the same trait list and the same interaction position are non-interchangeable in a way none of the seven bonds can express, this three-way split fails and the Operators table needs a re-census.

### Q2 — Which bonds are symmetric, and why it matters

- Fig 12 :59: conjunction + is symmetric; :69 identity ≡ is symmetric; :74 sequence → is ordered; :79 production ⇒ is ordered; :84 subtraction − is ordered; :89 comparison ⋛ is ordered.
- Ecological "interchangeable" is symmetric in the abstract: if substituting B for A loses nothing, substituting A for B should also lose nothing at the same slots and conditions. That is a property of the identity test, not of the species.
- The relations that carry the function are mostly **not** symmetric. Pollination followed by seed set is sequence: swap them and the substance changes. Predation producing a trophic cascade is production: the second term did not exist before the first fired. A threshold such as "one pollinator suffices until a density limit" is comparison ⋛, a threshold relation between quantities.
- Consequence: a claim that two species share a role can be symmetric at the ingredient level and ordered at the bond level. The two readings are not in conflict; they are about different quantities. Mode `general`.

### Q3 — Order of the test: substitution before or after environmental change

- Fetzer et al. 2015 (PNAS, open access, read this session) assembled 883 microbial communities across three abiotic environments and found that *"Shapes suggesting functional redundancy in one environment can change, suggesting functional differences in another environment."* The paper concludes that *"Species that are functionally redundant in one environment may become pivotal in another."*
- Applying Fig 12 :75 production (⇒: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*), the substitution result in environment 1 does not contain the substitution result in environment 3. The second is not a replay of the first; a new condition produced a new term.
- So the correct order of the assay is: (a) measure per slot in condition 1; (b) change the condition; (c) measure per slot again; (d) keep both lineages. Running only (a) and calling the pair redundant is Fig 4 :25 "A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion."
- **Falsifier:** if the same substitution residuals are obtained under two genuinely different environmental conditions, with the same cascade depth, the condition-dependence claim is refuted for that pair.

### Q4 — Aggregate curve versus per-slot reading

- Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :13: *"a global R near zero with one live slot residual is a legal state"*. Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*
- The biodiversity-function saturation curve is an aggregate instrument. A flat tail can be produced by genuine compensation or by one slot carrying a live residual that the total hides. These are different sources of one reading, the metamerism case of `05_what_shape_means.md` §4.
- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."* Bits price the description, actions price finding out whether it holds. A trait list is cheap to state and may take a whole field season to settle. Summing the two into one "redundancy score" is the error the figure names.
- **Falsifier:** the per-slot refuter in `01` §1.

### Q5 — The engineering analogue, deliberately outside ecology

- *Redundancy (engineering)* (Wikipedia, 2026, read this session) defines dissimilar redundancy: *"dissimilar redundancy uses different things. The idea is that the different things are unlikely to contain identical flaws."* It also notes that the failure probability formula *"assumes independence of failure events"*, with the counterexample of *"two power supplies connected to the same socket in such a way that if one power supply failed, the other would too."*
- This is Fig 2 :18-20's "one evidence pool, they never talk, and still agree" at a different stratum. Two redundant species that share a single vulnerability (same pathogen, same phenology, same water source) are one frame wearing two names under that stressor.
- The same article records Perrow's backfire: *"Redundancy sometimes produces less, instead of greater reliability"* because it adds complexity. That is a corroboration of the shape's conditional form, not a refutation: it says redundancy is a relation to a failure mode, not a property of the count.
- **Overlap check:** this source is from engineering and shares no evidence pool with the framework or with the ecology articles. It is a different witness for the structure, but it is still secondary; the figures decide.

## CONCLUSION

The flat functional-group reading and the bonded reading are different quantities. Identity for a function is a symmetric bond settled by substitution. Functional-group membership is a flat ingredient list. Isomerism is what the notation loses when two species share the list but not the arrangement. The assay is ordered: measure per slot, change condition, measure again, keep both lineages, and check independence of the frames. Nothing here names a species pair. Mode `general` structural, `specified` for any community, ungrounded.
