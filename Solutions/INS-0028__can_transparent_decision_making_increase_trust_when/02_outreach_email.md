**Use:** one to one, or as a cover note attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Subject:** One checkable claim about transparency and trust, with its refuter written in advance

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific claim I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read any theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with me. A one-line failure is more useful to me than an endorsement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **The residual.** Trust is not one number for a population. It is a gap measured per affected group, between what an institution's decision process predicts citizens will accept and what those citizens actually register after an unpopular outcome. Aggregating across groups is how a live signal disappears into an average.
>
> **The claim (`specified`, not `grounded`).** Under an unpopular outcome, disclosure increases trust only when it changes the institution's contact with the affected group's ground, by putting evidence about the decision's contact into the hands of someone outside the institution who can refuse the institution's account. Disclosure that only shows the institution's own reasoning is evidence about the maker, not about the gap, and should not be counted as a trust repair. More information is not monotone: a channel can saturate, go stale, or pool distinct groups into one reading. This is a conditional shape, not a measured effect size.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** Not that transparency is always good, not that a published average trust score settles anything, not that any particular disclosure design works in all fields, and not that this is a result. I have no measurement. The question of how much trust rises is a declared gap in this work.
>
> **What would refute the shape (written before this message):**
> 1. A disclosure of the same kind raises trust by the same sign and size across a documented change of the underlying grievance, with contact held constant.
> 2. Disclosure that shows only the maker's reasoning, with no outside verifier, closes the gap in the affected group.
> 3. A single population average fully predicts compliance in every affected group, leaving no live group residual.
> 4. A published account whose round trip from the decision and back has a measured gap of zero, with no saturation or pooling.
> 5. The institution's own verification of its own disclosure changes citizen contact.
>
> If any holds, the shape is withdrawn. What I would like from you is what you would count as a failure, in your own terms, before you see any framework.
