# What kind of term is a symmetry? Structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

If a symmetry is a composition leftover (F1) rather than a member, two supporting questions are owed: (1) under Fig 12's vocabulary, is a symmetry an **isomer**, a **functional group**, or something else? (2) In what order must origin and exactness be decided, and what analogue tells us a union compound is the right reading?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: space, time, energy (states) and spectrum, gradient, network (structures). Bonds held: the seven, with negation as a unary operator. No new primitive is available without import. The symmetry is not in atoms(Γ); it is in what composition leaves behind.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Is a symmetry an isomer or a functional group?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

A Lorentz-type symmetry is not an isomer of the six, because it is not built from the same atoms under a different bond; it is what several bonds leave behind. The better reading is closer to a **functional group at a level**: the presence of cross-kind composition fixes that *some* symmetry arises (the group says what kind), while which symmetry arises depends on the atoms and the bond (the qualifier says which member). This matches Fig 13 :43-44, where "Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member."

So the figure vocabulary permits saying "a symmetry is a kind of derivative", but not "this symmetry is the isomer of that one" unless two specific molecules are named over the same atoms.

**Falsifier, written before this conclusion:** if a symmetry can be shown to be a different arrangement of the *same* atoms as another symmetry, with the same ingredient set and only the bond changed, the isomer reading becomes available and the functional-group reading is downgraded. If no such pair exists, the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2 — Why does the symmetry decide whether an arrangement is new?

Fig 12 :92: "Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing." Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term; under an ordered bond, different orders are different terms.

This is the bridge from F1 to the rest of the derivation. "Symmetry" in the figure is a property of a *bond* that decides counting, not an ingredient. So when the question asks for the "origin of Lorentz invariance," the framework's own answer is categorically: a symmetry originates as a fact about how operands join, which is why it cannot be a primitive member. Mode `general` (structural), single-source for the definition (Fig 12), triangulated with Fig 13 :124-125.

### Q3 — What is the order of the two sub-questions?

Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

The dependency order is:

1. **D1, legality:** what composes across the two kinds (Fig 13 :74-75). Without this, there is no object called a symmetry to test.
2. **D2, binding:** how the physics frame binds onto the held atoms (metric, interval, generators). This is a binding, not a new member (Fig 8 :50-52).
3. **D3, settlement:** what an instrument can return (Fig 2 :29-30, Fig 6 :32-35).

A reading taken at D3 before D1 is not a weak reading of D1; by Fig 3 :22-23 it is "a reading of nothing." This is why a phenomenological bound cannot, by itself, answer the origin question.

### Q4 — Why is the compound a union, not a partition?

Fig 9 :54 "when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again"; :57 "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

Origin and exactness overlap in vocabulary (both mention space, time, symmetry) but have different currencies and different anchors: D1's currency is composition and its anchor is the figures; D3's currency is residuals and its anchor is a detector. They are therefore two conditions, not one condition split into cases. A single lever, for example "assume Lorentz invariance is fundamental," clears D1 by stipulation and leaves D3 entirely standing. That is the signature of a union (Fig 9 :55-56).

**Analogue.** The examplar's *isomer* case (silver fulminate and silver cyanate, Fig 12 :97-98) is a useful analogy for what a bond changes, but it is **not** an analogue for this question's structure: here the atoms are not the same and the symmetry is a leftover. The closer analogue is Fig 2's independence test: two terms that drew on one underlying fact would be one term wearing two names.

### Q5 — Does a settled symmetry become an atom at the next level?

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."

If a first-order composition over space and time is settled against the ground (decades of relativity tests), it can serve as an atom for a higher-order composition, for example the comparison of a settled space-time relation with a settled energy-time relation. That is recursion, not a new primitive. But note the open type question: at the higher level the operands are molecules-now-atoms, and whether they count as "quantities" for a threshold operator needs an instrument, not an argument. This is **strained for now**, named rather than hidden. It does not affect the verdict in `01`, which stops before that comparison.

## CONCLUSION

A symmetry is best read as a functional group at a level: its presence fixes *that* a symmetry arises, while which symmetry arises is a binding onto held atoms. It is not an isomer of the six and not a seventh member. The two sub-questions are a union compound and must be ordered D1 (legality), D2 (binding), D3 (settlement). The only live strain is the operand type of a higher-order comparison, which is recorded and not used in the verdict. The falsifier in Q1 is the one to fire if this reading is wrong.
