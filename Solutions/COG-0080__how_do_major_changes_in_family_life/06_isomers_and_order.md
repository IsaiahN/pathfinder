## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the three changes isomers, or a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- As usually described, the three changes do **not** share an atom set. Family-life change is largely relations and conditions; technological change is largely an introduced actor (the device, the platform) with its own valence; educational change is largely a curriculum and an institution. They share a **functional group**: each is a change in the habitat that alters contact. By Fig 12 :123 the group says what kind (habitat change) and the qualifier says which member (family, technology, education). So the weaker, defensible reading is **functional group**, not isomer.
- The stronger version: if all three are written over a **shared habitat atom set**, differing only in which actor or relation is introduced and which bond carries it, then they are isomers in Fig 12's sense: same atoms, different arrangement, different developmental substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it makes a checkable prediction: two changes over the same atom set should differ in outcome only through the bond, not through a hidden extra atom.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one of the three changes requires an atom the others do not share (for example, a platform's engagement optimisation that has no counterpart in family or education), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 plus the critical caveat below), single-source for the empirical test, which is unmeasured.

### Q2 - What is the order: sequence or production?

- Fig 12 :71-74 defines sequence (→): "A then B; B needs A to have happened." Its test: "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."
- Fig 12 :77-78 defines production (⇒): "A produces B; B did not exist before." Its test: "is there a B before A fires? No."
- Within a childhood, the dependency order from Fig 3 is a **sequence**: perception of changed conditions, then terms for what they afford, then a statement of progress, then a route, then learning and carrying. Swapping the steps produces a different thing (an intervention aimed at a link whose input has not arrived), so → is the correct bond for the chain.
- Across the membrane, the relation is more often **production**: a settled method handed to the next generation makes a new capability exist that did not exist in that generation before (Fig 4 :11, "a head start, never a recording"). But this is conditional. If what is handed down is a recording, the bond is → over an old occasion, not ⇒ of a new term, and the receiver repeats a past success (Fig 4 :26). The distinction is checkable: ask whether the receiving generation has a capability that did not exist before the handoff.
- **Strain named:** the figure warns that "the only bond a flat chain can express" is → (Operators table :34), so a record that writes every intergenerational transmission as a sequence will overstate replay and understate production. Re-census: the bond question is about what the handoff *does* at the receiving level, which needs the ground, not an argument. Marked, not hidden.

## CONCLUSION

The three changes are most defensibly members of a **functional group** (habitat changes that alter contact), with a real but unmeasured **isomer** claim available if they can be written over one shared habitat atom set. Their internal order is a **sequence** (→) by dependency, and the intergenerational handoff is **production** (⇒) only when it delivers a method that creates a capability the receiver did not have; a recording is replay and is →. The membrane round trip has a nonzero R_T whose three dropped constraints are named. No new primitive, no import that moves the wall, and no six-term developmental derivation is used.
