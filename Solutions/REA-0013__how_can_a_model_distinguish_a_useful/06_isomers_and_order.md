## DERIVATION

### Q1: Same features, different bond?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

A regularity and a coincidence can have the same ingredient set: same feature counts, same support, same in-sample accuracy, same codelength under a flat formula. What differs is the bond. In one case the term is bonded to the ground (it survives contact not produced by the frame); in the other it is bonded only to the corpus (it survives re-sampling of the same pool). That is exactly Fig 12's isomer case: the formula cannot tell them apart, and the behaviour decides.

- **Single-source at figure level** for the definition (Fig 12), **corroborated from a different pool** by the MDL article's biased-coin example (W1): same data, different hypothesis class, different verdict. The record of where each came from is what separates them (Fig 8 :51).
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two candidates with identical atom sets and identical bond structure to the ground behave differently at new contact, with no other difference to point at, then the bond is not the carrier and the isomer reading fails. The functional-group reading (Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member.") would stand instead: both are members of the same kind with different qualifiers.

### Q2: What is the order, and where does the chain usually break?

Formula step 2 (ROUTE) orders the internal work: SUPPORT before the search, REACHABILITY during, NOVELTY after a candidate exists, and REFUTED before a mint is offered. Fig 5 :35, :37, :39 states the same order as checkability, not importance.

Across the three domains the order is:

1. **Describe the residual in effect terms** (Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit").
2. **Price the candidate against the whole corpus**, per slot (Fig 1 :19).
3. **Refuse before mint** if a held term is what is wrong (formula step 2; Fig 5 :53: "refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so").
4. **Settle at the ground** (Fig 5 :32: "then check it against the ground, then carry it up").
5. **Promote a method, never a recording** (Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down.").

Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Most practical effort in this question lands on step 2, because that is where the measurements already exist. If the broken link is step 4 (no ground contact), then better fitting, more regularisation and more cross-validation folds are effort above the break. Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

**Falsifier:** show a case where improving the gate (step 2) reliably restores ground settlement (step 4) without any change in contact. That would reverse the dependency order and break Fig 3 :19.

### Q3: Do the two currencies add?

Fig 12 :126: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."

Fig 12 :127: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."

Fig 12 :128-129: "The two readings come apart in both directions. A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."

Here: a compact pattern may be cheap to state and ruinously expensive to settle (needs a new instrument or an external frame). A sprawling pattern may be expensive to state and refuted by one probe at the right slot. Neither reading predicts the other, so a scalar that sums description length and test cost is blind in both directions. Fig 12 :130: "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."

**Falsifier:** a single scalar, mixing bits and actions with fixed weights, that preserves the regularity/coincidence ordering under every re-pricing of either currency. That would collapse the two-currency claim.

### Q4: Partition or union?

Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."

Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."

The gate condition and the contact condition overlap. A term can pass the gate and fail contact (the common coincidence), fail the gate and later prove useful (a novelty the current code cannot compress), or pass both. A single lever that clears one condition leaves the intersections standing. So the answer must be searched as a union and recombined, not averaged into one score. Fig 9 :60: "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."

## CONCLUSION

The distinction is structurally an **isomer** distinction: same features, different bond to the ground, invisible in a flat formula and decisive in behaviour. Its order is descriptive, then priced, then refused where needed, then settled, then promoted as a method. The two currencies, bits and actions, must be held apart. And the conditions form a union, not a partition, so no single lever or scalar is sufficient. All four claims are `specified`, ungrounded, and each carries the falsifier written above.
