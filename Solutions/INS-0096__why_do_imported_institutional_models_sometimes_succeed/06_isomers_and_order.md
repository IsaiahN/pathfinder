# Structure, order and analogues: what actually crosses a border

**Mode: `specified`.** A simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

## RESIDUAL

A model M is held in source S. The same written form is installed in targets T1 and T2. T1 succeeds, T2 fails. Three questions are owed: (1) what is the object that crosses, (2) are T1 and T2 isomers under Fig 12, and (3) what is the dependency order that decides whether the chain reaches the objective link.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the comparator's frame: an accountability seat, a budget rule, a procedural rule, a reporting metric, a refusal or appeal route, and the informal norms already present in each target. Bonds held: the seven, including sequence (→), production (⇒), subtraction (−) and comparison (⋛). No new primitive is available without import. Anchor: the realized outcome per target slot, never agreement about the design.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What crosses the membrane? CORRECTION TO MY OWN FIRST PASS

- Fig 4 :22 *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."* Fig 4 :23 *"upward: a method that can be reapplied. Never the recording of a particular success."*
- Fig 12 :5 *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- A written institutional model is an ingredient list. The bonds (who can refuse whom, what evidence travels, which contact the rule keeps reachable) are not in the list. What must cross for success is the **arrangement plus the method for reapplying it**, or at minimum a head start that makes the target's own search cheaper.
- Fig 12 :107-109 *"Where it is not, the result carries forward and the deriving does not, so every lineage pays again."* If the target holds the text but not the record of why it worked, the target must re-derive the whole thing. That is the structural difference between a transfer that succeeds and one that fails.
- **Correction to my first pass.** I initially wrote that a transfer carries "the institution" intact. That is too strong. Fig 4 :27 *"The transform between scales loses information, and that is the point."* The transform is lossy by construction; no transfer carries the whole object. The correct claim is about *which part* is carried: generator versus recording, bond versus ingredient list. First pass retracted, replacement kept.
- **Falsifier:** a transfer in which only the verbatim text and one-occasion story crossed, no bonds or head start were recorded, and the target still reproduced the source outcome on a non-updating ground in more than one slot.

### Q2 - Are the two targets isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123 **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As the comparativist usually writes them, T1 and T2 "received the same model" is a statement about the ingredient list, not the molecule. If both targets hold the same institutional atoms (court, budget rule, reporting metric, appeal route) but arrange them with different bonds (one appeal route can refuse the metric, the other cannot; one budget rule produces a new enforcement action, the other only sequences a report), then T1 and T2 are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the copied text and decisive in the outcome.
- If they share only the arrangement kind (both have a separated accountability function) while filling it with different atoms, they are members of one **functional group**: the group says what kind, the qualifier says which member.
- **Which reading applies is empirical.** No figure selects it. The isomer reading is stronger and is the one worth testing, because it makes the same-text-different-outcome phenomenon a structural claim rather than a cultural residue. Mark `specified`, single-source on the figure definition (Fig 12), with an analogue from chemistry as corroboration only.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** two nominal copies of the model with identical atoms *and* identical ordered bonds in the two targets that still diverge. That kills the isomer claim and leaves a residual attributable to habitat contact or measurement, to be re-censused under D3 and D5.

### Q3 - What is the order of the chain?

- Fig 3 :20 *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Fig 3 :19 *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- The transfer chain reads: perceive the target residual, acquire terms for what the imported institution affords, say what would count as progress in the target's own terms, plan the enactment, learn and carry.
- Fig 3 :25-27: the middle link (having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress) has neither an obvious failure nor an obvious test, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments. *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- This is the predicted break in transfer studies. Effort lands on perception (data collection) and planning (reform design), because those have measurements. The objective link (what would count as success here, in a currency that does not update on the program's own report) usually has none. Effort above the break cannot rescue it.
- **Falsifier:** a transfer program in which the objective link has a working instrument that ranges against a non-updating ground, and the chain still breaks below it.

### Q4 - Import, refusal and the order of operations

- Fig 8 :53 *"Residual first, frame second."* Fig 9 :27-28 *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."* The target residual must be described in effect terms before the source frame is chosen. Picking a famous model and then finding where it fits is the apophenia reading.
- Operators table :58 *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*. If the target cannot refuse the imported convention when the ground turns against it, the transfer can only accumulate; it cannot correct. A refusal seat is a structural requirement, not a cultural preference.
- Fig 10 :47 *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"*. A transfer whose success metric is fixed by the imported rule has promoted a convention into a verdict.
- Fig 13 :115 *"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."* Rearranging the atoms of a model cannot add a missing capability. If the target needs an atom it does not hold, rearrangement is the wrong instrument; a provenanced import is owed. Importing against a budget limit adds an atom that was not needed (Fig 6 :51-52).

### Q5 - The silent failures at the border

- Fig 11 :66 *"Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."*
- Fig 11 :67 *"Move the residual and you must reproduce everything its goal required, and you inherit the new habitat's actors, which you did not choose."*
- Fig 11 :68 *"what you failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails"* and Fig 11 :69 *"what you unintentionally introduced is invisible until it acts"*.
- A pilot enclave or a test harness is a substituted habitat: Fig 11 :70 *"A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability."* Success in the pilot proves the wiring, not that the model works when the surrounding habitat is the real one.
- **Falsifier:** a transplant in which every condition the source goal required was explicitly enumerated and reproduced, no unchosen actors entered, and the model still failed. That would return the derivation to the weighting-and-channel domains (D4, D5) rather than the habitat domain.

## CONCLUSION

What crosses a border is a method or a head start, never a whole object. The copied text is an ingredient list; the arrangement and the derivation record decide whether the target pays the bill again. Two targets that hold the same atoms under different bonds are candidates for the isomer reading; two that share only a functional group are a weaker claim. The chain usually breaks at the objective link, where no instrument exists, and import is owed only when the target lacks an atom, not when it lacks effort. Every transplant is a habitat substitution with two silent failure modes. This framing rescues the checkable part of the transfer question without producing a universal recipe.
