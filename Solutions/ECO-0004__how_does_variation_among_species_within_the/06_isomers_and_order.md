# Isomers, catalysts and order: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the bond and order rules, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit establishes that the group fixes the kind and the qualifier fixes the member. Two supporting questions remain:

1. Are two species in the same functional group **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, and what exactly is held fixed for that claim to be legal?
2. Does **order** matter, either in the sense of a bond (which condition arrives first) or in the sense of Fig 12's catalyst and cycle structures (which members are spent and which regenerate)?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this supporting derivation: the effect traits that place a species in the group (the "what kind"), and the response traits that say how it performs under a condition (the "which member"). Bonds held: the seven typed relations, especially `+` (symmetric conjunction), `→` (ordered sequence) and `⋛` (ordered comparison). No new primitive is available without import. Anchor: the focal function measured through the perturbation. Same four domains and the same union as `01`; this file works inside D1 and D3.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are within-group species isomers? NARROWED, NOT REFUSED

Fig 12 :117, verbatim:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :123, verbatim:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Take two species A and B in the same functional group. If "same functional group" means the same effect-trait atom set (both graze the same algae, both pollinate the same plants, both remove the same dung), and they differ in response traits (A performs under cool conditions, B under warm), then:

- the ingredient sets are the same at the level that decides group membership;
- the bond to the condition differs (A's performance bond is to cool, B's to warm);
- the behaviour differs decisively when warming arrives.

That satisfies the figure's definition. They are isomers over the fixed effect-trait atom set.

**The fixed-atom condition is load bearing.** If B also brings a trait A lacks (a different diet, a different habitat, a new mutualist), the atom set has changed and the relation is no longer isomerism. It is composition plus an import or a subtraction, and Fig 6 :22 applies: *"NOVEL means novel relative to atoms, not to the world. IMPORT adds an atom, and the added atom is what expands the closure."* The earlier, unqualified isomer claim is narrowed and the narrowing kept visible here and in `01` F5 / `11`.

**Falsifier, written before the conclusion:** if the within-group difference changes no system-level outcome under any perturbation, the arrangement is not decisive, the isomer claim is empty, and the functional-group line collapses into a mere label. A second falsifier: if no fixed effect-trait atom set can be specified for the group, the "same atoms" premise fails and the claim is withdrawn rather than strained into a metaphor.

### Q2. Why the group member list is not the molecule

Fig 12 :5 and :94, verbatim:

> **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
> **"The arrangement is the substance."**

A trait table that lists species and their traits has recorded the ingredients. It has not recorded the bond between a member's response and the condition that selects it. Fig 12 :46: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."*

Concretely: two records can both say "species present, performs function F". One may be a member that performs F *and* a different set of conditions; the other a member that performs F only in the condition currently observed. The table cannot distinguish them. Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*

This is also why a mean trait value is not a response diversity measure. Averaging presumes a symmetric bond where the operative bond is ordered (the member performs *given* the condition). Under a symmetric bond Fig 12 :92 says k operands have k! spellings and one term; under an ordered bond different orders are different terms (Operators :60-62). The wrong bond choice undercharges or overcharges the description in opposite directions.

### Q3. Catalysts and spent parts: a second structure inside the group

Fig 12 :119, verbatim:

> **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

A resprouting plant after fire, a seed bank that survives a drought, a pollinator that recolonises after a heatwave: these are parts that survive their own use. A tree killed by the perturbation and replaced by a different member is a spent part. Both may be recorded in a trait table as "present in the group", and the figure says nothing records which is which. That is a named structural failure, not an ecological analogy invented for this case.

Consequence for the main verdict: response diversity has at least two sub-structures, differing-response members and regenerating members, and they are not the same term. Fig 12 :121 adds the cycle: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Recovery after disturbance is a cycle only when the output (re-established function) re-enters as input (habitat condition for the next generation). Whether a given system's loop closes is an empirical question, not a figure verdict.

**Falsifier for Q3:** a longitudinal study shows that "survives its own use" and "spent and replaced" members have identical post-perturbation trajectories under all conditions measured, so the distinction carries no information. Then this sub-structure is dropped without touching F1 to F4 of `01`.

### Q4. What order is the union?

Two distinct order questions must not be conflated.

**(a) Order of operands in a comparison.** `⋛` is an ordered comparison bond: Operators :50, *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."* A group's response under warming is a comparison between the condition and each member's tolerance. It is not a symmetric conjunction of member and condition; swapping them produces a different claim. Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* The restoration goal (persistence of F) is a comparison, and the route to it is a sequence.

**(b) Order of perturbations through time.** `→` is sequence, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :72; Operators :31). A drought followed by a heatwave is not the same molecule as a heatwave followed by a drought: the first may remove the member that would have carried the function in the second. Fig 13 :88-89: *"The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no two passes begin from the same place."* The member set after the first perturbation is an input to the second.

Fig 13 :115 keeps the scope honest:

> **"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."**

Species within a group can be re-arranged without limit; the set of response types they hold does not grow by re-arrangement. A new response type has to arrive from a differing closure: a migrant, a novel mutation, an introduced actor, or a managed intervention. Fig 11 :61: *"Introduction is available; exclusion is not."* Fig 11 :63: *"Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."*

**Falsifier for Q4:** a demonstration that perturbation order has no effect on the focal function across a set of independent sequences, with the group held fixed. That would dissolve the ordered-bond reading for this case and leave only the symmetric comparison.

### Q5. The two currencies again, at this level

Fig 12 :125-128:

> **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
> **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
> **"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind."**

Describing a response-diverse group (many members, many bonds to conditions) costs bits. Finding out which member actually carries the function under a given perturbation costs actions: a field measurement, a transplant, an experiment. A group can be cheap to describe and expensive to settle, or the reverse. Summing them into one resilience score is the error Fig 12 :130 names. `01` F7 and `09` carry the same split at the system level.

## CONCLUSION

Two species in one functional group are **isomers over a fixed effect-trait atom set**, with the fixed-atom condition stated as part of the claim. The member list is not the molecule: the bond between response and condition carries the information the list loses. Catalysts and spent parts are a second, separate structure, and order matters both inside the comparison and across successive perturbations. No figure selects which member, which variance, or which sequence is optimal; that is the `gap` in `01` F8. This framing rescues the structural content of the question without naming a species or importing a number.
