## Restating the question as an effect

Two clocks run against one another: the environmental clock moving the adaptive optimum away from a population's current trait distribution, and the evolutionary clock moving that distribution toward the moving optimum through heritable variation and selection. Option A ("yes, evolution is fast enough") asserts that the evolutionary clock closes the gap before any population's absolute fitness goes negative for long enough to be lost. Option B ("no, evolution is too slow") asserts the converse. Both options, as worded, treat an ecosystem as one slot with one clock. The figures return a different shape: the residual is indexed per slot (Fig 1 :19), the comparison is local and per habitat, and the anchor is the ground for each slot, not an aggregate verdict.

## What an answer would look like (bound as an ordinary term)

An answer would be a shape with four parts, written down before the run so it can accrue a residual like any other term: (1) a threshold comparison between two rates, (2) two per-generation factors, either of which at zero stops adaptive progress, (3) a per-slot cascade rule saying how one lost population touches others, and (4) a statement of which side of the comparison the ground must settle. Anything that returns a single global yes or no without these four parts is the wrong shape.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with the shape claim.
- No bulk-send plan.

Three reasons, in the figures' own words: a room's agreement is not a verdict (Fig 7 :35); selecting for likely agreement manufactures the correlation that disqualifies a verdict (Fig 2 :40); and asking a reader to hold the framework before giving a verdict spends their independence (Fig 10 :42).
