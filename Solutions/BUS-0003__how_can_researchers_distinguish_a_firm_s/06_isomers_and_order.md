## Q1 - Two sources, one reading: metamer or isomer?

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :124)
> **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** (Fig 12 :92)
> *"If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information"* (*The Hard Problem is About Source*, read 2026-11-22)

Two explanations of one high return (a protected resource versus a favorable habitat) have different atoms, so under Fig 12's strict definition they are **not** isomers of each other. They are **metamers** in the source essay's sense: different underlying causes producing an identical reading, where the reading is a lossy projection. The essay's spectrometer example is the governing case: green can come from one green light, from yellow plus blue, from RGB, from CMYK, or from a scheme not yet discovered. The reading did not discriminate; the instrument did.

The isomer reading becomes available at a different level. Two firms with an identical resource list are isomers if they bond those atoms differently: one composes them as a repeated sequence, the other as a reconfigurable network. Under Fig 12 :117 the arrangement is itself a substance, and the difference is invisible in the resource list. This is the figure-grounded version of "it is not what you have, it is what you do with it", stronger because it says the bond carries information the ingredients do not (Fig 12 :5, *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*).

If two firms share only a pattern (both use `state → routine → market`) while differing in which atoms occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: they are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), the qualifier saying which member. The presence of the pattern fixes the kind, not the source of the reading.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two firms with identical atoms and identical bonds nevertheless diverge in source attribution with no difference in ground contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D3), which moves the explanation out of D2. Separately, if a reading maps uniquely to a source, the metamer framing is insufficient and the source essay's central claim fails.

**Status:** `specified`. Triangulated for the legality of the isomer reading (Fig 12 plus Fig 11 :50, different figures); single-source for the business application; framework-internal for the metamer reading.

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20-21)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)

Order for a researcher facing a performance reading:

1. **Perception.** Can the return be read at all? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests (accounting, share, survival data).
2. **Vocabulary.** Does the frame have terms for the candidate sources, and can it say what would count as discrimination? This is the middle link. Fig 3 :26: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."*
3. **Objective.** Can a comparison be stated that would count as getting somewhere? Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot state a source.
4. **Planning.** A contrast or design that could separate the sources under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what discriminated, verify it, carry it to the next firm.

The four conditions map onto this chain: P (per-slot) is a failure of links 1 and 2; S (discriminator) is a failure of links 2 and 3; A (anchor) is a failure of link 3; I (independence) is a failure of links 4 and 5, because the design inherits the same pool. That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear all four (Fig 9 :55).

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* Link 1 has accounting and share data; link 3 has significance thresholds that are gates, not grounds (Formula step 5); link 2, the source vocabulary, has almost none. So budget piles up at links 1 and 4 while the break sits at 2 and 3.

**Falsifier:** a study whose chain demonstrably works at 1, 2 and 3 and fails only at 4. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim of the primary audit's F8 analogue.

## Q3 - Competing explanations, and what each can and cannot reach

| candidate explanation | what it names | figure-line bearing | what it cannot reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bootstrap performance tests** (Fama and French 2010; Harvey and Liu 2022) | a distributional benchmark for whether extreme returns exceed chance | real proto-instrument; Fig 6 :32 | The same mutual-fund evidence pool, and a benchmark is not a firm-level source discriminator |
| **Potential outcomes and instrumental variables** (Imbens and Wooldridge 2009) | a contrast that defines a causal effect | closest formal name for S and A | Requires an instrument that changes only the source; no such instrument is held for "genuine advantage" |
| **Significance and variance decomposition** | a threshold or a share of explained variance | Fig 9 :60: a filter for budget | Which named slot is genuine; an average erases the per-slot signal (Fig 1 :22) |
| **Factor models** | controls adopted from asset pricing | an import from another closure (Fig 6 :23); provenance owed (Fig 8 :51) | Discriminating a resource from a habitat when both load on the same factor; no source gate |
| **Hot-hand and randomness perception** (Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky 1985) | the human tendency to read a streak into chance | low-overlap corroboration of F5 | A firm-level market measurement; different stratum (Fig 4 :20) |
| **Meta-science and registration** (Ioannidis 2005; AOM Registered Reports) | bias, stake, pre-specification | corroborates F3, F9 | The source discriminator itself; it improves the checker, not the instrument |
| **Mechanisms in economics** (Deaton 2010) | the demand for mechanism over reading | corroborates F4, F8 | A specific firm-level discriminator |
| **Luck / information asymmetry** | an unearned early reading that persists while unfuted | Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* | Cannot be excluded from inside a frame. Honest `gap`, not a rival verdict |

No candidate is discarded by prestige or familiarity. Each is ranged against the same residual in the order Fig 8 :53 requires. The point is not to pick one; it is to notice that the first four are methods claims nested in related evidence pools, while the cognition, meta-science and economics sources are low-overlap witnesses for the *shape of the problem*, not for the firm-level fact.

## Q4 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the bootstrap emphasis on chance benchmarks, as an instrument already returning something (Fig 6 :32, F10);
- the causal-inference emphasis on contrasts, as S and A;
- the registration emphasis on pre-specification, as the verification seat's non-access (Fig 10 :42, :43);
- the mechanism emphasis, as F4 and F8.

It cannot generate the *name* of the source that produced a given firm's run. That is the `gap` recorded in F11 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 (*"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*) and Fig 8 :39 apply. The source essay states why: a reading *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists."*

## CONCLUSION

Two explanations that fit one reading are metamers under the source essay, not isomers in the strict Fig 12 sense, because their atoms differ. Two firms with the same atoms but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117, and two firms sharing only a `state → routine → market` pattern are members of one functional group under Fig 12 :124. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the source vocabulary and the objective the usual breaks, which is why S, I, A and P need different instruments. The standard method answers are mostly bindings generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim and the metamer claim each have a written falsifier; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
