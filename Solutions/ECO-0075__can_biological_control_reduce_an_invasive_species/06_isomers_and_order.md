# Union compounds, cascade order, and analogues

**Mode: `general` for the structural claims, `specified` for any habitat-specific reading.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed beyond `01`: (1) is the reduction-plus-safety question a union compound or a partition, and (2) what is the order of the contact cascade such that enumeration can stop? A third question is asked as an analogue check: are two control agents with the same host range different substances under Fig 12?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the invasive species, the native community, the conditions, the relations, and the candidate agent as an import. Bonds held: introduction, predation, competition, apparent competition, dependency. No new ecological primitive is available without an import. The question is a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones.

## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  Union, not partition

- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
- Fig 9 :56 states the lever effect: a single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds.
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- ⇒ "reduce the invader" and "hold every other slot within tolerance" share the same agent and the same habitat. They cannot be searched separately and recombined afterwards. The release is one intervention into a union.
- **Falsifier:** a control tool whose effect on the target is provably disjoint from every non-target contact. Then the union degenerates to a partition and the unbundling is unnecessary. Write this down before the run; it is the same as refuter 1 in `01`.

### Q2  -  What order is the cascade?

- Fig 11 :44 requires enumeration outward "until the cascade stops mattering."
- Fig 11 :52 gives a worked depth: "first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out." The DDT example reached the eagles through the food chain.
- The cane toad case reaches the same depth from the other direction: the agent consumes the pest, poisons the pest's native predators, releases the crop-eating rodents those predators suppressed, and the direct benefit is outweighed by the mediated effect. The world source Shine et al. 2020 states that "any direct benefit of toads on agricultural production (via consumption of insect pests) likely was outweighed by negative effects that were mediated via the toads' impacts on other taxa" (abstract retrieved via search, publisher fetch blocked; corroboration only).
- ⇒ There is no figure-derived fixed order. The order is a property of the habitat and is read off the world. What the figure forbids is scoping the habitat to direct contact. **Specified, unmeasured** for any given site.
- **Falsifier:** a demonstrated bound on cascade depth that holds for a class of habitats, so "until it stops mattering" becomes a finite rule rather than an open-ended reading. This is refuter 2 in `01`.

### Q3  -  Are two control agents with the same host range isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Two candidate agents with the same host range but different phenology, dispersal or habitat use share the host-range atom and differ in the bond to time and space. By Fig 12 they are different substances, even though a host-range table (a flat formula of ingredients) cannot tell them apart. The world source Taylor and Snyder 2021 supplies the mechanism: apparent competition depends on when the agent is active relative to the susceptible non-target, and a moderate generalist can be more harmful than a specialist.
- ⇒ "Same host range" is not "same agent." The safer comparison is at the level of the whole arrangement, including season and dispersal. **Specified**, with the figure definition as the only authority.
- **Falsifier:** a case where two agents differ in phenology and dispersal yet produce identical target and non-target outcomes in the same habitat, so the arrangement carries no information. Then the isomer reading fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4  -  Is a laboratory host-range test a composition or a substitution?

- Fig 11 :70: "A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability."
- Fig 11 :68-69: what you failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails, and what you unintentionally introduced is invisible until it acts.
- ⇒ A quarantine test is not a smaller version of the field. It is a different habitat with different actors. It composes a result inside its own closure, and the result does not transfer for free. This is the figure-level reason post-release monitoring is part of the required shape.
- **Falsifier:** a substituted habitat that preserves every contact of the target habitat at the same scale, so the round-trip gap is zero. No such habitat is asserted here.

## CONCLUSION

The reduction-plus-safety question is a union compound and must be unbundled before search. The cascade has no figure-derived fixed order, only a forbidden shortcut (direct-contact scoping); the actual order is a ground reading. Two agents with the same host range are different substances under Fig 12 when their bonds to season and dispersal differ. A host-range test is a substituted habitat and proves wiring, never capability. Each of the four claims has a written falsifier.
