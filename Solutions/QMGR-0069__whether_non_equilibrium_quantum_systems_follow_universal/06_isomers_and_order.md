# Is a "universal law that mirrors gravity" an isomer, a functional group, or only the same kernel?

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The question says two domains obey laws that "mirror" one another. The figure set has precise terms for same-atoms-different-arrangement, for shared substructure, and for recurrence across levels. Which term applies? And at what order of composition does the comparison sit? Answering this decides whether the "mirror" claim is a real structural claim or a loose analogy.

Atoms held: Space, Time, Energy (states) plus Gradient, Network, Spectrum (structures). Bonds held: the seven, including sequence → and comparison ⋛, plus negation ¬. No new primitive is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are the two cores first-order derivatives of the six-term set?

Fig 13 :74: **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."**
Fig 13 :75: **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."**

A first-order molecule is `state - structure - state`, one cross-kind step.

- A candidate D1 core: `Energy - Gradient - Time` (a driven system's energy captured into an ordering over time, as in a fluctuation relation or a entropy-production bound). Legal at first order.
- A candidate D2 core: `Space - structure - Time`, the figure's own schema from the exemplar's derivation. Legal at first order.
- Fig 13 itself licenses more than one such molecule: *"A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."*

**Result:** each core is a first-order molecule *if* the physics-frame binding is accepted. The binding is `specified`, unmeasured. Triangulated for legality (Fig 13 :74-75 plus the shape rule); single-source for the particular physics mapping.

### Q2: Are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

- Read minimally, the two candidate cores do not share an atom set: D1 uses Energy, D2 uses Space. They are therefore **not isomers** under Fig 12's definition. They are two members of a **functional group** (`state - structure - state`), which is the weaker claim.
- Read strongly, if both are written over the full shared atom set `{Space, Time, Energy, Gradient, Spectrum, Network}` and differ only in which structure mediates which state pair, they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance.
- The "mirror" wording does not distinguish these two readings. That is the defect in the question as asked.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either domain's law can be written without an atom the other requires (D1 with no Space, or D2 with no Spectrum), the strong isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. If both require all six, the strong claim survives as a checkable structural hypothesis.

### Q3: At what order is the comparison?

Fig 12 :103: **"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."**
Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"**

D2 is settled against the ground over decades of experiment; D1 is partially settled (fluctuation relations are experimentally verified, the chaos bound is a conjecture with supporting results). A comparison between them is therefore second-order only to the extent that both first-order molecules are settled enough to serve as atoms. Where D1 is not settled, the comparison is not a legitimate second-order composition; it is a proposed import.

**Strain named:** the Operators table defines ⋛ as *"a threshold relation between two quantities."* At second order the operands are molecules-now-atoms. Whether those count as quantities at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. Triangulated for the recursion (Fig 12 :103-104); strained-for-now on the operand type.

### Q4: What does "mirror" add that the isomer reading does not?

Nothing at the level of form, and something dangerous at the level of evidence. There is no bond called "mirror" in the operators table. The figures' only mirror object is Fig 7's chain: *"same closure, handed along; agreement compounds, evidence does not."* Where two fields rhyme because they share a culture, the rhyming is that chain. Where they converge from separate instruments, it is Fig 8 :50: *"Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence."*

So the honest translation of the question's "mirror" is: *are the two cores isomers over a shared atom set, and is their agreement convergent rather than adopted?* That is two checkable questions, which is exactly the union finding in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` F1.

### Q5: Does the continuum make them the same substance?

Fig 13 :43-44: **"A continuum was the seventh and was removed for failing exactly this: it is a spectrum without gaps, or smoothness over space and time."**

The analogue-gravity literature shows that horizon-like and continuum-like behaviour can appear in fluids and condensates (Barceló, Liberati and Visser, *Analogue Gravity*, cited in the fetched overview). But the continuum is a derivative in this framework, not a primitive. So a shared continuum would be a shared *derivative*, which is allowed, not a shared atom, which is forbidden. It does not make the two cores the same substance.

## CONCLUSION

The two cores are first-order molecules of the six-term set, each legal under Fig 13 :74-75. They are **not isomers as minimally written** (they share no full atom set); they are members of one functional group. The stronger isomer claim, over the full shared atom set, is real and has a stated falsifier. Their comparison is second-order only where both molecules are settled, and the operand type of ⋛ at that level remains a named strain. "Mirror" adds no bond and no evidence. It translates into two checkable questions: arrangement over a shared atom set, and convergence at low overlap.

This rescues what is defensible in the question without inventing a new primitive: the order and the isomer test need no seventh term, and no claim that a specific physical law was derived.
