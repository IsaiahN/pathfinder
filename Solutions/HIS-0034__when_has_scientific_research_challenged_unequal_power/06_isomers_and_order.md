# Are the two valences isomers? Operators, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The audit left two things unexplained. First, the two valences were said to use different operators, but the figure vocabulary for "different arrangement of the same parts" is **isomerism** (Fig 12 :117). Are challenge and justification isomers, or something else? Second, the question asks "when", which is an ordering question: what must happen before what?

Atoms held in the sense relevant here: a research act, a held regularity, a ground, an interest. Operators held: seven bonds plus the unary negation. No new primitive is admitted. Scale: individual, institution, society, with a lossy transform between (Fig 10 :68).

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are CHALLENGE and JUSTIFY isomers under Fig 12? No.

Fig 12 :117, verbatim: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :112: "A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond." Operators :70-72: "negation is the one operator here that acts on a single operand... Negation joins nothing, so its symmetry entry is empty rather than no."

A challenge, as bound in `01` §0, applies ¬ to a held regularity: it is a **unary refusal**, not a relation joining two operands. A justification composes a settled regularity into a frame: it is a **bonded composition**. The two cases therefore do not share the structure isomerism requires. They differ in **arity of operator**, not in the arrangement of a shared atom set.

This is the correct figure-level answer, and it matters: if we called them isomers we would be forced to look for the different bond, and the actual difference, the presence or absence of a refusal operator, would be lost. Fig 12 :140: "A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single binary bond can be shown to express both the refusal of a held regularity and the accumulation of a settled one without a unary operator, the arity claim fails and the isomer reading should be reinstated.

### Q2: Are they at least a functional group?

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Both cases contain the same substructure: a research act plus a ground that can register it. What fixes the outcome is not the substructure but whether the ground was moved and whether a refusal was admitted. So the honest weaker claim is a **shared functional group with different operator families**, not a shared atom set under different bonds. This is weaker than isomerism and stronger than mere analogy. Single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12); the world sources do not use this vocabulary, so there is no corroboration to claim.

### Q3: What is the order? Refuse, settle, promote.

1. **Refuse before mint.** Formula step 2 routes a persistent, non-trending residual to REFUTED: "a term we hold is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation." `01`'s F2 and F3 both depend on this. A challenge that skips the refusal and immediately proposes a new account is out of order.
2. **Settle at the ground.** Formula step 5: "the ground settles it. Verified only by what pays. A gate is not the ground." A refusal that is paid for by an audience rather than a ground is not settled.
3. **Promote only what is settled.** Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world." Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down." A result becomes usable at the next level only after it is settled, and what crosses is the method, not the recording.

**Order falsifier:** show a case in which a result composed successfully into a higher-level frame before it was settled at its own ground, with no later settlement, and in which that composition still paid. Then step 3 is not a requirement and the order collapses.

### Q4: Which analogues actually govern, and which are decorations?

Three analogues in the figures bear; each is checked for whether it governs a quantity or merely paints a picture.

A fourth analogue, "science is a tool that can oppress or liberate" (Bautista 2025), **does not govern** a quantity. It is a useful public-facing description of the phenomenon. It was tried in `01` F2, strained against the arity distinction, and re-censused to a different level rather than discarded. It is recorded here as a named strain, not hidden.

### Q5: Scale and the danger of averaging

Fig 10 :68: "Different actors, a different currency, a different ground, and a lossy transform between." Fig 12 :103: "Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."

A refusal at the level of a single paper (retracting a result) is not the same object as a refusal at the level of a field (rejecting a paradigm) or a society (withdrawing legitimacy from a classification). Each level has its own ground and its own currency. Averaging across levels would produce the aggregate error Fig 1 :22 names: "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." Verdicts are therefore kept per domain and per level in `07` and `09`.

## CONCLUSION

The two valences are **not isomers**. They differ in operator arity: one is a unary refusal, the other a bonded composition. They share a functional group (a research act plus a registerable ground) and differ in whether a refusal was admitted and whether the ground could be moved. The required order is refuse, settle, promote. Three figure analogues govern the derivation; a fourth, the popular "tool" framing, is retained as a named strain at a different level. Two falsifiers are registered above and neither has been run.
