**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Subject:** Check one claim about definitions of life before you decide whether the framing is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read any theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`general` on the shape, `specified` on research practice, not `grounded`):**
>
> The persistent disagreement about what counts as life is not one hard question that we have failed to answer. It is a union of well-formed sub-questions, because the disagreement does not shrink with effort and each candidate definition keeps working well on a different subset (a Darwinian definition works for lineages and fails on a sterile mule; a metabolic definition works for a self-sustaining cell and fails on a virion; a cell-based definition works for extant life and conflicts with an RNA-world replicator).
>
> The shape of a reasonable answer is therefore a family of definitions, each with four things written down: the question it is for, the actors and scale it ranges over, the ground that could settle that question, and a guard that stops it from deciding an outcome it was not authored to decide. No definition is the final one.
>
> **The guard is the part I most want checked.** A definition is a convention, not a measurement. It makes no claim about the world, so several of them do not contradict one another. But a convention that decides an empirical outcome has stopped being a convention. On a question like "did a self-sustaining evolving chemical system arise here", the anchor is the experimental and geological record, not any definition.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that all definitions are equally good (a definition that explains everything by saying nothing fails for the same reason a too-narrow one does); that consensus on a definition is evidence that it is true (agreement about a shared meaning is the product of a convention, not corroboration); or that this settles which definition your field should adopt. I am claiming only that a plurality of scoped definitions is scientifically reasonable, bounded by that guard.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. the disagreement demonstrably shrinks with effort to one definition that works on viruses, mules, RNA-world replicators, synthetic cells and hypothetical extraterrestrial life;
> 2. a definition is shown to make a world-claim that the ground settles true or false, so it is a verdict rather than a convention;
> 3. a definition is shown to settle an empirical origin question without an independent experiment or observation.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, I will retract or narrow the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figure text, not my summary of it, is the authority.
> - Verbatim figures with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Canonical usage rule (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`
> - The eight-step loop: `FRAMEWORK/figures/THE_FORMULA.md`
>
> **If you want to push back on the method:** the claim is derived from the figures, and the figures are offered as a generator of shape, not as a final answer. Write down what would refute your own result before you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
