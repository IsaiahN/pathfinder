# Structure and order: are plausibility and completeness isomers, and which comes first?

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The headline answer orders the two objectives. Two supporting questions are owed:

1. Are chemical plausibility and system completeness **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or do they stand in some other structural relation?
2. Which is upstream of which, and does that order apply to instrument development, to the verdict, or to both?

If the order is wrong, the recommendation in `01` is wrong even if the per-slot finding is right.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (carried from `01` §1, not rebuilt)

- R: two conditions with a non-empty intersection, so a union compound, not a partition.
- D1 chemistry frame: actors feedstocks and reactions; currency yield; anchor measured outcome.
- D2 evolution frame: actors replicators and compartments; currency heritable variation; anchor generational selection response.
- D3 design frame: actors experimenter and reviewer; currency residual and agreement; anchor the D1 and D2 outcomes.
- Dependency order (Fig 3): perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, carry. The vocabulary and objective link is the one usually unmeasured.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are they isomers? No. They are two conditions of a union, at different levels.

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Plausibility and completeness do not share an atom set. Plausibility is a property of a feedstock-and-condition set measured at the reaction scale. Completeness is a property of a lineage measured at the generational scale. They are not "same atoms, different bond"; they are different objects at different levels that meet in a compound. So the isomer reading is **refused**. The correct structural relation is Fig 9 :57-58, "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

What *would* be an isomer claim here: two different arrangements of the **same admitted feedstock set** that both satisfy plausibility and differ only in which structure mediates replication (for example, a vesicle-mediated arrangement versus a mineral-surface arrangement). Those are same atoms, different bond, different substance, and Fig 12 :117 would govern them. That is a real, checkable claim, and it is narrower than the question.

**Falsifier for Q1:** show two experiment designs whose admitted atom sets are identical and whose only difference is the bond arrangement, yet which do not differ in behaviour. If they behave identically, the isomer reading fails even in the narrowed form, and the designs are one term with two spellings.

### Q2: Which is upstream? Two different orders must be kept apart.

Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
Fig 12 :106: "Nothing composes over what was never admitted as a part."

**Verdict order.** For the claim "this experiment produced a prebiotically relevant evolving system", plausibility is upstream of completeness: what was not admitted as a possible prebiotic feedstock cannot be composed into a prebiotically relevant system. Settling completeness before plausibility risks proving a wiring result and calling it an origin result. This is Fig 11 :70, "A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability."

**Instrument order.** For developing the *instrument that can detect evolvability*, the order can and often should be reversed. tractable model systems (in vitro RNA evolution, directed evolution, protocell analogues) are used first precisely because they are measurable, even when their prebiotic plausibility is unresolved. The RNA-world source states this openly: "Regardless of its plausibility in a prebiotic scenario, the RNA world can serve as a model system for studying the origin of life." Fig 6 :31 requires this separation: "inward: is our representation adequate to hold it?" and "outward: does another frame already hold it?" are "two tracks" and "never read one against the other."

**Why this is not a contradiction.** Re-census: the verdict order governs what may be *composed into the answer*; the instrument order governs what may be *used to sharpen the probe*. Different quantities. Fig 6 :29-30 gives each its own failure mode. A reading that forced one order onto both would strain the figure, so it is refused by the strain test in `01` §5.

### Q3: What is the bond between the two objectives?

Fig 12 :79-82 defines subtraction as "A with B removed or absent" and comparison as "a threshold relation between two quantities". Neither fits.

- Not `+` (conjunction both hold at once), because the two are measured at different scales and cannot be simultaneously present as one reading.
- Not `→` (sequence A then B), because instrument development can run in the opposite direction from the verdict.
- Not `⇒` (production), because plausibility does not produce evolvability; it admits the atoms from which evolvability may be composed.
- The nearest fit is **⋛ comparison at the compound level**: the experiment asks whether the plausibility measure and the completeness measure stand in a threshold relation (does the system meet both conditions at once?). The Operators table defines ⋛ as "a threshold relation between two quantities", and the quantities here are the two per-slot residuals, which is a legitimate operand type. This is a `specified` structure, not a measurement.

**Falsifier for Q3:** show that the two residuals are incommensurable in the specific sense that no threshold relation can be defined between them, so ⋛ is mistyped. If that is shown, the compound must be reported as two unconnected verdicts rather than one qualifying experiment, and the design recommendation is revised accordingly.

### Q4: Is there an analogue from another field?

Goodhart's law (Wikipedia, 2026-11-23): "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The directed-evolution literature gives the laboratory version: "selecting for improvement in the assayed function simply generates improvements in the assayed function", leading to "evolutionary specialisation to the proxy without improving the desired activity."

These are analogies, not figure lines. They corroborate F2 in `01` from economics and from protein engineering, two fields with low overlap with origin-of-life work and with the framework. They predict exactly the failure the two-slot design is built to prevent: a programme that optimises the measured slot (yield, or binding to the proxy) while the unmeasured slot (a real evolving lineage) stays dead. Corroboration from a shared evidence pool would be worthless; here the pools are genuinely different (Fig 2 :31-32).

**Falsifier for Q4:** exhibit a programme where the assayed measure is also the complete goal, so optimising it cannot produce proxy specialisation. Then the analogy stops applying and the two-slot separation loses its cross-field support. The figure verdict would still stand on Fig 9 :60.

## CONCLUSION

Plausibility and completeness are **not isomers**; they are two conditions of a union compound that meet at a comparison (⋛) of two per-slot residuals. The narrower isomer claim about two arrangements of one admitted feedstock set is real and checkable. Two orders must be kept apart: plausibility precedes completeness in the **verdict**, while a tractable but implausible model system may precede a plausible one in **instrument development**. Fig 6 :31 requires that these be run as separate tracks. The structure is `specified`, the order is `general`, and neither is grounded.
