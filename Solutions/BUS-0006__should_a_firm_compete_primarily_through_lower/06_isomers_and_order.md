# Are the three levers isomers, and in what order do they come?

**Mode: `general` for the structural result, `specified` for any application to one firm.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit refuses the three-item list because it is a union, not a partition. Two supporting questions remain: (1) are lower cost and differentiation **isomers** under Fig 12, that is, the same atoms arranged differently, or are they different functional groups; and (2) what is the dependency order among perceiving the gap, changing the game, and competing inside the game?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the business frame: the firm, rival firms, customers, price, cost, features, margin, the accepted basis of competition, and the value chain. Bonds held: the seven of Fig 12, in particular sequence (→), subtraction (−), and comparison (⋛). No new atom is available without an import. A "new basis of competition" is the candidate imported atom.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are cost leadership and differentiation isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

- Written narrowly, cost leadership `firm → cost → price → customer` and differentiation `firm → features → value → customer` do **not** share the full atom set. The first needs cost and price; the second needs features and value. Under Fig 12's definition they are not isomers; they are two members of the same **functional group**, *compete inside the given game*. The group says what kind (a within-game position); the qualifier says which member (cost or differentiation).
- Written over the full shared business atom set, both are re-arrangements of *firm, customer, price, cost, features, value, value chain*, differing in which atom carries the margin and which bond connects it to the customer. Under Fig 12 :117 they **are** isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance, invisible in the ingredient list and decisive in behaviour. This is the stronger, testable form.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either strategy can be written without an atom the other requires, and no shared full set can be exhibited, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. Mark `specified`, ungrounded.

### Q2 — Is rule change an isomer too?

No. Rule change introduces a new basis of competition, which is a new atom relative to the current game. Fig 6 :22: *"IMPORT adds an atom, and the added atom is what expands the closure."* Fig 13 :115: *"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."* An isomer re-arranges what is already held. An import adds what was not held. Conflating them is the blend collapse of Fig 8, where *"their union adds nothing"* and two names are mistaken for two frames.

- **Falsifier:** show a "rule change" that is only a re-arrangement of the existing atoms with no new basis of competition and no expansion of what customers can choose. Under that result it is an isomer, not an import, and F3 of `01` fails.

### Q3 — What is the order?

Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**

1. **Perception and vocabulary.** Name the residual per customer slot. Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."*
2. **Objective.** State what would count as progress in the firm's own terms and make it measurable. If it is not, the chain breaks at link 3 and no lever choice is assessable.
3. **Closure test (the gate).** Ask whether the current game's closure can still pay. Fig 8 :32: *"Closure is idempotent. Composing inside a closed set never leaves it, so no search, however long, adds an atom."* If the answer is no, the next move is an import.
4. **Game change (if gate fires).** Introduce a new basis of competition with a source gate and a shadow test, then check that an external anchor pays. Fig 10 :47: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one."*
5. **Within-game position (after the game is set).** Choose cost or differentiation per slot. Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* The route needs all three bonds, not just a noun.
6. **Ground.** The market selects. Fig 7 :35: *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes."*

The order is part of the specification, not a preference (Seat Reasoning move 9). Reversing steps 4 and 5 puts the firm in the position of optimizing a position inside a game it has not yet chosen. Fig 9 :40-41: *"The room is summoned by the residual rather than composed."*

### Q4 — Does a settled strategy become an atom for the next level?

Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**

A position that the market has settled (a real cost advantage, a real differentiated offering) can serve as an atom for a higher-level composition, for example a portfolio of businesses. But Fig 5 :69-72 adds the warning: settling is necessary and not sufficient. *"A term is settled when the ground pays for it. A term is usable when something can compose over it."* A profitable position may still not compose if the arrangement does not carry. Mark `general` for the recursion, `specified` for any particular portfolio claim.

## CONCLUSION

Cost and differentiation are at minimum members of one **functional group**, *compete inside the given game*. Under the full shared atom set they have a real **isomer** claim: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour. Rule change is **not** an isomer; it is the import that adds a new atom and moves the wall. The dependency order is: perceive per slot, state a measurable objective, test the closure, change the game only if the gate fires, then choose a within-game position, then let the market settle. This rescues the useful part of the original question without naming a global winner.
