# Cycles, isomers and order in a pre-genetic network

**Mode: `specified` on the structural readings, `general` on the bond definitions.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two things are owed that the main audit does not settle. (1) Several candidate "first networks" are offered in the literature: an iron-sulfur surface metabolism, an alkaline vent micropore network, a lipid vesicle with encapsulated catalysis, and an abstract RAF over a food set. Are these rivals, variants of one thing, or instances of a kind? (2) What exactly is the order in "before genetic molecules", given that the figures forbid treating order as mere importance?

Atoms held in the figure vocabulary: a spendable gradient (Fig 13 :22, :46), a catalyst (Fig 12 :119), a cycle (Fig 12 :121), a boundary or habitat (Fig 11), a comparison threshold (Operators table), and a channel that can decay (Fig 10 :24). ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - A self-sustaining network is a cycle, not a chain

Fig 12 :121:

> **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Fig 12 :119:

> **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

A linear sequence of prebiotic syntheses can produce monomers, but it cannot close on itself. Closure requires that a product catalyze a reaction that leads back to the product, and that the catalyst survive its use. The "sign of the loop" is the threshold question: does production exceed loss? A chain is the degenerate case of a molecule with one bond type (Fig 12 :48, Symbols table :66), so a chain is not a different kind of object; it is a cycle that has not closed. This is why the figure's cycle line is the decisive structural definition for a self-sustaining network.

### Q2 - Are the candidate first networks isomers or members of a functional group?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :123:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Write the four candidates over the shared atom set {spendable gradient, catalyst, cycle, boundary, food flux}:

- iron-sulfur surface: a mineral catalyst in a flow, products retained by surface bonding;
- alkaline vent micropore: a proton gradient across an inorganic membrane, catalysts in the pore walls;
- lipid vesicle: a boundary that concentrates products, catalysis inside;
- abstract RAF: a food set and mutual catalysis, boundary left implicit.

Over that shared atom set they differ in which structure occupies the boundary slot and which occupies the catalyst slot. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The difference is invisible if one records only the ingredient list ("gradient plus catalyst plus compartment") and decisive in behaviour, because the vent version and the vesicle version have different failure modes.

If the atom set is not shared (for example if one candidate genuinely requires a new kind of membrane that the others do not), the isomer claim fails and the weaker **functional-group** claim stands: each is a member of the kind "cycle plus catalyst plus gradient", with a qualifier saying which member. The functional-group line is the honest default until the shared atom set is established by a measurement, which it is not here.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one of the four candidates can be shown to require an atom that is absent from the other three, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group claim replaces it. If all four can be written with the same atom set and only the bond or slot occupancy differs, the isomer claim stands as `specified`, ungrounded.

### Q3 - What is the order in "before"?

Three different orders must not be confused:

1. **Compositional order (within a scale).** Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Gradient before cycle before compartment before compounding channel. This is the order the figures govern.
2. **Recursive order (across scales).** Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"*. Once a network is settled, it becomes an operand at the next level, so heredity composes over it. This is the metabolism-first ordering as a composition relation.
3. **Historical order (in time).** No figure line governs this. Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*. Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*. "Composable before" is not "occurred before". Conflating (1) or (2) with (3) is the central error this folder refuses.

The word "before" in the question is therefore ambiguous between a dependency claim, which is supported, and a chronological claim, which is a `gap`. Both are reported; neither is silently substituted for the other.

### Q4 - An analogue, marked as an analogue

Fig 13 :4: *"There is no order of rank here. The two kinds are in lockstep, a crossing is a change of role rather than a step upward,"* and :28: *"They alternate, and the alternation is where both the recursion and the randomness come from."* This is about the six substrate kinds (states and structures), not about molecules in a reactor. Used directly on chemistry it would be a strain, and section 1.5 of `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` declines that reading. As an **analogue only**, it warns against turning the dependency order (D2 to D1 to D3 to D4) into a ranking of importance. A crossing of role is not a step upward. The gradient is not senior to the catalyst; both are load-bearing. The analogue corroborates the shape, not the chemistry.

### Q5 - Where comparison sits

Operators table :51: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*. A self-sustaining network has a comparison built into its closure condition: production versus loss, catalyst regeneration versus decay. That comparison is `⋛`, an ordered bond, not a symmetric conjunction. So "self-sustaining" cannot be represented by a flat ingredient list; the arrangement, including the threshold, is part of the term (Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*).

## CONCLUSION

A self-sustaining network is a **cycle of surviving catalysts** driven by a spendable gradient, not a chain. The candidate first networks are isomers over a shared atom set, or members of a functional group if the atom set cannot be shown to be shared; the isomer claim carries a stated falsifier. The "before" in the question splits into a compositional order (governed by Fig 3 and Fig 12), a recursive order (governed by Fig 12 :104), and a historical order (a `gap`). The last is not recoverable from the first two. No new primitive, no wall-moving import and no naming of a seventh kind is needed for any of these results.
