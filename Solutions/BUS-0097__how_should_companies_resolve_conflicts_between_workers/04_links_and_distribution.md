## What the wider literature bears on the question

1. **Two standing lists, one structure.** Friedman's owner-first rule and the Business Roundtable's all-stakeholders list are opposed in content and identical in form: both fix who counts before the residual is read. Jensen's objection to stakeholder theory, that it leaves managers with no principled way to make trade-offs, applies with equal force to both. This corroborates F3 and F1 only after re-mapping to Γ and the claimant habitat; the sources share a business evidence pool and count as one witness (Fig 2 :31).
2. **Negotiation is the named failure, from a different field.** Blattberg's criticism of stakeholder theory focuses on its "*emphasis on negotiation as the chief mode of dialogue*". Rawls's "independent criterion for a fair outcome" and Leventhal's "bias suppression" and "correctability" are the same structural requirement stated in political philosophy and organizational psychology. These are the genuinely low-overlap corroborators for F2 and F6.
3. **The unvoiced party is a measurement gap, not a missing name.** Waddock's category of "unvoiced or silent humans who have no voice in the present", the ombudsman proposals in the intergenerational-equity literature, and construal-level theory's account of psychological distance all converge on the same shape: what is missing is a channel that returns a reading, not an extra name on a list. Fig 6 :34-35 governs; the world sources corroborate.
4. **Commons governance without a central authority.** Ostrom's guidelines supply a third institutional frame in which conflict resolution and monitoring accountable to users are design variables. It rhymes with the figures but comes from fisheries, forests, and committees, not from the framework author's field.
