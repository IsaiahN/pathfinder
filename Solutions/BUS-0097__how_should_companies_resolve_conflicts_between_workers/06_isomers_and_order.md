# Priority lists, flat formulas, and the order of a governance arrangement

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed after `01`. (1) Is a standing priority list a distinct kind of governance object, or just another arrangement of the same atoms? (2) In what order must the parts of a resolution stand, and does hearing a party produce a change in that party's outcome? The first is a structure question; the second is an operator question.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the governance library: a claim, a right, a contract, a metric, a seat, a record, and an instrument. Bonds held: the seven, including sequence `→`, production `⇒`, comparison `⋛`, and the unary refusal `¬`. No new primitive is available without import. Five claimant slots are indexed by party, not composed as atoms of one substance.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is a priority list a flat formula?

- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- Fig 12 :6-17 names the collapse: three changes with one written formula, because *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not"*, so *"nothing downstream can tell them apart"*.
- A shareholder-first charter and an all-stakeholders charter both list the same ingredients: parties, interests, a board, a metric, a decision rule. What differs is the bond, that is, which party's claim is composed over which. The list of ingredients cannot tell them apart; the behaviour does.
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
- **Reading:** a priority list is a flat formula. It records ingredients and mistakes the record for the arrangement. What governs the resolution is the typed bond, not the inventory. Mode `specified`, triangulated for the notation claim (Fig 12 and Fig 2, different figures).
- **Falsifier:** two governance charters with identical ingredient lists and identical bonds whose outcomes diverge across three different conflicts. If that occurs, an unrecorded variable exists and the flat-formula diagnosis is incomplete.

### Q2 - Are the arrangements isomers over a fixed atom set?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Over the fixed atom set {claim, right, contract, metric, seat, record, instrument}, "owner-first governance" and "per-slot ranged governance" are **isomers** if they use all seven atoms and differ only in which bond attaches the seat and the metric. Under Fig 12 :117 that makes them different substances with the same ingredients, not a better and a worse version of one thing.
- If one arrangement adds an atom the other lacks, for example a channel returning a long-horizon residual, then they are no longer isomers. The relation becomes composition plus import (Fig 6 :23 - *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."*), and the import needs a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 6 :12-13).
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** show that one of the two arrangements can be written without an atom the other requires. The isomer claim fails and the composition-plus-import reading stands instead. This is a live strain, named in `11_audit.md`, because an ombudsman for future generations would be a new atom relative to an ordinary board.

### Q3 - What is the order? Dependency first.

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Order for a resolution: 1 perception of each party's outcome → 2 vocabulary for what that party affords and what progress would count as → 3 objective, stated per slot → 4 planning the action → 5 learning and carrying. A resolution that begins at step 4, for example appointing a mediator before any party's residual has been measured, spends effort above the break.
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** The objective link, step 3, is the one without an instrument in ordinary stakeholder processes.

### Q4 - Does hearing a party produce a change in its outcome?

- Fig 12 :70-72 defines sequence `→`: **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"**, with the test *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*
- Fig 12 :75-76 defines production `⇒`: **"A produces B; B did not exist before"**, with the test *"is there a B before A fires? No."*
- A hearing is `→`. A change in the party's measured outcome is `⇒`. A company can complete a sequence of consultations in which no outcome is ever produced: the hearing is real, the production never fires. Writing `consultation ⇒ fairness` mistakes a sequence for a production.
- Operators table :58: **"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."** A process that can record claims but cannot record "this claim does not hold" accumulates obligations without refusing any. The refusal is a unary operator; it is not subtraction, which only removes an operand (Operators :73-74).
- **World strain, kept open.** The procedural-justice synthesis reports Tyler's finding that giving members a voice can make a process viewed as fair even when the voice is *"a voice that will not have any weighting on the decision-making process"*. That is a real effect on perceived fairness with no production of outcome change. It does not refute the figure distinction; it shows that two quantities are being measured. Fig 4 :20 requires the anchor to be re-named at the new scale: perceived fairness is an instrument for a question about people, not the ground for a question about what happened to a party. Recorded as S4 in `01` section 6.

### Q5 - Why a standing list cannot be saved by iteration

- Fig 8 :32: **"Closure is idempotent. Composing inside a closed set never leaves it, so no search, however long, adds an atom."**
- A standing priority list handed along from one decision to the next is Fig 7 :12-13: *"same closure, handed along;"* *"agreement compounds, evidence does not"*. Adding more stakeholder meetings inside the same closure does not add a new term for the party whose residual does not register.
- Fig 13 :115: **"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."** Iteration explores arrangements; only import moves the wall. For future generations, the wall-moving move is an instrument imported with provenance, not a further meeting.

## CONCLUSION

A priority list is a flat formula: it records the ingredients of governance and omits the bond that decides behaviour. Shareholder-first and all-stakeholders charters are **isomers over a fixed atom set** when they use the same atoms with different bonds; they become composition plus import when one adds an atom the other lacks, and the import then needs a source gate and a shadow test. The order is dependency order (Fig 3), with the objective link the unmeasured one. A hearing is a sequence and a changed outcome is a production; conflating them is the error the procedural-justice "voice" finding makes visible without refuting it. Refusal is a unary operator that a governance vocabulary must possess or only accumulate. No figure returns the correct priority order; that is a `gap`, not a hidden answer.
