# Compounds, order and isomers in the transparency question

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The headline question treats transparency as a single lever applied to a single outcome. Three questions are owed: (1) what is the order of the compound, (2) is it a partition or a union, and (3) do two institutional arrangements with the same atoms differ only by their bond?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held, in effect terms: D (disclosure of a relevant record), A (an actor able to act on the record), S (a sanction actually applied), O (an outcome reading on corruption). Bonds available: the seven of Fig 12 plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. The anchor is the outcome reading O when it does not update on what the actors think (Fig 2 :29).

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the order? (Dependency, not importance)

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step"* that cannot use it.
- The dependency order is D to A to S to O: a record must exist before an actor can use it; an actor must act before a sanction exists; a sanction must exist before the outcome changes. This is an ordered bond (→ sequence, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*), not a symmetric one.
- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* If A or S is broken, increasing D is effort above the break.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a setting shows O changing when D changes while A and S remain absent and unchanged, the dependency order is wrong and D must be re-censused as a direct producer of O.

### Q2: Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific"* compound.
- The question's conditions (disclosure present, enforcement weak) overlap: weak enforcement changes what disclosure can do, and disclosure changes what enforcement can see. This is a union with a compound residue, not a list of two independent variables. A single lever (more disclosure) clears the region where only the disclosure condition holds and leaves the intersection standing.
- **Consequence for method:** the verdicts must be kept per domain and never averaged. An average effect across countries with strong and weak enforcement is exactly the "aggregation" that Fig 1 :22 warns about: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*

### Q3: Are the two regimes isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Consider regime R1: disclosure and oversight and sanction joined by **sequence** (D then A then S), with a pinned anchor. Consider regime R2: the same atoms joined by **conjunction** (D plus A plus S all nominal, but A cannot move S because S updates on the disclosed actors' preferences). Same atom set {D, A, S, O}, different bond and different anchor status, different substance: R1 reduces corruption, R2 does not.
- Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."* A policy recipe that lists "transparency plus oversight plus sanctions" as an ingredient list has recorded half of itself. The bond (who can act on whom, and whether the anchor is pinned) carries the rest.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two regimes with identical atom sets and identical bonds produce different corruption outcomes, and the difference is explained by an atom neither list contains, the isomer framing fails and an import is owed.

### Q4: The two currencies are not a single price

- Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."* Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"*
- D is paid in bits. S is paid in actions. A reform that reports only bits has not paid the action bill, and by Fig 12 :127 *"will not notice the shortfall. It proposes arrangements it cannot afford to test"*.
- The correct evaluation therefore has two columns, not a summed score. This is the structural reason a meta-analysis finding a small average effect does not settle the conditional question: the average mixes regimes that paid the action bill with regimes that did not.

### Q5: Negation: what a vocabulary without refusal can say here

- Operators table :57: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*.
- A reform vocabulary containing only "more transparency", "more oversight", "more participation" can accumulate but cannot say that the transparency-only arrangement is *wrong*. Fig 12 amendment: *"Without a unary operator a system can write what a situation contains, what follows what, and how far along it is: and it cannot write that any of those is false. A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."*
- The pre-registered refuter in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §4 is that unary operator, deliberately written before the conclusion.

## CONCLUSION

The compound is ordered D to A to S to O by dependency, not by importance. It is a union with a specific compound residue, so verdicts are kept per domain and never averaged. Two regimes with the same institutional atoms but different bonds (sequence with a pinned anchor versus conjunction with a movable one) are genuine isomers under Fig 12, which is why an ingredient-list policy recipe cannot predict which one reduces corruption. The two currencies, bits and actions, must be reported separately. None of this invents a new primitive; the remedy where the action atom is absent is an import into the habitat (Fig 11 :61, *"Introduction is available; exclusion is not."*).
