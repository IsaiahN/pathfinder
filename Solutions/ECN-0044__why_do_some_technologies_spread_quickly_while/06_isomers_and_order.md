# Structure, order and analogues: what else the question needs

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The primary audit leaves three structural questions open: (1) is the question a union or a partition, and what follows from the answer; (2) can two technologies that use the same components diffuse differently because the arrangement differs, and what does that license us to say; (3) where does the chain that should close the residual actually break? Each has its own falsifier.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Rogers' review notes that diffusion research has historically bundled "the innovation, the adopters, the communication channels and the social system" into one narrative; Comin and Hobijn's adoption data and Triplett's productivity-paradox list measure different objects.
- ⇒ Spread speed and productivity visibility form a union. The overlap is a compound with its own loop and its own ground. A single lever clears only the region where one condition binds; the intersection survives and is categorically different.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** one intervention that removes both the adoption lag and the productivity-visibility lag with no categorically different residue, across technologies and contexts, would collapse the union into a partition and refute this finding.

### Q2: Are fast and slow technologies isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Two technologies built from the same components but arranged differently (a different sequence →, or production ⇒ in a different order) are isomers under this definition: the ingredient list cannot tell them apart and the bond carries the difference. If their diffusion speeds differ after habitat is matched, that difference is carried by the arrangement, not by the atoms.
- A general-purpose technology is **catalyst-like**: it survives its own use, so its effect compounds across uses, and spent and regenerating instances behave differently. The world source for this reading is the historical electricity and ICT discussions in Triplett's Brookings review, which is corroboration only; the figure definition is primary.
- The weaker claim is a **functional group**: both technologies are "state → structure → state" style molecules using general capabilities, with different qualifiers in the slots. The stronger claim, full isomerism over a shared atom set, needs the technologies to be written over the same components.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two technologies with identical atoms and identical bonds (same arrangement, not merely the same ingredients) show identical diffusion speeds after habitat matching, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim survives. If two technologies with different atoms and different arrangements show identical speeds, neither claim is doing work.

### Q3: What order does the chain have?

- Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
- The chain is perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry. For technology diffusion: the using unit must perceive the artifact, have terms for what it affords, be able to say what counts as progress with it, plan a route under incomplete information, and name what worked so it can be carried to the next slot.
- The middle link (affordance vocabulary plus objective) is where the chain usually breaks, because it has neither obvious failures nor obvious tests. Fig 3 :25-26 describes why: perception and planning have tests, the link between them has neither. This matches Griliches' finding that differences in "origins" (when an adaptable hybrid appears and when seed producers enter) matter as much as differences in slopes and ceilings. Origins are a vocabulary-and-objective failure, not a planning failure.
- ⇒ Subsidising adoption (effort at planning or carry) cannot rescue a broken affordance vocabulary. More search at the top of the chain arrives at a step that cannot use it.
- **Falsifier:** adoption speed unchanged after a measured improvement in the using unit's terms for what the technology affords and what would count as progress with it, while planning and carry are held fixed.

### Q4: What do the two currencies price?

- Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."
- Fig 12 :126: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."
- A technology can be cheap to describe (a short specification, a widely copied design) and expensive to settle (each adopter must test it in their own context, pay for complements, and rebuild routines). The two readings come apart in both directions, and summing them is the error the figure names.
- This is why "it is obvious that this technology is better" and "it spread slowly" are not contradictory. The first is a bit-price claim; the second is an action-price observation. A vocabulary holding only one accounting will not notice the shortfall. Rogers' trialability attribute is the closest world concept to an action price; it is corroboration, not a figure line.
- **Falsifier:** a clean demonstration that description bits and settling actions are convertible into one accounting for technology adoption, with a fixed exchange rate that predicts speed across contexts.

### Q5: Refusal before mint

`THE_FORMULA.md` step 2 sorts the residual before minting: transferred, novel, broken rebinding, broken mechanism, refuted. For a slow slot, the first question is whether the complement is already in the closure (SEARCHED), then whether the instrument can be extended (INWARD), then whether a frame elsewhere holds the atom (OUTWARD). A refutation of the assumption that "the technology is good, adopters are slow" is a negation (¬), which is not a bond and cannot be written by a vocabulary that only accumulates terms. The Rogers review's "individual-blame bias" is the world-name for that failure. **Falsifier:** a slow slot is closed by adding new positive terms only, with no refusal of any held assumption about the technology or the adopter.

## CONCLUSION

The question is a union (Q1). Fast and slow technologies can be treated as isomers when the difference is in the arrangement rather than the components, with a catalyst-like special case for general-purpose technologies and a weaker functional-group reading otherwise (Q2). The chain breaks at the affordance-and-objective link, which has no instrument, so effort above the break cannot rescue it (Q3). Bits and actions price different things and must be held apart (Q4). Refusal of the "good technology, slow adopters" assumption precedes any mint (Q5). All five findings are `specified`, ungrounded, and carry written falsifiers.
