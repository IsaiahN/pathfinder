# Union, isomers and order in the dark-matter question

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the order, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question offers three candidates as if they were a list of alternatives. Two questions are owed: (1) is the list a partition or a union, and (2) if the rival models are written over a fixed atom set, are they **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or only members of a **functional group**?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: Space, Time, Energy (states) + Gradient, Network, Spectrum (structures). Bonds held: the seven plus negation. In the physics frame the working atoms are: observed baryons, gravitational potential, lensing convergence, rotation velocity, CMB acoustic scale, and a measured acceleration constant a₀. Candidate new atoms: WIMP, axion, sterile neutrino, primordial black hole. Candidate bond change: μ(g_N/a₀). No seventh substrate member is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is the three-option menu a partition? NO, it is a union.

- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

The overlaps are concrete, not rhetorical. Moffat 2007 fits the Bullet Cluster convergence map without non-baryonic matter, so the modified-gravity closure reaches into the cluster discriminator the particle camp treats as decisive. A primordial black hole is a compact object (energy in a slot) rather than a new elementary atom, so it straddles inventory and object-closure. An axion and a WIMP share the particle shape but are different atoms with different detectors. Unbundle first: describe each slot and ask which condition actually binds there.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the three options were mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive at every slot, with one lever clearing all three, the union reading would fail. No such exclusivity is observed.

### Q2 - Are the rival models first-order derivatives of the six-term set?

- Fig 13 :74: **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."**
- Fig 13 :75: **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."**
- Fig 13 :13-14: energy is **"conserved, finite, directionless alone"**.

A first-order molecule is `state - structure - state`, one crossing. Dark matter is not a missing *kind*; it is energy whose gravitational source is unregistered. The particle candidate adds an atom to Γ; the modified-law candidate changes the bond between mass and the gravitational potential; the import candidate brings an atom from a frame with a different closure. All three operate on the held set. Gravity itself maps to a structure between space and time only as an **unproven binding** inherited from the exemplar; this derivation does not use it to name anything.

- **Falsifier:** a derivation showing mass-energy is not among the six, or that a seventh member is composable. That would break the substrate and this reading with it.

### Q3 - Are the rival models isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Two readings, kept separate:

1. **As usually written, they are a functional group, not isomers.** A ΛCDM fit and a MOND fit do not share one atom set: one adds a non-baryonic halo atom, the other changes the force law and keeps the baryon inventory. Under Fig 12's definition, differing atom sets mean the *isomer* claim fails. What they share is the functional group *"account for gravitational acceleration from an observed baryon distribution"*; the group says what kind, the qualifier says which member.
2. **The stronger, checkable form.** If both are written over the full shared set {baryons, potential, lensing, rotation, acoustic scale, a₀}, differing only in which atom is added and which bond is changed, then they are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it converts a rhetorical either/or into a structural difference with a falsifier.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either model requires an atom the other demonstrably cannot be written without (for example a law-only fit that cannot produce the acoustic peaks, or a particle fit that cannot produce the galaxy regularities without a law change), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 - What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- Fig 5 :59: **"a term whose residual PERSISTS AND DOES NOT TREND DOWN is the one to point the refusal at"**
- Fig 8 :53: **"Residual first, frame second."**

Order:

1. Per-slot residual (D1 observation) before any frame is named.
2. Refusal of the flat-residual held term (WIMP) before a new mint (Fig 5 :59, Operators :58 *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*).
3. Discriminator (the missing link) before ranking the three moves (Fig 3 :27, Fig 6 :32 *"An instrument is not built from a description. It is improved from a worse instrument already returning something."*).
4. Import with source gate and shadow test only after 1 to 3 (Fig 6 :30, Fig 8 :50-52).

Effort spent on step 4 before step 3 arrives at a step that cannot use it: that is the Fig 3 break in this question.

### Q5 - What do the two currencies say?

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

A particle term can be cheap to state (one new atom, a cross-section) and extremely expensive to settle (tonne-years of exposure). A modified-law term can be expensive to state (a new interpolation function and a relativistic completion) and relatively cheap to test against existing rotation-curve data. Neither figure predicts the other; reading only one is choosing blind. This is why the null detections (actions spent) cannot be converted into a description verdict, and why a tight fit (bits saved) cannot be converted into a ground verdict.

## CONCLUSION

The three options are a union, not a partition (Fig 9 :54-57). Over the six-term set the rival models are first-order compositions; as usually written they share a functional group rather than being isomers, but over a full shared atom set the isomer claim becomes checkable with a stated falsifier (Fig 12 :117, :123). The chain breaks at the discriminator link: observations and vocabulary are strong, but the instrument that would separate missing source from changed law is the unbuilt link (Fig 3 :27). Refusal precedes mint (Fig 5 :59), and description cost and action cost must be read separately (Fig 12 :125-126). Nothing here is settled at the ground.
