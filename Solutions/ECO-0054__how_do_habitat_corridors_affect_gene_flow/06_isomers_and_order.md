# Order and structure in the corridor cascade

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question lists three effects, gene flow, species interactions and the spread of ecological disturbances. Two structural questions are owed: (1) what is the **order** among them, and (2) are they **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or merely members of one functional group? A third question follows: which bond closes the loop in each case, since the sign decides whether the effect is beneficial or harmful.

Atoms available in the ecology frame: patch A, patch B, the surrounding matrix, the corridor strip, a carrier (individual, pollen, seed, spore, pathogen, fire front), an allele or propagule, an interaction partner. ## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the order of the three effects?

- Fig 11 :52 supplies the cascade directly: *"first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."*
- Fig 12 :72: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* makes the order a sequence, not a set. The corridor must be in place and used before an atom crosses; an atom must cross before a gene frequency or an interaction rate can change; a changed interaction or a new carrier must exist before a contagion can travel.
- **First order: contact.** An individual, pollen grain or seed moves from A to B through the strip. Anchor: mark-recapture, telemetry, fluorescent marks.
- **Second order: import and interaction.** An allele enters the deme (Fig 6 :23); pollen transfer raises fruit set; seed dispersal changes recruitment. Christie and Knowles (2015) find the two mechanisms, *"the exchange of individuals between patches and the reduction in genetic drift"*, and Tewksbury et al. (2002) find the interaction effects.
- **Third order: disturbance.** A pathogen, invasive species or fire uses the same connection. The cycle can now close with a positive sign: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* (Fig 12 :121)
- **Strain note:** a reading that puts fire at first order only fits by calling the corridor a gradient. That is S1 in `01` §6; re-census keeps fire third order and uses Fig 13 only for the network-as-relation reading.

### Q2: Are the three effects isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Written over the **base shared atoms** `{patch A, patch B, corridor strip, carrier}`, all three effects have the same ingredient list. What differs is the bond and the loop sign:

| effect | bond chain | loop sign | substance |
|---|---|---|---|
| gene flow | A → corridor → carrier → B ⇒ allele frequency change | no re-entry at first pass | demographic and genetic unit |
| species interaction | A → corridor → pollinator/disperser → B ⇒ fruit or recruit | conditional re-entry next season | mutualism or predation |
| disturbance spread | A → corridor → pathogen/fire → B ⇒ new infection or burn → carrier regrows | **positive re-entry**, Fig 12 :121 | contagion |

- Under Fig 12's definition they are **isomers over the shared base**: same atoms, different bond arrangement, different substance, with the difference invisible in a flat "connectivity" formula and decisive in behaviour. This is the strongest structural claim worth testing.
- The weaker, safer reading is that they share a **functional group** (contact → import → consequence), with the qualifier (which carrier, which bond) deciding the member. As actually measured in the field, the carriers differ, so the weaker reading is always available and should be the default until a shared-atom experiment is run.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one of the three effects can be written without the corridor atom or without a carrier crossing a previously closed boundary (gene flow changing with no import, fire spreading with no fuel path, a mutualism changing with no moving partner), the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group reading stands. This is the same test as refuter 4 in `01` §4, applied at the structural level.

### Q3: Is a corridor a catalyst?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- A corridor survives use by a pollinator and by a pathogen alike. Nothing in its description records which partner regenerates and which is spent. That is exactly the figure's point: the same part can be a catalyst for gene flow and a spent or spent-and-regenerating part for a host population, and the notation cannot tell you which.
- **Falsifier:** a corridor that is consumed by first use (so that a second disperser cannot cross) would not be a catalyst; the catalyst reading would be refuted for that system. No such case is claimed here.

### Q4: The two currencies, and why one score fails

- Fig 12 :125-126: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."** Bits price the description; actions price finding out whether it holds.
- In this field: a connectivity model is cheap to state (a resistance surface, a few parameters) and expensive to settle (seasons of genetic sampling). Conversely, a mapped fire spread may be costly to describe in a model and cheap to refute with one incidence map. A scalar connectivity score tries to sum the two, which is the error Fig 12 :130 names: **"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."**
- **Falsifier:** a single index whose bits-cost and action-cost are shown to be commensurable for all four conditions would collapse this objection. None is supplied.

## CONCLUSION

The three effects are **ordered**, not simultaneous: contact (first), gene flow and interaction change (second), disturbance spread (third, where the loop can close with the opposite sign). Over a shared base of patch, corridor and carrier they are a real **isomer claim** with a stated falsifier; as actually measured, with different carriers, they are members of one **functional group** (contact → import → consequence). A corridor is a catalyst whose sign is not recorded in its description. The two currencies of description and action do not add, so one connectivity score cannot witness all four conditions.

This rescues what is structural in the question without the part that fails: the order and the isomer test need no universal corridor rule and no scalar score. Which member of the functional group dominates in a named landscape remains a ground question.
