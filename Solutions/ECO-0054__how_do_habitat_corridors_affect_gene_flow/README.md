## What an answer would look like (bind the shape as an ordinary term)

Before the run, per the Formula's "Direction" discipline: the sought-for answer would be (a) a characterized change in the habitat's contact structure, (b) a per-species valence attached to that change rather than to the corridor itself, and (c) a cascade from first-order movement to second-order gene flow and interaction effects to third-order disturbance spread, with an instrument that can tell a failure at one order from a failure at another. Until bound and predicted, that shape is a preference, invisible to the loop. It is bound here, so the refuter in `01` §4 can fire on it.

## Residual in effect terms

R_s = |Γ(b,a) − o′| per slot s. Here b is the expectation of connectivity encoded in a resistance or least-cost model, a is the act of building or retaining a corridor (an introduction into the habitat), Γ(b,a) is what that model predicts about movement, gene flow, interaction rates and spread, and o′ is what genetic, demographic and incidence data actually return. s is the species pair, the patch pair, the interaction and the disturbance event. R is per slot, never an industry-style average (Fig 1 :19). The question is not "are corridors good" but "why does the gap between predicted connection and observed connection stay small in some slots and grow in others, and why does it grow at a different rate for gene flow than for disease or fire." The anchor that settles it is genetic and demographic data, not agreement among corridor advocates (Fig 2 :29).
