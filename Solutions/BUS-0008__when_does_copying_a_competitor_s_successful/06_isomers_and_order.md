# Isomers, functional groups and the order of a copy

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

Two figures need a supporting derivation before the answer is complete. (1) When a firm copies the same visible ingredients as a competitor, has it produced the same substance, an isomer, or a different member of a functional group? (2) In what order must the conditions of a successful transplant be met, and where does the chain usually break?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the visible ingredients of a strategy (price, product, channel, hiring, cadence) plus the invisible bonds between them and the habitat conditions they were bonded to. Bonds held: the seven, especially `+` conjunction, `→` sequence, `≡` identity, and `⋛` comparison. No new primitive is available without import. Two conditions are in play, not one: the method must cross (Fig 4) and the habitat must be reproduced or remade (Fig 11). Their intersection is a compound with its own loop (Fig 9 :55).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Same ingredients, same substance?

- Fig 12 :23: **"this bond is symmetric, so the four are one term"** (under `+`, ingredient permutations are spellings, not candidates).
- Fig 12 :92: **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**
- Fig 12 :117: **"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

So "same ingredient list" is not a copy of the competitor's substance. Under a symmetric bond the permutations are one term, so rearranging the list changes nothing; under an ordered bond the same ingredients in a different sequence are a different term. Without knowing which bond held the competitor's success, the copier cannot tell whether it has reproduced the substance or made an isomer. This is the structural form of F1.

### Q2 — Isomer or functional group?

- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

The competitor's method is the functional group: a substructure whose presence fixes whether the transplant pays. The visible artifact is the qualifier: it says which member of the kind, not whether the kind is present. A firm that copies the qualifier has copied the member without the group. A firm that acquires the group can instantiate a different member and still get the outcome. The catalyst reading matters too: a routine that survives its own use behaves differently across uses from a campaign that is spent; the artifact records neither, so repeated copying of a spent campaign pays once and then stops.

**Falsifier, written before the conclusion:** if a documented transplant reproduces the competitor's market return across repeated occasions with the functional group absent and only the qualifier present, the isomer/functional-group reading fails and the artifact is the generator.

### Q3 — Convergent derivation or adopted import?

- Fig 8 :50-52: **"Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them"**
- Fig 13 :121: **"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."**
- Fig 6 :23: **"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."**

Two firms can reach the same visible practice independently, in which case their agreement is corroboration at maximum independence (Fig 8 :50), or one can adopt it from the other, in which case the surplus is only where their closures differ. The artifact cannot tell you which. Only the record can. This is why the outreach packet asks for provenance before treating a similarity as a copy.

### Q4 — What order, and where does the chain break?

- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**
- Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**
- Fig 12 :112: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."**

Dependency order for a transplant: perception (see the practice) → vocabulary (name ingredients *and* bonds) → objective (say what progress in the receiving habitat would be) → planning (route the import) → learn and carry (verify and carry a method). Most copying efforts land at the first two links, because those are the visible ones. The break is between vocabulary and objective: a firm can name the competitor's ingredients and still have no term for what would count as progress in its own habitat, because its vocabulary holds ingredients and not bonds. Fig 3 :25-26 says this link "has neither" obvious failures nor obvious tests, "so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments." A read taken below that break is a read of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23).

**Falsifier:** if a firm with an ingredient-only vocabulary and no stated progress measure nevertheless reproduces the return repeatedly in a different habitat, the order claim fails and the break is elsewhere.

### Q5 — Why one lever cannot clear the compound

- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
- Fig 9 :57: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

Method crossing and habitat reproduction overlap. Applying only the first lever leaves the intersection standing: a firm that acquires the method into a substituted habitat still fails, and a firm that rebuilds the habitat around an artifact still has no generator. The residue after one lever is not a harder case of the first condition; it is a specific compound with its own loop. This is why a single "copy their process" prescription is structurally insufficient.

## CONCLUSION

The visible ingredient list is not the substance. Under a symmetric bond its permutations are one term; under an ordered bond the same list is a different term, an isomer whose behaviour differs because the bond is not recorded. What a copier needs is the functional group, the part whose presence fixes the outcome, not the qualifier that names one member. Whether a similarity is convergent derivation or adopted import is decided by the record and not by the artifact. The transplant order runs perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry, and the chain usually breaks where a firm can name ingredients but has no term for bonds or progress. Both conditions, method and habitat, must be cleared; one lever leaves a categorically different residue.
