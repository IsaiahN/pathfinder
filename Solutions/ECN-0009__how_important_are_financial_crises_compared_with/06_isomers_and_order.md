## RESIDUAL

The primary audit leaves three structural questions open. (1) If the two classes are a union, what is the order of the union and what is the bond at its intersection? (2) Are a crisis regime and an ordinary-fluctuation regime isomers over a shared atom set, or merely members of a functional group? (3) What order does the per-slot rule impose on the comparison, given that a numerical rank is a gap?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — What is the bond at the intersection of the two classes?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- The volatility paradox source gives the world-side instance: low measured volatility raises leverage and yield-seeking, which builds fragility, which raises the chance of a crisis. The output of the ordinary regime (low volatility) re-enters as input to the crisis regime (fragility). That is a cycle, not a chain.
- Fig 13 :31-32: *"a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos"* governs the amplification. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes. A negative feedback loop that damps a demand shock is a different object from a positive feedback loop that amplifies a credit shock.
- **Verdict:** the intersection compound is a signed cycle. Mark `triangulated` across Fig 12 :121, Fig 13 :31-32 and the OFR source (three low-overlap routes). Mode `specified`, unmeasured.

### Q2 — Are the two regimes isomers or members of a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Written narrowly, as the economics sources write them, the ordinary regime and the crisis regime share atoms (output, time, credit to a degree) but differ in which bond dominates (damped demand loop versus amplifying credit cycle). Over a shared atom set they satisfy the figure's definition: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour. That is the **isomer** claim.
- Written as the world literature sometimes writes them, as "a recession with a financial crisis" versus "a recession without one", the two share only the recession substructure, so they are two members of a **functional group** (a contraction with different qualifiers) rather than isomers over a full shared set.
- **Which reading is licensed?** Fig 12 :94 says *"The arrangement is the substance."* The isomer claim is the stronger and the more informative one, and it is checkable: it requires both regimes to be written over the full shared atom set. If one regime requires an atom the other does not, it fails.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either regime cannot be written without an atom the other requires, for example a crisis with no credit or leverage term, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. This is the same falsifier shape used in the exemplar's `06`, applied to this domain.

### Q3 — What order does the per-slot rule impose?

- Fig 1 :19: **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."** Fig 1 :22: **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."**
- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- The order is therefore: (a) name the slots, here at minimum ordinary contraction, crisis contraction and the overlap compound; (b) obtain a per-slot residual for each; (c) declare the currency in which the residuals will be compared; (d) only then apply ⋛; (e) report the numeric weight as a gap until a ground pays.
- Reversing this order, computing a national importance score and then looking for the slot that explains it, is a reading below the break in Fig 3's sense. It cannot be diagnosed from the score; only its predecessor can.
- **Verdict:** `triangulated` across Fig 1, Fig 3 and the per-slot rule as used in *How a Society of Agents Would Work*. Mode `general`.

### Q4 — What does ⋛ actually compare once the order is respected?

- Operators table :48-49 defines ⋛ as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, with test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."*
- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Once a currency is declared, ⋛ can compare the two per-slot residuals as quantities. Without a declared currency, applying ⋛ to "crisis importance" and "ordinary importance" is a type error: the operands are not yet quantities in one unit. This is the live strain recorded in `01` F3.
- The Operators table :51 also says *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*. So declaring "crises matter more" before a currency exists states a goal, not a finding. The honest form records the goal as a preference, binds it as an ordinary term, and lets it accrue a residual, exactly as THE_FORMULA "Direction" prescribes.

## CONCLUSION

The union of ordinary fluctuation and crisis has a signed cycle at its intersection, not a mere additive tail. Over a shared atom set the two regimes are a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier; over the narrower set that shares only "contraction" they are a functional group. The per-slot rule imposes an order: slots first, per-slot residuals second, currency third, comparison fourth, numeric weight last and owed to the ground. ⋛ is licensed only after the currency exists; before that it is a type error and the claim is a goal. Nothing here requires a new primitive, an import that moves the wall, or a name for the seventh term.
