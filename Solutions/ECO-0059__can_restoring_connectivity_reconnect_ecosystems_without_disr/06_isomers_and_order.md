# Isomers and order in connectivity restoration

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §8).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks whether restoring connectivity can reconnect ecosystems without disrupting established local communities. Two supporting derivations are owed. (1) Are a corridor and a barrier that occupy the same land with the same species **isomers** under Fig 12, or merely instances of one functional group? (2) What is the **order** of the cascade, and which link in the dependency chain actually governs a restoration decision?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Are a corridor and a fence isomers?

Fig 12 :117 *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Both a corridor and a fence can be written over the same atom set: two patches, a linear element, several species, a matrix. The ingredient list is identical. What differs is the bond: the corridor composes the patches for movement; the fence composes them against movement. Under Fig 12 the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance, so they are **isomers**, not variants of one thing. A thesis that lists only "linear landscape element, two patches, species present" has recorded half of itself.

Fig 12 :95 *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."* A management plan that records corridor length, width and cost but not which residual the link is bonded to has exactly this notation failure.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a corridor and a fence over the identical atom set produced identical values for every residual at every site, the isomer claim fails and the two are one functional group with a label difference.

### Q2 : Do they share a functional group?

Fig 12 :123 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

Corridors, fences, roads and dams all share the substructure "linear element that changes movement across a landscape mosaic". That fixes the *kind*: a movement-modifying structure. The qualifier "connects" or "blocks" says which member. This is the weaker, safer claim, and it is the one a landscape-level inventory can actually support. The isomer claim needs the bond to be recorded, not just the species list.

### Q3 : What is the order of the cascade?

Fig 11 :52 *"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."*

Order the effects, not by importance but by dependency:

1. **First order, movement.** Target individuals cross the link. This is the quantity the corridor meta-analysis measures (Gilbert-Norton et al. 2010, about a 50 percent increase).
2. **Second order, relations.** Pollination, seed dispersal, predation and competition change (Fig 11 :47 *"relations: pollination, predation, competition, dependency"*). Tewksbury et al. 2002 found pollination and seed dispersal facilitated beyond the area the corridor added.
3. **Third order, the decisive residual.** Pathogen establishment, invasive plant spread, or a prey population decline several steps out. The Leeds 2025 Elwha example is a third-order effect: dam removal revived fish and increased invasive plants through the same new link.
4. **Fourth order and later, the management channel.** Monitoring, reversal and re-design. This is the seat's work, not the corridor's (Fig 11 :57).

Fig 13 :85 *"and on"* and :86 *"Back to new relations and fields, which is what restarts it."* The chain does not terminate at a solved state. A decision that stops at first order is a reading of nothing at the level where the question is actually decided.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if across restoration cases the decisive non-disruption residual is always first-order and no second- or third-order effect ever changes a verdict, the cascade-order claim is refuted.

### Q4 : Which dependency link governs?

Fig 3 :20 *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*

Perception (enumerate the habitat) → vocabulary (what does the link afford, for which residual) → objective (what counts as reconnection, what counts as disruption) → planning (which link, where) → learn and carry (monitor, reverse, record). Fig 3 :26 *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."*

For ECO-0059 the likely break is between vocabulary and objective: teams can enumerate species and build corridors without ever stating what would count as "not disrupted". Fig 3 :19 *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* More sophisticated corridor design cannot rescue a missing objective.

### Q5 : Sequence, production, comparison, refusal

Fig 12 :72 *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* defines `→`. Enumeration precedes introduction, and introduction precedes reading the ground; that is a real sequence, and swapping the operands produces a different thing (a design before a census is not the same ceremony).

Fig 12 :77 *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* defines `⇒`. A new movement path is production: before the link fires, that path did not exist.

Fig 12 :87 *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* defines `⋛`. The goal "without disruption" is a comparison against a threshold, not a substance and not a state. Fig 12 :112 *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* is the direct figure warrant for binding the goal as a term with a threshold. The Operators table :52 adds *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"*.

Fig 12 :112 *"A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."* When monitoring shows the resident residual growing, what is owed is a refusal of the design's standing to be composed over, not a new explanation. Nothing is deleted from the record; the reach falls because something was added (Fig 6 :42).

### Q6 : Two analogues, and where they stop

- **Catalyst.** Fig 12 :119 *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* A corridor is a catalyst for crossing: a thousand animals use it and it remains. A beaver dam is the natural instance named in the architecture essay's citation list for Jones, Lawton and Shachak 1994. The analogue stops at the seat: a beaver does not choose for a target residual, a manager does (Fig 11 :57).
- **Cycle.** Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Monitoring output must re-enter as design input if management is permanent work. Without the return path it is a chain, not a maintained loop, and Fig 11 :60 forbids calling it solved.

## CONCLUSION

A corridor and a fence over the same land and species are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The weaker and more attainable claim is that they share the functional group "movement-modifying linear element" (Fig 12 :124). The cascade has a dependency order: first-order movement, second-order relations, third-order decisive residual, fourth-order management feedback. A decision that stops at first order is not a weak reading of the question, it is a reading of the wrong link. The goal "without disruption" is a comparison, and a failure of that comparison is a refusal, not an explanation.
