# Isomers and order: the structure of the governance transition

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit shows that corruption as governance is a union of five conditions and that the pivot is a convention crossing into deciding outcomes. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) is that crossing a new atom or a change of bond and role, and (2) in what order do the conditions become dependable? A third question follows: are an honest office and a captured office built from the same atoms arranged differently, that is, are they isomers under Fig 12?

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Is the convention-to-verdict crossing a new atom?

No. Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."** Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**

Before the crossing: `rule + convention`, with the rule as the reference for outcomes and the convention as a side arrangement. After the crossing: `convention ⇒ outcome`, with the rule still present but no longer deciding. The atoms are identical. What changed is which operand is bound to the outcome and with which bond. Seat Map :105 states the same transition from the checking side: an authored convention that decides an outcome *"has stopped being a convention and started being a verdict, and nothing flags the transition."*

This resolves a potential strain. The crossing does not import a new term into the institution; it re-bonds terms already held. That is why it is invisible from inside: a frame cannot recover the bond from the operands (Fig 12 :46), and the record of the prior arrangement is what would make it checkable.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit a convention that begins deciding outcomes while a genuinely new institutional atom appears with it, and show that removing the new atom restores the rule as reference. If that holds, Q1 is false and an import account is owed instead.

### Q2. Are the honest and the captured office isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Write office A (rule-bound) and office B (convention-bound) over the same atom set:

```
A: rule ⇒ outcome, monitor ⋛ rule, benefit → rule-compliant applicant
B: convention ⇒ outcome, monitor ⋛ convention, benefit → broker
```

Same atoms, different bonds, different substance. They are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117. The difference is invisible in an ingredient list (both offices have a rule, a monitor, a benefit, a convention) and decisive in behaviour. This is why an anti-corruption questionnaire that counts the presence of rules, audits, and codes of conduct cannot tell the two apart: it records ingredients, not arrangement.

A weaker claim also holds. If the monitors in both offices are drawn from the same professional pool and use the same evidence, they are a **functional group** (Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*): the group says "oversight", the qualifier says which office it actually serves.

World corroboration at low overlap: Helmke and Levitsky's typology (as read in Barbalet 2022/2023) distinguishes complementary, accommodating, competing and substitutive informal institutions. That is a four-way isomer table over the same formal and informal atoms. It corroborates the structure; it does not decide the figure verdict.

**Falsifier:** exhibit an office where the same atoms under the same bonds produce both rule-bound and convention-bound outcomes in the same period, with no difference in monitor reference. That breaks the isomer claim.

### Q3. Which structures recur in the stable form?

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

- **Catalyst:** the broker who arranges the favour and is not consumed by it. A categorical anti-corruption drive treats every actor as spent on first use; the figure says the durable actor is the one that regenerates, and nothing in the ordinary record marks which a term is.
- **Cycle:** benefit granted re-enters as obligation owed, which produces the next benefit. A chain cannot close by construction, so a reform that only lengthens the chain of approvals cannot close the loop; it may extend it. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes. This is Axelrod's reciprocity shape, corroborated by the Springer entry's question about cooperation *"in a world of egoists without central authority"* (low-overlap field, secondary only).
- **Comparison:** Fig 12 on goals, *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"* is stated in the Operators table :52. The objective link in Fig 3 is a comparison: what would count as progress for a citizen versus for a broker. A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot state that comparison (Fig 12 :111).

**Falsifier:** show a stable corrupt governance system in which the broker is consumed on first use and the benefit never re-enters as obligation, with no other catalyst or cycle present. That breaks Q3.

### Q4. What is the dependency order of the five conditions?

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**

1. **Perception.** Can the office tell a deviation from compliance? If no, there is no `R` to route (Fig 3 :4-5).
2. **Vocabulary.** Does it have terms for what an office affords: discretion, favour, obligation, outcome? Fig 3 :7: *"it has terms for what things afford: what can be moved, opened, joined, blocked"*.
3. **Objective.** Can it say what would count as progress in its own terms? Fig 3 :10: *"it can say what would count as progress here, in its own terms"*. This is the middle link that breaks (Fig 3 :24-27).
4. **Planning.** Is there a route to the objective under incomplete information?
5. **Learn and carry.** Does the successful deviation get named, verified, and carried to the next office? Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."*

The governance transition depends on steps 3 and 5. The anchor capture (F3) and channel failure (F4) are not steps in this chain; they are failures of the ground-maintainer seat that corrupt every step from above. Treating them as additional links would be a category error.

**Falsifier:** exhibit an office where step 3 is demonstrably working (a ground-checked objective instrument is read and used) and the method still crosses the membrane and the convention still decides, with no seat failure above. That breaks the dependency order.

## CONCLUSION

The convention-to-verdict crossing is a re-bonding of held atoms, not a new atom. A rule-bound office and a convention-bound office are isomers over the same atom set, which is why ingredient-counting accountability cannot tell them apart. The recurring structures are the catalyst (the broker that survives use) and the cycle (benefit re-entering as obligation). The five conditions order by dependency, with the vocabulary-to-objective link as the likely break and the seat failures acting from above rather than as links.

This rescues what is checkable in the shape without claiming a new primitive or a named culprit. What remains open is which bond actually holds in a given office. That needs the instrument named in F9 of the main audit, and is declared `gap`.
