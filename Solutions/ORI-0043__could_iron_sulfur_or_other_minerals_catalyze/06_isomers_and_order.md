## RESIDUAL

The main audit found that the figure-governed shape is a conjunction of three properties (persists through use, closes a cycle, couples to a spendable difference). Two supporting questions remain. (1) Under Fig 12, what kind of structure is an iron-sulfur cluster in a mineral versus an iron-sulfur cluster in an enzyme: an isomer, a functional group, or something else? (2) In what order must the four tests be run, and what is lost when the figure set is condensed into a one-line outreach claim?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the figures: space, time, energy (states) and spectrum, gradient, network (structures). Bonds held: the seven plus negation. No new primitive is available from the figures. ## Q1 - Are FeS and NiS isomers under Fig 12?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

FeS and NiS do not have the same atoms; iron and nickel are different. So they are **not isomers** under the figure's own definition. This is a retraction of my first reading, which tried to make the figure govern the mineral pair. Kept here as a visible correction.

The figure object that does fit is Fig 12 :123:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

The Fe-S core is a candidate functional group: a substructure whose presence may fix the catalytic outcome, while the qualifier (mineral lattice versus protein scaffold) says which member. Camprubi et al. (2017) report that "Iron-sulphur proteins are ancient and drive fundamental processes in cells, notably electron transfer and CO2 fixation" and that "Iron-sulphur minerals with equivalent structures could have played a key role in the origin of life." Tran (2024) reports that mineral iron sulfides look structurally like the clusters in carbon-fixation proteins. That is a functional-group claim in the figure's sense, not an isomer claim.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if catalysis tracks the whole mineral lattice and not the Fe-S core, so that changing the lattice destroys activity while the core is unchanged, the functional-group reading fails and the mineral must be treated as a whole-molecule catalyst instead.

## Q2 - Are two minerals with the same composition but different lattice different substances?

Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond." Fig 12 :41 "same two atoms, three substances".

This is the real isomer question for mineralogy: same composition, different structure. It would apply to polymorphs, not to FeS versus NiS. The figure licenses the distinction in principle. Whether any named iron-sulfide polymorph matters for prebiotic catalysis is an empirical question for the ground, not a figure verdict. Mark `specified` and unmeasured.

## Q3 - Dependency order of the four tests

Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

1. **Survival of use** (Fig 12 :119). Without it, "catalysis" is misnamed. This is the first link.
2. **Cycle closure** (Fig 12 :121). A chain cannot close by construction, so a linear sequence is not a metabolism.
3. **Gradient coupling** (Fig 13 :22). The cycle needs a spendable difference; an inventory of minerals is not an energy source.
4. **Persistence across conditions** (Fig 11 :43, habitat enumeration). The habitat is enumerated, never composed; list what acts, what conditions hold and what relations carry, then read what the ground kept.

A reading of link 4 before link 1 is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23). This is why the outreach email asks for the four refuters in the same order.

## Q5 - Round trip gap when the figures are condensed

Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."

1. **Sent up (x):** the verbatim figure set, chiefly Fig 12 :119, :121, :123 and Fig 13 :22, :46.
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** the outreach shorthand "minerals catalyze early metabolism".
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** the concrete claim "FeS or NiS catalyzed a specific prebiotic carbon-fixation step in a gradient".

**The gap is nonzero.** Three constraints held in the figure set do not survive the trip back down through the shorthand:

- the survival-of-use test, which the shorthand converts into a mere rate increase;
- the cycle requirement, which the shorthand reduces to a single reaction;
- the spendable-difference requirement, which the shorthand drops entirely.

One thing survives cleanly: a catalyst is a part that survives its own use. That is the residue worth sending. The gap is recorded qualitatively as the enumerated set of dropped constraints; no numerical R_T is claimed because no ground reading of the condensation was taken here. Treating "the formula is the condensed version of the figures" as identity would set the gap to zero and would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

## CONCLUSION

FeS and NiS are not isomers under Fig 12 :117; that first reading is retracted. The figure object that fits is the functional group (Fig 12 :123), with the Fe-S core as a candidate substructure and its falsifier stated. The four tests are ordered by dependency, and the condensation of the figures into a one-line claim carries a nonzero round trip gap that lists what was dropped.
