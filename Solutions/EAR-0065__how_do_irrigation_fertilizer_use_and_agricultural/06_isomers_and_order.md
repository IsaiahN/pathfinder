# Union, bonds and order in the agricultural residual

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the bond definitions. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).**

## RESIDUAL

The question bundles three actors (irrigation water, fertilizer nutrients, runoff transport) and two receivers (regional water, regional climate). Two questions are owed: (1) are the three actors **isomers** under Fig 12, independent inputs under a partition, or overlapping conditions under a union; and (2) what is the dependency order for searching them?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the earth-system frame: water quantity, reactive nitrogen, reactive phosphorus, energy flux, biomass, dissolved oxygen, transport path. Bonds held: the seven from Fig 12 plus negation, usable here as typed relations between quantities, not as chemical bonds. No new atom is available without an import with a source gate.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Partition or union? CORRECTION TO MY FIRST CENSUS IN `01`

- Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."* Fig 9 :58: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- A partition would treat irrigated land, fertilized land and runoff-generating land as disjoint classes. They are not: the same field is all three. A single lever (cut nitrogen) clears the region where only nitrogen binds and leaves the intersection standing because water and transport still bind.
- ⇒ The relation is a **union compound**. Fig 9 :57: *"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- **I overstated the partition reading in the first pass.** Retracted as applied to the question level. It survives only at the instrument level, where gauges, towers and flux chambers are genuinely separate closure frames. Triangulated (Fig 9 :54-58 plus Fig 9 :4-5).

### Q2: Are the three actors isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :46: *"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."*
- As the question writes them, the three actors do **not** share one atom set. Irrigation is water quantity plus energy flux; fertilizer is reactive nutrient mass; runoff is a transport relation. By Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**, because isomers require the same atoms under a different bond.
- The stronger, testable version: if all three are written over the shared atom set `{water quantity, reactive N and P, energy flux, biomass, dissolved oxygen, transport path}`, differing only in which bond connects which atoms, then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it makes a single coupled model legitimate without making the three mechanisms identical.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one actor requires an atom the other two never use (for example if irrigation cooling cannot be written with any nutrient atom, or runoff transport cannot be written with an energy-flux atom that the other two share), the isomer claim fails and the weaker **functional-group** reading stands. Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* All three are then members of the group *"agricultural intervention on a field"*, with different atoms in the slots.

### Q3: Which bonds hold?

- Fig 12 :72: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* is sequence (→). It holds between fertilizer application and the appearance of a leachable nitrate pool; the second does not exist as a field condition before the first fires.
- Operators table :37: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* is production (⇒). It holds for the algal bloom produced by a nutrient load: there is no bloom before the load arrives, in the cultural-eutrophication case.
- Fig 13 :76: *"Energy and a gradient compose a flow. A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."* The water gradient over space (runoff to the basin) and the moisture gradient over time (irrigation ET through the day) are different terms even when the same field produces both. This is the structural reason the two climate slots must not be averaged.
- Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Nutrient cycling in the basin (sediment release, denitrification, internal loading) is a cycle, not a chain. A management chain that assumes one pass will miss it.
- Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* Soil microbial communities and aquifer storage are catalyst-like parts: they persist and change what later applications do. A model that treats every season as a fresh occasion prices them wrong.

### Q4: What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- Order: field source term before basin load; basin load before coastal effect; first-order molecules before the coupled region; describe the residual before picking the frame (Fig 9 :27-28: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"*).
- Cross-scale handoffs are membranes (Fig 4 :21: *"The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a filter with a direction."*). What crosses upward is a method of accounting for losses, not the field's recorded success in one season. Fig 4 :30: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap."*

### Q5: A second falsifier, on order

If a regional effect can be measured and attributed without first measuring the field source term, and the attribution survives an independent instrument, the dependency order above is wrong for that case: the link has been reached from below the supposed break. That would not make the field measurement useless, but it would refute the claim that the order is mandatory.

## CONCLUSION

The three actors are not isomers as the question writes them; they are members of one functional group. They become a real isomer claim only under the full shared atom set, and that stronger claim has a stated falsifier. Their internal relations are distinct bonds (sequence into the soil pool, production into the bloom, spatial and temporal gradients into two different flows, cycles and catalyst-like stores in the basin). The dependency order is field, then basin, then atmosphere, then the coupled region, with the cross-scale gaps measured rather than assumed.

This rescues the coupled modelling ambition without the part that fails: no atom has to be invented, no standing list of "good" versus "bad" practices is needed, and the two opposite climate slots keep their separate currencies.
