# Isomers and order: centralized versus decentralized forms

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, with one live strain named below. Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading** (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

An org chart names boxes, not bonds. Two institutional forms can list the same actors and instruments and behave differently because a different relation holds between them. Three questions are owed:

1. Are a centralized and a decentralized form, over the same actors, **isomers** under Fig 12's definition?
2. What is the **order** of their composition, and which direction does a method cross?
3. Does the isomorphism claim survive when the reform adds or removes an actor?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the institutional frame: decision rights over a slot, a local ground reading, a shared convention or standard, an instrument that measures outcome, a checking seat with refusal power, a channel carrying reports. Bonds available: the seven typed relations plus negation; the relevant ones here are → sequence, ⋛ comparison, + conjunction, and ¬ refusal (Operators table).

No new atom is available without import. A reform that creates a new audit office or ombudsman adds an atom and is therefore not an isomer case. That condition is the live strain.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Same atoms, different bond: are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Take a fixed atom set:

```
A = {decision right over slot s, local reading L_s, standard K,
     instrument M, checking seat C, channel h}
```

Form X (centralized arrangement): `L_s --h--> C --> decision`, with `C ⋛ M` deciding conformance to K, and the refusal held at C.
Form Y (decentralized arrangement): `L_s --> decision local`, with `M --h--> C` and `C ⋛ K` as a later audit, and the refusal held at C but on a different operand.

Both hold A. The bond arrangement differs: in X the chain from local reading to decision passes through h before the decision; in Y it does not, and h carries the measurement after the decision. By Fig 12 :117 these are isomers. The ingredient list is identical; the behaviour differs. A chart that lists only the boxes has "recorded half of itself".

**Result:** the isomer claim is supported under the fixed-atom condition. Triangulated (Fig 12 :117 plus Fig 12 :5 plus Fig 4 :19-20, "Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change", which makes the arrangement the remaining variable).

### Q2 — What order does the composition take?

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."
Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."

Order is decided by dependency, not importance (Fig 3 :20). The legal order for a decision class is:

1. the local residual at slot s is read (perception);
2. a term exists for what the situation affords (vocabulary);
3. the objective at that slot is stated in local terms (what would count as progress);
4. a method crosses the scale membrane upward, or a head start downward (Fig 4 :22);
5. the ground settles the composed action, and a seat can refuse it.

Putting step 4 before step 2 is the common error: a central standard is imposed before the local frame has a term for what the standard is for. That is the dependency break Fig 3 names. Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

**Result:** the direction of crossing is a bond property, free to flip between the two forms. Order is not a degree of centralization; it is the sequence in which the loop closes. `specified`, triangulated (Fig 3 plus Fig 4).

### Q3 — Does the claim survive an atom change?

Fig 13 :115: "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."
Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."

If a reform adds a new checking seat or a new measurement instrument, the atom set changes. The relation between before and after is then composition plus import, not isomerism. Fig 6 :23 requires contact with a differing closure, and Fig 8 requires a source gate and a shadow test for the import. This is not a defect in the claim; it is a scope condition.

**Live strain:** real centralization and decentralization reforms frequently do add or remove actors. The fixed-atom isomer claim therefore covers only the subset of reforms that rearrange existing rights and instruments without changing the cast. I keep this open rather than treating it as a solved case. It is recorded in `01` §5, `09`, and `11_audit.md`.

**Result:** the isomer claim is `specified` and scope-limited; the scope limit is a named strain, not a correction.

### Q4 — The two currencies do not add

Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."

Coordination has a description cost (how hard the common rule is to state) and an action cost (how hard it is to check whether it holds). A tall unified standard may be cheap to describe and expensive to verify locally; a short local rule may be cheap to state and take a whole local budget to settle. Neither figure predicts the other. A comparison that sums them into one "efficiency" score is the error Fig 12 :125 names, not the fix.

**Result:** the alleged trade-off "coordination versus local knowledge" is not a single exchange rate. Different currencies, held apart. `general` structural, `specified` per case.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed set of actors and instruments, centralized and decentralized forms are isomers (Fig 12 :117): same atoms, different bonds, different substance. The variable is the **bond**, specifically where the reading enters the chain and which operand the refusal is aimed at, not the degree of centralization. Order follows dependency (Fig 3 :20), and a method crosses the scale membrane while a recording does not (Fig 4 :22). When a reform adds or removes an atom the relation becomes composition plus import, which is a live scope condition, not a refutation. Two currencies, description cost and action cost, do not add (Fig 12 :125).

This framing rescues the defensible part of the intuitive answer without the part that fails: there is no universal optimal degree, but there is a checkable structural difference between arrangements.

## FALSIFIER (written before the run)

1. **Bond falsifier.** Show a centralized and a decentralized form with the same atoms and the same bond arrangement but different behaviour. That kills the claim that the bond carries the difference (Fig 12 :117).
2. **Order falsifier.** Show a case where a shared method crosses the membrane before the local frame has vocabulary for what the situation affords and the decision still settles against the local ground. That narrows the dependency-order claim (Fig 3 :20).
3. **Import falsifier.** Show a reform that adds a new actor and still behaves as a pure rearrangement, with no source gate or shadow test. That dissolves the live strain by showing atom changes do not matter, which would strengthen rather than break the isomer claim; it is recorded either way.
4. **Currency falsifier.** Show a case where description cost and action cost can be added into one score and still predict which form pays. That breaks Fig 12 :125 as applied here.
