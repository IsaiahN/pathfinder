# Intervention as composition: order, union, and the catalyst analogue

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

An intervention is usually described as a scalar setting: a price, a penalty, a quota, a nudge strength. The question asks which ones work when agents adapt and the network rewires. Two questions are owed: (1) can an intervention be written as a bonded composition rather than a parameter, and (2) in what order must its parts be applied?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the six substrate terms (space, time, energy; spectrum, gradient, network) plus, from the design frame, the designer's belief b, action a, observation o-prime, residual R, and target quantity. Bonds held: the seven, including sequence (→), production (⇒), subtraction (−) and comparison (⋛), plus negation (¬). No new primitive is available without import. ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is an intervention a scalar or a molecule?

Fig 12 :111-112:

> **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**
> **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."**

Operators table :46 and :51:

> **"progress is a subtraction, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot say how far along it is"**
> **"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"**

So an intervention has at least three bonded parts: a sequence of actions (→), a comparison of the observed quantity against a threshold (⋛), and the subtraction of residual between passes (−). A scalar setting names a state of the system, not a route to one. This is the figure-grounded reason the answer-shape in `01` is a composition and not a list of levers.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single scalar setting keeps R trending down across both a change of residual and a change of topology, with no comparison or subtraction step anywhere in the mechanism, this finding fails.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 :19-20:

> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**

Fig 3 :24-27:

> **"Why the middle link is where chains usually break."**
> **"Perception has obvious failures and obvious tests. Planning has obvious failures and obvious tests. The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."**
> **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

The order is therefore: perceive R per slot, acquire terms for what the situation affords, state what counts as progress, plan the intervention, learn and carry. Most intervention effort lands above the break (a better optimizer, a bigger incentive) because those are the links with instruments. The middle link (what counts as progress) has none, so it is skipped. This matches the world literature: Meadows' leverage-point list puts parameters last and goals second, and adaptive management insists on an explicit objective and monitoring before acting.

**Falsifier:** an intervention programme that states its success criterion only after the intervention is deployed, and still reports a non-drifting per-slot residual against a ground that cannot be moved, breaks this order claim.

### Q3 - Union or partition?

Fig 9 :54-57:

> **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
> **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**
> **"A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."**
> **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."**

Strategic adaptation is condition C1; endogenous rewiring is condition C2. An intervention that changes incentives addresses C1; one that changes connection rules addresses C2. Their intersection is the case where agents both learn the incentive and rewire around it. That case is not "more of C1"; it is a compound with its own loop. This is why the world sources use combination strategies: Weng and Lee 2025 find that constraint level changes the cooperation regime, and Cremers and Nguyen 2019 review combination treatments against adaptive network resistance in breast cancer. Both are corroboration only; union status comes from Fig 9.

**Falsifier:** one lever that clears the intersection with no categorically different residue breaks the union reading, and the compound collapses back to a single loop.

### Q4 - The catalyst and the cycle: why one-shot fails

Fig 12 :119-121:

> **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
> **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

A network that rewires around an intervention is a cycle whose output re-enters as input. The sign of that loop decides whether it reinforces the intervention or defeats it, and a one-shot chain cannot close it. A maintained intervention is a catalyst: it survives its own use, or it is regenerated. An intervention that is spent after one use and not regenerated behaves differently across uses, and a report that does not record which kind it is has lost the distinction. Fig 11 :63 makes the same point at the habitat level: *"Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."*

**Falsifier:** a spent, non-regenerated intervention that keeps R down across multiple uses and across a habitat substitution breaks the catalyst/cycle reading.

### Q5 - Are adaptation and rewiring isomers?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 12 :124:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

As usually reported, the two interventions (an incentive change and a topology change) share no atom set, so they are not isomers; they are two members of the same functional group, "a bonded intervention aimed at a residual". The stronger form: if both are written over the full shared atom set held by the design frame, differing only in which bond mediates which slot, they are isomers, same atoms and different arrangement. That is the testable structural claim. The world sources cannot settle it; each uses its own atom set.

**Falsifier:** if either intervention requires an atom the other does not, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group claim stands.

### Q6 - What would settle the order empirically?

Not an argument, an instrument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: *"The answer is unreached until a better instrument exists."* Here the discriminators are:

1. Does the proposed target move when the agents push on it? If yes, it is not an anchor (Fig 2 :29-30).
2. Does the intervention change the rate of success per slot, or only the aggregate? If only the aggregate, the live slot is hidden (Fig 1 :22).
3. Does the connection structure change after the intervention, and does that change re-enter as input to the same residual? If yes, the cycle is closed and must be treated as such (Fig 12 :121).
4. Is the intervention spent or regenerated across uses? If spent, it cannot be treated as a catalyst (Fig 12 :119).

## CONCLUSION

An intervention is a molecule, not a scalar: sequence, comparison and subtraction are all needed to write a route to a residual. The dependency order runs through the middle link (what counts as progress), which is exactly the link with no instrument. Strategic adaptation and endogenous rewiring form a union, so one lever leaves the intersection standing. The maintained intervention is a catalyst facing a cycle, and the sign of that cycle decides whether it closes in favour of the intervention or against it. On the isomer question the ordinary reports fail the figure's definition; the stronger form over a shared atom set is a real claim with a stated falsifier.

This framing keeps what is defensible without naming a winning strategy. No import that moves the wall is claimed, no seventh primitive is invented, and the `gap` in `01` F7 stays open.
