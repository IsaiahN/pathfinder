# The two causes as different bonds: structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question offers two causes of a slow price: an obstacle in the adjustment channel (repricing cost, contract), and a strategic choice (waiting on rivals). Are these two names for one mechanism, opposite ends of one dial, or different structures? Two questions are owed: (1) what bond does each cause use, and (2) does the order in which they act matter for what a slot returns?

Atoms held in Γ: a price quote, a marginal cost, a contract schedule, a rival's price, an information set, an expected gain. Bonds held: the seven, including ⋛ comparison, → sequence and the cycle structure (Fig 12 :121). No new primitive is available without import. ## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are "costs" and "strategy" isomers, a functional group, or different bonds?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- The obstacle case composes as `cost + contract → ⋛ → no repricing`. The gain and the adjustment cost are compared; if the gain does not exceed the cost, the price stays. The Wikipedia *Menu cost* article, opened 2026-11-23, gives the firm's own inequality: "If Z < B − A, then the menu cost is less than the theoretical increase in profits and adjusting prices to N is economically profitable."
- The strategic case composes as `rival price → focal belief → focal price → rival belief → ...`. The output re-enters as input. The *Kinked demand* article, opened 2026-11-23, gives the instance: because of "a jump discontinuity in the marginal revenue curve, marginal costs could change without necessarily changing the price or quantity."
- ⇒ The two are **different bonds**, not isomers over a fixed atom set, because they do not share the same atoms: the obstacle case needs an adjustment cost and a contract schedule; the strategic case needs a rival and a return path. They share a **functional group** at most, the `state → (structure) → state` spell, with different qualifiers in the middle. Fig 12 :123: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if both causes can be written over one identical atom set with only the bond changed, they are isomers and the stronger claim holds; if one cause requires an atom the other does not, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. On the census above it fails, so the weaker reading is kept.

### Q2 — What is the order, and does order matter?

- Fig 12 :72 "A then B; B needs A to have happened." The sequence bond is ordered: swapping operands produces a different thing.
- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
- Order for a price decision: information about the state arrives (D5) → cost and contract state are evaluated (D3) → rival response is anticipated (D4) → the ⋛ comparison is made → the price is held or reset (D1) → the index pools the result (D2, D6).
- The position of the ⋛ comparison matters. If a rival's anticipated response is computed **before** the comparison, the gain B in "B − A" already includes the strategic offset; the obstacle is then compared against a strategy-adjusted gain. If the comparison is made **first** and the rival response is considered only afterwards, the two conditions are additive in the economist's account, which Fig 12 :130 names as an error: "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."
- The world literature corroborates that order is load-bearing. The *Nominal rigidity* article, opened 2026-11-23, distinguishes time-dependent models, where "firms decide to change prices and then evaluate market conditions," from state-dependent models, where "firms evaluate market conditions and then decide how to respond." That is a reversal of the first two steps, and the article records that "Prices change faster and monetary shocks are over faster under state dependent than time." The same atoms, a different order, a different aggregate result.
- **Falsifier:** an aggregate response invariant to whether the rival response is included before or after the adjustment-cost comparison. If observed, the order claim is false.

### Q3 — The physical analogue: stick-slip friction

- The Wikipedia *Stick-slip phenomenon* article, opened 2026-11-23, describes a block coupled by a spring to a constant drive: "This force increases until retarding force from the static friction coefficient between load and floor is exceeded. The load then starts sliding, and the friction coefficient decreases to the value corresponding to load times the dynamic friction." The result is "irregular, with brief accelerations (slips) interrupted by stops (sticks)."
- This is a shape analogue of the threshold case, not a derivation of it. A force climbs; below a critical value there is no movement; above it the object slips. The critical value is set by static friction, just as the band is set by adjustment cost. The article adds that "The frequency of slips depends on the force applied to the sliding load, with a higher force corresponding to a higher frequency of slip," which mirrors the menu-cost prediction that more inflation (a larger drift force) raises the frequency of repricing.
- **Limit of the analogue, kept visible:** friction is a physical force with a measured coefficient; a repricing cost is a composed economic quantity whose cost in bits and cost in actions "are in different units" (Fig 12 :126). The analogy carries the threshold shape, not the quantity. No figure line licenses importing the coefficient. This is a shadow, not an atom (Fig 8 :53 "Residual first, frame second.").

### Q4 — The strategic analogue: the kink as a return path

- Fig 12 :121 "The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."
- The *Kinked demand* article, opened 2026-11-23: the "kink" is "a hypothesized concave bend with a discontinuity at the bend," so that "the first derivative point is undefined and leads to a jump discontinuity in the marginal revenue curve." Hall and Hitch based the initial price on "full cost" and reported that "An overwhelming majority of the entrepreneurs thought that a price based on full average cost…was the 'right' price."
- That last sentence matters: the entrepreneurs' own account is a **Γ-internal reading**. The article's *Criticism* section records Stigler's objection that the model "only explains observed phenomenon and is not predictive" and "does not describe the mechanism that establishes the kink and how the kink can reform once prices change." The kink is a shape; its source is owed. Fig 10 :52 "Bring a characterised residual, never the source."
- **Falsifier:** a market in which the rival return path is measured directly and the marginal revenue curve is smooth at the incumbent price. Then the cycle mapping fails for that slot.

### Q5 — Is there a seventh condition hiding in the two?

- The *Nominal rigidity* article records that "Sticky information models do not have nominal rigidity: firms or unions are free to choose different prices or wages for each period. It is the information that is sticky, not the prices." It reports that this is "at odds with the empirical evidence on prices" and that "This has led to attempts to formulate a 'dual stickiness' model that combines sticky information with sticky prices."
- Re-census: information is a **channel** condition (Fig 10 :24), not a renamed adjustment cost and not a strategic response. Adding it does not create a new primitive; it selects a different already-held atom and a different anchor. It is the third member of the union that the question's either/or omitted. No seventh kind is introduced, so Fig 13's closure is not breached.

## CONCLUSION

The two candidate causes are **different bonds with different anchors**, not isomers and not opposite ends of a dial. The cost-and-contract case is a threshold comparison (⋛); the strategic case is a feedback cycle whose return path is the rival's price (Fig 12 :121). They share only a functional group, the price spell itself. Their order is load-bearing: whether the rival response enters before or after the adjustment-cost comparison changes the aggregate response, and the time-dependent versus state-dependent literature is the world-side instance. Stick-slip friction is a genuine low-overlap shape analogue for the threshold, but it carries the threshold form only, not the economic quantity. A third condition, the information channel, is already present in the union and was omitted by the question. The falsifiers above are stated in advance; none has been run here.
