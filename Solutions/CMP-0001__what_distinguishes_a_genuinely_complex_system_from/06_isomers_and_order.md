# Why the parts are not the thing: structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two questions are owed before the main verdict can be used: (1) why does an inventory of parts fail to decide the question at all, and (2) what kind of comparison is "complex versus complicated"? Arity, symmetry and order matter here because a wrongly typed comparison will produce a plausible but false criterion.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting)

Atoms held: the parts of any system under discussion, whatever the field. Bonds held: the seven, plus negation. Currency: description bits and test actions, held apart (Fig 12 :125-126). Anchor: the behaviour of the whole. No new primitive is introduced; this derivation only re-arranges what the figures already hold.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Why an ingredient list cannot decide it

Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."*

The figures cite a real precedent, not an invented analogy. Fig 12 :97-98: *"This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation. Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; named isomerism by Berzelius, 1830."*

So "how many parts" is the wrong *kind* of question about a system. It asks for the formula where the bond is what carries the difference. This is the structural reason for F1 in `01`, and it is a tutorial precedent, not a borrowed verdict.

### Q2. The four structures that actually carry the difference

Fig 12 :116: *"Four structures a compositional system exhibits, and each names something the flat form cannot write."*

| structure | verbatim figure line | what it contributes to CMP-0001 |
|---|---|---|
| isomer | Fig 12 :117 *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* | Same parts, different arrangement gives different behaviour. The core of the answer. |
| catalyst | Fig 12 :119 *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* | A part count cannot tell a regenerating part from a spent one, so it cannot tell a self-maintaining arrangement from a static one. |
| cycle | Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* | The positive separator. A complicated chain cannot close by construction. |
| functional group | Fig 12 :123 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* | Lets us say "this is a complex kind of arrangement" without claiming that every complex system shares the same parts. |

The four are not a checklist to be scored and summed. They are four different ways the flat form fails, and a system can exhibit any subset. This matters for F5: a single number would erase which failure is present.

### Q3. The order of the comparison

The comparison "genuinely complex" versus "merely complicated" is a threshold relation between two quantities, not a symmetric join. Operators table :48-52 defines ⋛ as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* with the test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."* So the separator is ordered, and swapping the terms does not produce the same claim.

Two orders must be kept distinct:

1. **Dependency order (Fig 3).** Anchor first, then vocabulary for bonds and loops, then the criterion, then classification. Fig 3 :19 *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
2. **Bond order within the system.** Fig 12 :74 (Operators table :30-33) defines → as sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* with the test *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."* The order in which parts are bonded is part of what the system is, so two systems with the same unordered parts can be inverses of one another. Fig 12 :96: *"Two entries can share every ingredient and be inverses of one another."*

These two orders are not the same object. Conflating them is a strain and sends the reading back to the census; it did not require one in this run.

### Q4. Two currencies, held apart

Fig 12 :125: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."*
Fig 12 :126: *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."*

A part count is a bits quantity. Testing whether the arrangement actually produces non-decomposable behaviour is an actions quantity. A system can be cheap to state and expensive to settle, or the reverse. Fig 12 :128-129 (normalised): *"A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle."* Neither figure predicts the other. So the decision cannot be made by summing "number of parts" with "number of feedback loops" into one score. That is the structural reason for F5 and F8 in `01`.

### Q5. Chaos is a derived quantity, not a seventh member

Fig 13 :124: *"Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member."*
Fig 13 :125: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*

This closes the question raised by the world-source conflict recorded in `01` section 5. Chaos is what a turn leaves, not a member of the separating set, and different arrangements can leave the same trace. Kauffman's *"Chaos is a subset of complexity"* (Edge, 1996) and Wiesner and Ladyman's *"Certainly, chaos and complexity are two distinct phenomena"* (2020) are both consistent with this reading once chaos is put in the derived column. They are not consistent with the draft that put chaos in the criterion column. That draft is retracted.

## FALSIFIER, written before the run

This supporting derivation fails if any of the following holds:

1. A chemistry-style isomer case is found where identical ingredients under different bonds give identical behaviour under a discriminating instrument, so the bond does not carry the difference (kills Q1 and Q2).
2. "Complex versus complicated" is shown to be a symmetric bond, so swapping the terms leaves the claim unchanged (kills Q3).
3. A single additive score in one currency correctly ranks complex versus complicated across cases where the two currencies come apart (kills Q4).
4. Chaos is admitted as a seventh member of the separating set by a figure line not considered here (kills Q5).

## CONCLUSION

The part list is a flat formula: it records ingredients and drops the arrangement. The separator is the arrangement, expressed as isomer, catalyst, cycle and functional group, priced in two currencies that do not add. The comparison is ordered, and the orders of dependency and of bonding are different objects. Chaos is a derived trace, not a member of the separating set. None of this yields a single number, which is the intended result.
