# Supporting derivation: the two failure regimes as isomers, and the order of fidelity versus selection

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit names two failure edges of the fidelity band: dispersal at too-low fidelity and stasis at too-high fidelity. Two supporting questions are owed. (1) Are these two different states of the same system, or two different systems? Fig 12's isomer test decides. (2) Which comes first, a copying channel with the right fidelity or a habitat that applies pressure? Fig 3's dependency order and Fig 9's union rule decide, and the answer is not the obvious temporal one.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: a template, a copy, a residual (error), a habitat. Bonds held: the seven, including identity ≡, production ⇒ and comparison ⋛. Negation ¬ is available but is not a bond. No new primitive is available without import. Scale: one replication cycle is the level on which the two regimes are compared; a lineage is the level on which the order question is decided.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the two edges isomers of the copying relation?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance."** Fig 12 :95-96: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."**
- At the high-fidelity edge the bond between template and copy is identity, ≡: *"two names, one referent"* (Operators table :65-67). Substituting the copy for the template loses nothing. The copy adds no term to the library.
- At the low-fidelity edge the bond is production with noise, ⇒: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Operators table :75-77). The test is *"is there a B before A fires? No."* The copy is a genuine new term, but so many new terms appear that localisation about the original is lost.
- Both regimes use the same atoms: template, copy, residual, habitat. They differ only in which bond holds. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers of the copying relation**, not two points on one dial and not two different systems.
- This is what makes the question hard in practice: a flat notation that records only ingredients and an error rate cannot tell the two regimes apart (Fig 12 :6-17, collapse 6, *"the parts are not the thing"*). The bond carries the information the rate does not.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the two regimes can be fully distinguished by the magnitude of the error rate alone, with no difference in bond type, the isomer claim fails and the band is a continuous quantity rather than a structural compound. Equivalently, if a perfect identity copy can generate adaptive heritable novelty from the copying operation alone, the identity reading fails.

### Q2 - Is self-replication a cycle or a chain?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- A template that produces a copy which serves as the next template is a cycle. The question "does the lineage persist?" is the question "does the loop close?". The sign of the loop is decided by the remainder: too small and the cycle closes but generates nothing new (stasis), too large and it fails to close (dispersal).
- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** A catalytic RNA that copies while surviving its own use is a catalyst in the figure's sense. A non-catalytic template is spent. This distinction is exactly what Obermayer and Frey (2010) analyse as the enzyme/substrate twofold role, quoted in `04`.
- **Falsifier:** a self-replicating system whose output does not re-enter as input and whose persistence is still a lineage would break the cycle reading. A single-use template with no heredity would not count as a lineage.

### Q3 - What is the order: fidelity first, or selection first?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** The dependency chain runs perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry (Fig 3 :4-15).
- Applied: first contact (can a nucleotide be copied at all), then vocabulary (can errors be indexed per site), then objective (what counts as progress at a handoff), then route (hold both factors nonzero), then carry (the survivor becomes the next template). Fidelity is a property of the route step; selection pressure is the objective step's ground. **The objective precedes the route in dependency order.**
- But this is **dependency order, not temporal order**. Fig 9 :57-58: **"What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."** In the prebiotic case there is no seat to install the objective first and then tune the chemistry. Both conditions must be present together in the compound, or the lineage does not get off the ground. Asking "which evolved first" imports a timed sequence the figures do not license.
- Fig 3 :26-27: **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."** **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."** The objective link is the one most often assumed rather than measured in origins-of-life work.
- **Falsifier:** a demonstration that the objective is installed temporally before the copying channel in a concrete prebiotic system, with a measurable interval in which selection pressure exists without any replication, would overturn the "both together" reading and require a new census.

### Q4 - What kind of claim is the fidelity question?

- Fig 12 :112: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."**
- "Accurately enough but inaccurately enough" is a comparison against two edges: a threshold relation, ⋛. Operators table :48-52: *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, with the test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."* Operators table :51: **"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"**.
- Fig 12 :125-127: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."** **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."** Bits price how much information a sequence can carry; actions price testing a variant against the habitat. A claim that reports only an error rate (bits) and never the cost of testing (actions) reads only one currency.
- **Falsifier:** show that the question is better written as a sequence (→) or a conjunction (+) of two independent conditions rather than a threshold comparison with two bounds. That would shift the bond typing and change the kinds of instrument needed.

## CONCLUSION

The high-fidelity and low-fidelity failure regimes are **isomers of the copying relation**: same atoms, different bond (identity versus noisy production), different substance (a mirror chain versus a delocalised mutant cloud). Replication is a **cycle**, not a chain, so persistence is the closure of the loop. The order between fidelity and selection is a **dependency order** (objective before route), not a temporal order; in the unmaintained prebiotic case both conditions bind at once. The fidelity question itself is a **comparison with two bounds**, and its answer must be priced in both bits and actions.

This rescues what is defensible in the single-number answer without the part that fails: the band, the two bonds and the cycle need no new primitive, no import that moves the wall, and no naming of the critical rate. The claim remains `specified`, ungrounded. The isomer and cycle readings are structural; whether any actual prebiotic system realised them is a ground question.
