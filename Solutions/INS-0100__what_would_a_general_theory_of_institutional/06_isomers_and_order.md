# The cycle, the order, and the modes of institutional change

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Three questions are owed: (1) what kind of arrangement joins "rules shape choices" to "people reshape rules"; (2) which direction comes first in the dependency order; (3) do the observed modes of institutional change (layering, drift, conversion, displacement) differ as bond types, as isomers, or only as spellings?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What bond joins the two directions?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

A chain cannot close by construction, so a linear "rules cause choices cause new rules" model cannot be the right structure. The two directions must be written as a cycle: the output of one turn (a choice, a compliance reading, a contested outcome) re-enters as the input to the next (a refusal or a re-binding of the rule). A rule that survives its own use is Fig 12's catalyst: it is spent in each application and regenerated by the field; a rule that is not regenerated is spent and drops out of the reach. The two directions share a functional group (a per-slot residual ranged against a ground) with different atoms in the slots.

- **Status:** triangulated for the cycle (Fig 12 :121 + Formula step 8, the reach driving the loop); single-source for the catalyst reading (Fig 12 :119) until an instrument distinguishes spent from regenerated rules.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Operators table :30-33: `→` is *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*; the test is *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."*

Order downward first: a rule can only be refused after it has been held and has generated a prediction, so perception and vocabulary precede the objective (Fig 3 :4-15). The objective link ("what would count as progress") is the least instrumented and therefore the one to build first (F9 of the audit). Formally:

```
perceive rule and choice -> predict per slot -> observe o' -> residual R
  -> refuse the held rule whose R persists flat (unary ¬)
  -> mint or import a replacement -> settle against the ground
  -> the settled replacement becomes an atom for the next turn
```

Swapping refusal before perception produces a different term: refusing a rule no one has held generates no residual and is not a change. So the ordered bond `→` is correct here, and the symmetric bond `+` would overcharge by pretending the order does not matter.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a field can refuse a rule it has not held and for which no per-slot residual exists, the order claim fails.

### Q3 - Are the observed modes of institutional change isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :95-96: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."**

The world literature supplies the candidate spellings: Streeck and Thelen (2005) name *"displacement, layering, drift, conversion, and exhaustion"*; Mahoney and Thelen (2010) define *"Displacement: the removal of existing rules and the introduction of new ones"* and *"Layering: the introduction of new rules on top of or alongside existing ones"*, plus drift and conversion. Are these isomers?

- **As commonly written, no.** The modes use different atoms (new rules vs old rules vs changed environment vs same rules redeployed), so by Fig 12's definition they are not the same atoms under a different bond.
- **The stronger reading, yes.** If all five are rewritten over the same atom set (a held rule, a persistent residual, an introduction available to a seat, an unchanged habitat, a ground), they become same atoms, different bond, different substance:
  - **displacement** is refusal plus production (`¬ ⇒`): old rule refused, new rule produced;
  - **layering** is conjunction plus sequence (`+ →`): new rule added to old, order matters over time;
  - **drift** is comparison against a changed environment with the rule held fixed (`⋛` with the habitat as the changing operand);
  - **conversion** is the same rule under a new production (`⇒` with a changed objective);
  - **exhaustion** is subtraction of what the rule can spend (`−`), the depletion reading from Streeck and Thelen's table.

This is a checkable structural claim, not a restatement. It predicts that the five modes leave different observable traces even when the formal rule text is unchanged (Fig 12 :95-96: the difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour).

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two modes written over the shared atom set produce the same trace on the same ground, the isomer claim fails and the weaker functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q4 - What analogue corroborates the cycle from a low-overlap field?

- Fig 11 :49: **"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."**
- Formaux et al. (2021/2022), animal behaviour: *"conventions readily emerge in our experimental set-up and that they share three fundamental properties of human conventions (arbitrariness, stability and efficiency)."*
- van Leeuwen (2021), animal behaviour: *"chimpanzees adhere to arbitrary group-specific handclasp preferences that cannot be explained by genetics or the ecological environment"*, with within-group homogeneity and between-group heterogeneity sustained over 12 years.

Guinea baboons coordinating on touchscreen choices and chimpanzees maintaining a group-specific handclasp are instances of a convention that no designer authored, that is arbitrary, stable and locally efficient, and that persists through changes in membership. That is the habitat and room-chain shape at a non-human stratum. It corroborates two things: a rule need not be decreed to shape choices, and stability is not evidence of correctness. It does not corroborate anything about which human institution is good. Fig 2 :37 remains the anchor rule: *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people."*

- **Status:** single-source within the figures (Fig 11 valence), with a genuinely low-overlap world source. World sources corroborate; they never decide.

### Q5 - Where is the round-trip gap?

- Fig 4 :30: **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

A general shape abstracted upward from many institutional studies (T_A) and brought back down to a specific field (T_E) will not match what was sent. Three things are dropped: which ground settles that field's residual; which frame has a closure different enough to import from; and which actor holds the seat that can introduce but not subtract. R_T is nonzero and is recorded as an enumerated set of dropped constraints, not assumed to be zero. Treating "the formula is the condensed version of the metalaws" as identity would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a field-level prediction made from the shape survives re-concretisation without naming a ground, an import source and a seat, the gap claim is refuted.

## CONCLUSION

The two directions form a **cycle**, not a chain, with a catalyst-like rule that is spent and regenerated across turns. The dependency order is downward first, refusal second, mint or import third, settlement fourth. The five observed modes of institutional change are **not isomers as usually written**, but become a real isomer claim when all are restated over one shared atom set and distinguished by bond type; falsifiers for both readings are stated above. A low-overlap animal-behaviour analogue corroborates that conventions emerge and persist without a designer, while the anchor for human institutional questions remains human judgment corrected by an outcome neither frame can move. The round trip from general shape to specific field carries a nonzero R_T, which is why the theory must stop at a shape and record its lineage.
