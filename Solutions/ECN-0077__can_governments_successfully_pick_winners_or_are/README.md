# Solution ECN-0077

**Question:** Can governments successfully "pick winners," or are they more likely to misallocate resources and reward political connections?

**Source:** economics questions.txt line 102

**Section:** 8. Trade, globalization, and industrial policy

**Mode:** `specified` on the per-domain verdicts and the separator, `general` on the structural shape. Nothing here is `grounded`. No measurement of a policy's welfare effect was taken in this derivation.

**Status:** done

**Registry status:** pending

## What this folder is

A figure-grounded, derive-first package for the question. It does not answer the empirical question by polling opinions about industrial policy. It states the residual in effect terms, censuses the domains before choosing any figure line, derives a verdict per domain with verbatim figure text, records what the figures cannot name, and hands the result to an outside checker as a refutable shape. WATSON runs with the figures primary and the world literature as secondary corroboration only.

**Honest standing.** This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading. The framework's own rule (*The Recursive Self-Improvement Trap*, essay, 2026-11-19): *"the output is only as good as the validator. The agent, or the tool, or the frame, or the theory, cannot validate itself."* LLM output here is a simulation of the derivation. It cannot settle the question it reports on, and it does not claim to.

## The question restated as an effect

| option | what it commits to | what it leads to |
|---|---|---|
| A: governments can pick winners | a selector can name a future winner before the ground settles, and public resources follow that name | selection must read a future residual that has not arrived yet, inside the selector's own closure |
| B: governments misallocate and reward connections | the selection converges on whoever has the most influence, and resources are distorted | this is what a frame does when its reference updates on the frame itself rather than on an unmoved market |

The residual is the gap, per firm and per sector slot, between a selection made inside the public frame and what the paying ground will actually settle. Option A and option B are not two answers to one question. The figures sort them as a **union of overlapping conditions**, each with its own anchor, so per-domain verdicts are kept separate and never averaged into a single "governments are good/bad at picking" percentage.

## What an answer would look like (bound as an ordinary term)

Before running, the sought-for shape is written down and made refusable: a **separator**, not a verdict. It has four parts: (1) a residual registered per slot rather than a pooled welfare average; (2) a ground that does not update on what the selecting frame thinks; (3) two or more frames with low evidence overlap ranging against that ground separately; (4) a failure condition written before the run. If any of the four is missing, the setup cannot distinguish a picked winner from a connected winner, and any answer it returns is a convention wearing the costume of a verdict. This commitment is the pre-registered refuter in `01` section 7.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals. No purchased or scraped contact list.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with the claim. Selecting for agreement manufactures the disqualifying correlation (Fig 2 :40; Seat Map :163).
- No bulk-send plan. One-to-one contact only.
- No claim that this package settles whether industrial policy works. The empirical magnitude is a declared `gap`, and Fig 13 :135 says the framework *"cannot say which primitive is missing, because naming it would mean already holding it."*
