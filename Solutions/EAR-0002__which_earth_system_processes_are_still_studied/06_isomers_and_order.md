# Supporting derivation: structure, order and analogues for coupled Earth-system processes

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the bond definitions.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit establishes that the combined effect lives in a typed coupling bond and that the literal list of processes is a `gap`. Three supporting questions are owed:

1. **Structure.** Are two named coupled feedbacks, say ice-albedo and carbon-climate, isomers over a shared atom set, or merely members of a functional group? This decides whether "different coupled outcomes over the same processes" is a strong or weak claim.
2. **Order.** What is the dependency order of a coupled derivation, and where does a chain fail to express a feedback?
3. **Analogue.** Does the cycle bond give a better notation for a feedback than the sequence bond, and does that change what counts as "studied too separately"?

## DERIVATION

### Q1  -  Are coupled feedbacks isomers over a shared atom set?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 13 :121:

> **"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."**

Fig 12 :123:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Take two published coupled mechanisms as candidates. Ice-albedo coupling: temperature (state) changes a surface property (structure, spectrum-like reflectivity), which changes absorbed energy (state), which changes temperature. Carbon-climate coupling: carbon burden (state) changes a radiative structure (spectrum-like forcing), which changes temperature (state), which changes the carbon sink (structure, network/gradient), which changes the burden. Over the full shared atom set `{Space, Time, Energy, Spectrum, Gradient, Network}` both can be written as first-order cross-kind molecules chained into a cycle. On the stricter reading where each is written with only the atoms it visibly uses, the two share only the state side and are **not** isomers under Fig 12 :117; they are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :123), a state perturbed through a structure back to a state.

The strong claim, worth testing: if both are written over the full shared atom set, differing only in which structure mediates which state pair, they are **isomers**, same atoms and different bond, with a behaviour difference invisible in the ingredient list. This is exactly what the planetary-boundaries literature reports in effect terms when it says interactions create both stabilizing and destabilizing feedbacks: same set of nine processes, different arrangement, different system behaviour.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either mechanism can be written without an atom the other requires, for example a temperature coupling with no structure term at all, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q2  -  Cycle versus chain: why a list cannot express a feedback

Fig 12 :48:

> **"A molecule is a bonded arrangement. A chain is the degenerate case: one bond type, no branching. Nothing about a chain is a different kind of object."**

Fig 12 :121:

> **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Fig 12 :111:

> **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."**

A list of processes is a flat formula: the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not (Fig 12 :14). A chain can express "A then B" (Fig 12 :71: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*) but cannot close. A feedback is a cycle whose sign decides whether it closes. Therefore the question's list form is not merely incomplete, it is the wrong notation for the objects it names. This is a structural result about notation, not an empirical claim about any particular feedback.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a coupled feedback that is correctly represented as an acyclic chain under every measurement, so the cycle bond adds nothing.

### Q3  -  What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 :19-20:

> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**

Fig 3 :27:

> **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

Fig 12 :106:

> **"Nothing composes over what was never admitted as a part."**

Fig 12 :104:

> **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world, and there is no bottom"**

The order is:

1. **Enumerate the habitat** (Fig 11 :43-44), because the residual may be third order.
2. **Settle each component process** against its own ground. An unsettled component cannot be an operand (Fig 12 :106).
3. **Compose the pair** using the bond that matches the observed relation, then settle the coupled term. Once settled, the coupled term becomes an atom for the next level (Fig 12 :104).
4. **Refuse** a term whose residual persists and does not trend down before minting another (`THE_FORMULA.md` step 2). *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"* (Operators :58).

Ordering by importance, or by publication volume, is a filter for the budget, not a dependency order. Effort spent on a well-instrumented link above the break arrives at nothing (Fig 3 :19).

### Q4  -  Bond type at the coupled level: a live strain

Fig 12 :87:

> **"a threshold relation between two quantities"**

Fig 12 :104 (above) permits a settled coupled model to serve as an atom at the next level. Whether that atom counts as a quantity at that level is a type question. If it does, a comparison bond between two settled coupled models is legal; if it does not, the comparison is a claim that has not been made yet (Operators :68: *"A relation that fails all seven is not an operator you are missing; it is a claim that has not been made yet."*) This needs an instrument, not an argument. It is recorded as live in every file that uses it, including `01` section 7 and `11_audit.md`.

**Falsifier:** show that a settled coupled model can never be treated as a quantity at the next level, which would forbid the second-order comparison outright.

### Q5  -  Scale and the round trip

Fig 4 :19-20:

> **"The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked, whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."**

Fig 4 :30:

> **"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."**

A component model carried up into a coupled model is a method or a head start only if it can be reapplied (Fig 4 :22: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."*). A recording of one successful component simulation is a description of one occasion (Fig 4 :25). Because the transform between scales loses information, the round trip must be measured; the gap R_T is not zero and is not an error to eliminate but the measure of what the coarser description cannot hold.

## CONCLUSION

Coupled feedbacks written over a full shared atom set are a real isomer claim (Fig 12 :117), with the functional-group reading (Fig 12 :123) as the weaker standing fallback when the atom sets differ. A feedback is a cycle (Fig 12 :121), so a process list is the wrong notation. The dependency order is enumerate, settle, compose, settle, refuse, and never optimise above a broken link. One type question remains live: whether a settled coupled model is a quantity for a comparison bond at the next level. The isomer and cycle claims are `specified` and ungrounded; the type question is an instrument requirement.
