# Species as atoms, relationships as bonds: structure and order in a conservation strategy

**Mode: `specified` on the per-slot ordering, `general` on the structural claim.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks which strategy protects relationships as well as species. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) are species and relationships the same kind of thing in the composition, so that one metric can rank them? (2) what is the order of a strategy's effects, first-order contact versus second-order cascade, and does that order change the ranking?

Atoms available: the species present and the abiotic conditions. Bonds available: the typed relations (pollination, predation, competition, dependency; the figure's four examples at Fig 11 :47). ## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Are species and relationships the same kind of thing? NO.

Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
Fig 12 :46-47 "A recipe that names only its ingredients has recorded half of itself."
Fig 11 :50 "The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."

A species is an atom; a relationship is a typed bond between atoms. They are different kinds of thing in the composition, so a single score that mixes them is a flat formula: it records the ingredients and not the arrangement. Fig 12 :94 "The arrangement is the substance." The consequence for conservation is that species richness and interaction persistence are two axes, and a ranking that reports only one has dropped half the problem. Triangulated across Fig 12 and Fig 11, two figures with low overlap.

### Q2 : Do two strategies with the same species set differ only by arrangement? YES: the isomer case.

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two protected areas with the same species list but different interaction wiring are isomers under Fig 12's definition. They have the same atoms and different bonds, so they are different substances, and the difference is invisible in a species-only formula. Two strategies that share only the structural motif "state, relation, state" but not the species are weaker: they are members of the same **functional group**, not isomers. The isomer claim is the stronger, checkable one: hold the species set fixed and vary the interaction arrangement, then measure whether outcomes differ.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two strategies over the same species set with different interaction wiring return identical per-slot residuals at the ground, the isomer reading fails and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3 : What is the order of a strategy's effects?

Fig 11 :44 "List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."
Fig 11 :52-53 "Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."

Order is by dependency, not importance. A first-order effect is on a species in direct contact with the intervention. A second-order effect is on a relation one step out. A third-order effect is on a species or relation two steps out, as with DDE biomagnification into eggshell thinning. Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." So a strategy ranked by first-order species response can be overturned by a third-order interaction loss. The DDT case is recorded at the ground by the US EPA CADDIS page, opened 2026-11-23, as a world corroboration, not as the verdict.

**Falsifier:** if a strategy's ranking is invariant under adding second- and third-order contacts to the enumeration, the order claim fails.

### Q4 : Is introduction equivalent to restoring the original habitat? NO: substitution.

Fig 11 :66 "Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."
Fig 11 :67 "Move the residual and you must reproduce everything its goal required, and you inherit the new habitat's actors, which you did not choose."
Fig 11 :70 "A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability."

An ex-situ population, a restored plot rebuilt from a species list, and an offset site are substitutions. The order of loss matters: what was not reproduced is invisible until the goal fails, and what was unintentionally introduced is invisible until it acts (Fig 11 :68-69). Jones, Lawton and Shachak 1994 supply the world concept for the maintained mode, "modify, maintain and create habitats"; Snyder et al. 1996 supply the caution that captive breeding "should not displace habitat and ecosystem protection". Both are secondary corroboration.

### Q5 : What analogue does the framework itself use for this domain?

The canonical README and the *Arrival of the Fittest* essay both refuse the obvious analogue. The habitat is explicitly not the ecological niche: a niche is defined by an organism's relation to its environment, while a habitat here is defined by contact with a residual and changes when the residual changes. So the analogy is a near miss, and using it as the derivation would be echo without shadow. The legitimate analogue is the **cascade**: introduction is available and exclusion is not, and an unenumerated cascade is the cane toad and kudzu failure. This is why F3 and F5 in `01` are derived from Fig 11 rather than from niche theory.

## CONCLUSION

Species and relationships are different kinds in the composition, so a species-only metric is a flat formula that has dropped the arrangement (Q1). Strategies with the same species set and different wiring are isomers, a checkable structural claim with a stated falsifier (Q2). Effects must be ordered by dependency out to the point where the cascade stops mattering, because a third-order interaction loss can overturn a first-order species gain (Q3). Substitutions are not restorations and hide two distinct failure modes (Q4). The near-miss niche analogy is declined; the cascade analogue is retained (Q5).
