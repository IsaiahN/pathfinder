**Subject:** Check one claim about trust-learning before you decide whether the framework is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it or to trust it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`general` on the shape, `specified` on the domain predictions, not `grounded`):** a learner learning whom to trust is doing a per-person calculation, not forming one global trust level. The framework's own rule is that the residual is indexed by object slot, never aggregated. So "how trusting is this person" throws away the signal that "whom do I trust here" asks for.
>
> Three further predictions, from separate domains and kept separate:
>
> 1. The anchor is what the trustee actually did, not how many peers endorse them. A network that repeats one report produces agreement, and the framework says an anchor must not change its answer because a frame thinks something. Two people reading the same report are not two independent checks. Importantly, the framework also says correlation has valence: high correlation is disqualifying for a verdict about the world and efficient for a convention about shared meaning. So a closed network can produce a perfectly good norm and still tell you nothing about whether the trustee is reliable.
> 2. The weighting between private experience and inherited prior is learnable, and that is the axis on which networks and institutions differ. Kinship-based trust and court-backed trust are different priors, not different levels of the same virtue.
> 3. Trustworthiness is a relation between person and habitat, not a property the person carries. The same individual can be the right trustee under one set of norms and enforcement and the wrong one under another. Betrayal is a refusal of a held term, not one more low observation to average in.
>
> **What it does not claim:** that it names the cognitive algorithm, that it produces a single trust score, that a high-trust society is morally better than a low-trust one, or that this is settled by experiment. It returns a shape, not a name. The mechanism is recorded as a gap at this budget, not hidden.
>
> **What would refute it (written before this message):**
> 1. a trust decision where the residual is one scalar, the per-slot structure carries no signal, and aggregation loses nothing;
> 2. a network that agrees on a trustee from strictly independent readings of that trustee's behaviour, where independence changes nothing about what the agreement means;
> 3. the same actor rated trustworthy under two different habitats and enforcement regimes with no change in contact;
> 4. a trust system that tracks the ground through betrayal while recording no refusal at all.
>
> If any one holds, I retract the corresponding claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figure text, not my summary of it, is the authority.
> - Figures and formula (local canonical extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`, `FRAMEWORK/figures/THE_FORMULA.md`
> - Figures README, canonical usage rule (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`
> - WATSON 60, the figures as metalaws (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/60_FIGURES_AS_METALAWS.md`
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the formula file. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result before you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
