**Subject:** Check one output on catalyst encoding before you decide whether the framing is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output about the transition from prebiotic catalysts to genetically encoded catalysts that I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified` on the parts, `general` on the shape, `gap` on the history):**
>
> 1. A catalyst is a part that survives its own use. That persistence, not a list of ingredients, is what makes it a catalyst.
> 2. Genetic encoding is the recording of an arrangement so that a later lineage pays a lookup instead of re-deriving the catalyst. Where the arrangement is not recorded, the result carries forward and the deriving does not, so every lineage pays again.
> 3. The move from chemical catalysis to inherited catalysis crosses a scale boundary. Only methods cross up; only head starts come down. A gene that reliably regenerates a working catalyst is a generator. A dead copied sequence is a recording of one occasion.
> 4. A catalyst that has been settled against chemical kinetics becomes an atom for whatever composes at the inherited level. No new primitive is needed for that step.
> 5. The catalytic function is a functional group: ribozyme, protein enzyme and metal cluster are members, not the same molecule. They are isomers only if you first declare a shared atom set, which I do not do by default.
> 6. An amino-acid alphabet is not derivable by rearranging an RNA-only closure. It enters as an import, and imports need a source and a shadow test. Cofactor fossils and exaptation are candidate shapes for that import, not derivations.
> 7. Nobody chose the route. In an unmaintained habitat, selection ran. The habitat has to be enumerated from the residual, not composed from a standing list of important molecules.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that this is the historical sequence of events; that the first encoded catalyst can be named; that the RNA world is settled; that ribozymes and enzymes are isomers in the strict sense; or that any of this is `grounded`. The literal route is a `gap`, and I am reporting it as a verdict rather than dressing it as a story.
>
> **What would refute it:**
> 1. a catalytic part that does not persist across its own use, so spent and regenerating parts are indistinguishable;
> 2. an inherited sequence that does not turn re-derivation into a lookup, so every generation still pays the full cost;
> 3. evidence that what crossed the boundary was a replay of one occasion with no re-applicability;
> 4. a settled catalyst that cannot become an atom for an inherited composition;
> 5. a derivation of the new alphabet inside the starting closure, or an import accepted with no source gate or shadow test;
> 6. an anchor for catalysis or heritability that moves when people agree on it;
> 7. a measurement of sequence-specified catalytic persistence arising without the recorded-arrangement step.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.

## Two-stage offer, explicit

1. **Stage 1.** Send the seven-point output and its refuter. Ask: what would you expect to see if this shape were true, and what would count as a failure? Record the verdict before showing any framework material.
2. **Stage 2, opt-in only.** If they are curious, offer the links. Their later agreement is never pooled as corroboration (Fig 2 :40: "agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool").
3. **Stage 3, optional.** Offer the formula as a method, with the instruction that each user writes their own refuter and states their own mode.

The first contact carries the residual and the refuter, never the framework interior. A Track B style reply, if one is ever received, is a conversation and carries zero verdict weight.
