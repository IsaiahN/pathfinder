## Q4 - Are the phases isomers, or members of one functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 12 :95: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond." Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."
- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Fig 12 :97 is emphatic that this is not an analogy: "silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties". The isomer claim here is the same structural relation, not a loose comparison.
- Three levels of the claim:
  1. **Ordered magnet versus spin liquid over the same spins.** Same atoms, different bond (symmetry-breaking order versus topological superposition), different substance. Strong figure support. The formula "spins on a kagome lattice" cannot tell them apart.
  2. **Z2 versus U(1) spin liquid over the full shared atom set** `{Space, Time, Energy, Spectrum, Gradient, Network}`. Both are compositions over the same six, differing in which structure mediates which state pair. This is the real isomer claim. Wen 2001 (opened) reports "we construct hundreds of symmetric spin liquids, which have SU(2), U(1) or Z2 gauge structures at low energies", which is the world-level statement that many members of one group exist.
  3. **Lattice geometry as a functional group.** Fig 12 :123: geometry fixes the kind; the gauge group is the qualifier. Fig 13 :76: "A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is." So a kagome Z2 and a honeycomb Z2 are not the same term even with the same qualifier.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either phase can be written without an atom the other requires (a U(1) spin liquid with no network atom, or a Z2 spin liquid with no spectrum), the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. This is the sub-claim C2 in `08_expert_verification_packet.md`.
- **Verdict:** `specified`, single-source on the figure definition (Fig 12), with the physics-facing check unmeasured. Do not upgrade it by citing two materials from one evidence pool.

## Q5 - What is the order of the union?

- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world". Fig 13 :110: "And each composition becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."
- Order 1: the spin phase. `state → structure → state`, one crossing. In the Kitaev honeycomb the crossing is exact and the phase is settled; in candidate materials it is a `specified` claim awaiting a ground.
- Order 2: the effective gauge theory, composed over the settled spin phase. Its own composition is the deconfinement or Higgs relation to a competing phase. Yang, Shannon and Lee (opened) describe transitions "from deconfined to confined phases of a lattice gauge theory" and daughter phases born of a U(1) QSL. These are second-order compositions, legal only because the first-order phase settled.
- Order 3: the continuum limit. Fig 13 :43: "A continuum was the seventh and was removed for failing exactly this". Smoothness is what a composition leaves behind (Fig 13 :124), so the continuum is a further lossy abstraction of the lattice theory, with its own round-trip gap `R_T`.
- Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance." A spin-first route (solve the model, identify the gauge structure) and a gauge-first route (write the lattice gauge theory, find the spin model that realises it) can converge on the same phase. Convergence at maximum independence is corroboration (Fig 8 :50), but only if the routes genuinely differ in their closures and the record shows where each came from.
- **Verdict:** `specified` that the union is second order; `general` that the continuum is a further abstraction. Triangulated across Fig 12 and Fig 13 for the recursion; the continuum step is single-source on Fig 13 with Fig 4 :30 for the gap.

## Q6 - An analogue from outside the field, and its limit

Anderson's "More is Different" (opened via the public biographical record) "emphasized the limitations of reductionism and the existence of hierarchical levels of science, each of which requires its own fundamental principles for advancement." This is a genuine low-overlap corroborator for Q5: a level can be irreducible without being fundamental.

Limit of the analogue: Anderson's levels are scientific disciplines, not the six-term substrate. Using the analogue to *name* a gauge group or select a material would be the echo-without-shadow error (Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"). The analogue corroborates the shape of the order claim; it does not supply a name.

## CONCLUSION

The question is a union of three overlapping conditions, not one relation. "Relationship" is symmetric in form while the substantive claim is ordered, so identity is refused and production retained as `specified`. The phases are isomers over a shared atom set with a stated falsifier; the lattice geometry is the functional group and the gauge group is the qualifier. The union is second order: the spin phase settles first and becomes an atom for the effective gauge theory; the continuum is a further lossy abstraction. What does not follow is a name for any material's gauge group. That stays a `gap`, a verdict and not a silence.
