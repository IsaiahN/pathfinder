**Subject:** One checkable claim about status, authority, and whose knowledge gets trusted

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific claim I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the effect of status and authority on whose knowledge is copied or trusted is not one effect. It is a union of at least three overlapping selections, copying a model, trusting a testimony, and crediting a name, and the valence flips with what the selection produces. Where the product is a shared meaning, a convention, correlation is efficient and a prestigious source is a cheap coordinator. Where the product is a verdict about the world, correlation is disqualifying if the agreeing parties share an evidence pool. The failure mode is structural: in a room with no reachable anchor that does not update on what the room thinks, the group converges on whoever had the most influence, and because the drift is coherent nobody notices. Status can fund a trial of a claim, but it cannot settle the claim. Only an outcome settles it.
>
> **What would make this a verdict rather than a deference:** independent readings, an anchor that does not move when the readers change their minds, and a checker who cannot reconstruct the claim from their own picture of it. Note that a verifier who can already reconstruct the claim cannot verify it.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that authority is always a bias, that a prestigious source is never useful, that any named person is untrustworthy, or that there is a correct amount of deference. The framework returns a shape and a route, not a rank order of people. No figure line gives a credibility weight, and I am not going to invent one.
>
> **What would refute the claim.** I wrote these down before sending this:
> 1. one mechanism with one parameter predicts all of the copy, trust, and credit selections at the decision level;
> 2. a group with no reachable anchor reliably converges on the least influential member, across domains;
> 3. a shared-meaning convention under deference shows no coordination benefit, or correlated agreement with a shared pool matches triangulated agreement against a later measurement;
> 4. a system where prestige settles outcomes shows no compounding error over repeated trials;
> 5. many frames drawing on one evidence pool, with no independent anchor, match a ground measurement as well as independent frames.
>
> If any one holds, I will retract the relevant part. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.

## The two-stage offer

**Stage 1.** Send the claim, its four domains, and the refuter. Ask what the recipient would count as a failure. Record the verdict before showing any theory.

**Stage 2, opt-in only.** If they are curious, offer the three source links with the note that the figures, not this summary, are the authority. Their later agreement is never pooled as corroboration, because by then they have seen the interior (Fig 10 :42, Fig 2 :40).

**Stage 3, opt-in only.** Offer the formula as a generator to reuse, with the instruction that each user writes their own refuter and labels their mode. Fig 4 :22 permits sending a method; what it does not permit is sending one occasion's result and calling it knowledge.

**Hard precondition:** stages 1 to 3 work only if the stage-1 claim survives. `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 4 lists the five failure conditions. Do not send a claim that has already fired one of them.
