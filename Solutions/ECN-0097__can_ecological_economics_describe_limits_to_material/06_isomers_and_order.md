# Union, order and incommensurable currencies: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question arrives as a two-way choice, "A or B?", but `01` §1 found the two frames share atoms (energy, materials, capital, labour, technology) and differ in currency and binding constraints. Three supporting questions are owed:

1. Is this a partition or a union, and what follows if it is a union?
2. What is the dependency order of the comparison?
3. Are the two frames isomers under Fig 12's definition, and does that help or mislead?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
- Fig 9 :55-57: a single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, so the residue becomes "a specific compound" rather than a harder case of either condition.
- Fig 8 :13-14: under high overlap "their union adds nothing"; two names for one frame.
- The two frames share the atoms above but differ in currency and in what counts as a binding constraint. That is a union of overlapping conditions, not a clean partition.
- **Unbundling:** (i) biophysical description of the limit, (ii) monetary valuation and tradeoff, (iii) dynamical projection of the path. Each condition has its own ground and its own lever. A claim about (i) is not refuted by a result about (ii).
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the two frames prove to have identical closures over the relevant slots, the union has no surplus and unbundling is unnecessary (Fig 8 :13-14). Recorded in `01` F8.

### Q2 : What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20-21: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- Fig 3 :22-23: a reading taken below the break is "a reading of nothing."
- Fig 9 :27-28: **"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"**
- Order for this question:
  1. Describe the residual in effect terms (where does the limit bind, per slot?).
  2. Pin the ground (an external measurement, not a frame-internal metric).
  3. Resolve first-order descriptions: each frame's account of one slot.
  4. Only then compare frames at second order with ⋛.
  5. Write the refuter before step 4 (Fig 5 :62-66).
- A verdict delivered at step 4 without steps 1 to 3 is a reading below the break. This is the structural reason a school-versus-school verdict issued before a ground test carries no weight.

### Q3 : Are the two frames isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use."
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As usually written, D1's core is an aggregate `K, L, A → Y` with money as the bond; D2's core is `throughput → stocks and sinks` with physical accounting as the bond. They do **not** share all atoms in the same roles, so under Fig 12's strict definition they are not isomers. They share a **functional group**: both are frames that compose stocks and flows to say what a economy can sustain, with different atoms in the slots.
- The stronger claim: if both are rewritten over the full shared atom set `{energy, materials, capital, labour, technology, sinks, time}`, differing only in which bond mediates which pair, then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is a checkable structural claim, not a school preference.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either frame requires an atom the other cannot express (for example a sink with no monetary proxy, or a price with no physical referent), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. This falsifier is the one named in `08` C2.

### Q4 : The currencies: two readings, held apart

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**
- Fig 12 :130: **"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it. A price in bits and a price in actions are two readings, held apart."**
- Here the two currencies are money (D1) and physical quantity (D2 and D3). They are in different units. A system holding one accounting "cannot compare them" (Fig 12 :126), so it will propose a single index it cannot afford to test.
- Ecological economics' methodological commitment to incommensurability is the shape Fig 12 licenses. Conventional models that monetise every factor commit the named error when the resulting scalar is used as the yardstick for "better."
- **Falsifier:** a single index that sums both currencies, preserves every slot-level residual and passes the ground test. This is the currency refuter in `01` §4, written before the conclusion.

### Q5 : Is "irreversibility" a negation or a subtraction?

- Operators table: "− is A with B removed or absent, which is the absence of an operand. Negation is the refusal of a claim." And: "A refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."
- A limit as "resources remaining after use" is a **subtraction**: the second operand's absence is the point.
- An irreversibility ("this high-entropy state cannot be returned to the low-entropy stock") is not merely a subtraction; it is a **refusal** of the claim that the composition reverses, so it requires ¬. A vocabulary with only − can say what is missing and cannot say that a return is impossible.
- This is why the five-property shape includes load-bearing finiteness and gradients rather than only a budget: a budget is a subtraction, but an irreversible limit is a negation. F3 in `01` is the finding; this is its operator-level derivation.
- **Falsifier:** show that every ecological "irreversibility" in the relevant slots is a reversible subtraction given enough energy. Then ¬ is not owed and the distinction collapses. This refuter is carried into `08` as a checkable sub-claim.

## CONCLUSION

The school question is a union, not a partition, and must be unbundled before search. The comparison is ordered: residual, ground, first-order slot descriptions, then second-order ⋛, with the refuter written before the last step. The two frames are a functional group as ordinarily written, and a genuine isomer claim only over a full shared atom set. Their two currencies are held apart; summing them is the named error. Irreversibility is a negation, not just a subtraction, which is why finiteness must be load-bearing rather than a tunable budget. No new primitive, no import that moves the wall, and no school name is needed for any of this.
