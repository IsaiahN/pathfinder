## The residual in effect terms

Two frames describe the same thing, the limit to material throughput. Conventional growth models describe it with money-denominated aggregates and substitutability assumptions. Ecological economics describes it with biophysical flows, finiteness, and sinks. "Better" is a comparison of the two descriptions against one ground. What each option leads to: treating money as the yardstick lets a frame-internal convention decide what counts as a limit; treating measured physical flows as the yardstick keeps the limit external to both frames and makes the comparison checkable.

## What an answer would look like (bound as an ordinary term)

A **better descriptor of limits** is a frame that (a) ranges against a biophysical ground rather than a frame-internal metric, (b) keeps the residual indexed by material and energy slot rather than aggregating it, (c) treats energy finiteness and spendable gradients as load-bearing, (d) holds monetary and physical currencies apart rather than summing them, and (e) enumerates the habitat out to second and third order. That definition is written down before the run so it can accrue a residual and be refused (Formula step "Direction"; Fig 5 :62-66).

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with ecological economics.
- No bulk-send plan.

Three reasons in the figures' own words:

1. Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." A list chosen for agreement is a mirror.
2. Fig 2 :40: "agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool". Ecological-economics journals and conventional-growth journals often cite the same national accounts; agreement drawn from that shared pool is one witness, not two.
3. Fig 10 :42: "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it." Sending the full framework before asking for a verdict spends the outside reader's independence.
