**Subject:** One checkable output on what moves workers' bargaining power

> Hi [name],
>
> I am writing with one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified` on the shape, `gap` on the ranking):**
>
> The question "productivity, competition, unions, law, or employer concentration?" is treated as a league table of five independent levers. The derivation says that framing fails for two separate reasons. First, the five conditions overlap: unions change effective competition, law changes whether unions can exist and sets wage floors, and employer concentration is one reading of the same network that labor-market competition describes. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing. Second, the residual is per slot. Aggregating across slots into one winner is how the signal about where to look disappears.
>
> What survives is a shape. Productivity is a support condition: it sets the size of the surplus, not who can claim it. Bargaining power is a property of the worker and the habitat together, not of the worker alone, so an improvement that does not change the worker's contact with alternatives changes nothing however much it improves. Competition and concentration are two readings of one network condition, not two independent determinants. Unions introduce an actor into the habitat rather than adding a new atom. Law arrives from the polity frame as a provenanced import, and a declared rule is not a measurement of what it does.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that any one of the five dominates, by how much, or in which country or period. The figures give a shape, not a coefficient. The empirical ranking is recorded as `gap`, which is a verdict and not a silence. I am also not claiming that agreement among economists settles it. The anchor for each slot is a measured outcome.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim.** I wrote these down before this message:
> 1. The five conditions are shown to be independent, with no overlaps: unions never change effective competition, law never changes union viability or floors, and concentration is not a reading of the same network as competition.
> 2. A controlled rise in productivity with all contact terms held fixed moves the worker's share of the surplus in the same proportion.
> 3. Repeated measures show employer concentration falling while workers' outside options worsen, or the reverse, so the two are independent determinants.
> 4. A legal rule changing the claim is derived entirely inside the firm-worker frame as a new composition of the same atoms, with no import from a differing closure.
> 5. A figure line is exhibited that directly governs the empirical ranking.
>
> If any holds, I will retract the finding it touches. A one-line "this fails because ..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.

## Stage order

1. **Stage 1:** send the shape claim and the refuter. Ask what the recipient would count as a failure. Record their verdict before showing any theory.
2. **Stage 2, opt-in only:** if they are curious, offer the three local links. Their later agreement is never pooled as corroboration (Fig 2 :40).
3. **Stage 3:** offer the formula as a method, with the instruction that each user names a ground and writes a refuter before running it.
