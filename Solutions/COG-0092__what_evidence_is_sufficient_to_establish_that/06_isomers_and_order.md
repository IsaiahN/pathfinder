## RESIDUAL

`01` left three things underdeveloped: (1) why the sources of a difference form a union rather than a partition, (2) what the dependency order of the checks is, and (3) what "same score, different meaning" is in the framework's own vocabulary. This file settles those with figure lines, and states a falsifier.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (compact)

Atoms held: one reading `Δ`, three candidate sources G (world), M (channel), P (habitat), plus the item responses as the fixed set for the isomer question. Bonds held: the seven, notably `+` conjunction, `≡` identity, `→` sequence and `⋛` comparison. No new primitive is available without import.

## Q1 — Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :4-5: *"Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."*
- Fig 9 :54-57: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*
- Fig 9 :57-58: what remains *"stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound"*.

The G/M/P sources overlap. A back-translation fixes a pure-M case; a larger, enumerated sample fixes a pure-P case; neither fixes the compound in which a translated item is also answered under a different response norm. The correct operation is to split and then name the compound, not to search harder for one cause and not to average the causes into percentages.

**Falsifier Q1:** one lever that clears every G/M/P intersection with a stateable failure condition. If found, union is refuted here and one-loop search is correct.

## Q2 — What is the order of the checks?

- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
- Fig 3 :22-23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can. Which is why a reading taken below the break is not a weak reading. It is a reading of nothing."*

Order: bind the sought shape (formula Direction) -> D1 translation and D3 habitat -> D2 channel invariance -> D4 substantive comparison -> D5 verification alongside. A group-mean comparison published before translation and habitat checks is not a weak version of the source claim; with respect to source it is no reading at all.

Fig 9 :43: *"The verdict tells you to leave. The lookup tells you where to go. Elimination is what you do after you arrive."* The verdict here is "source unassigned"; the lookup is the habitat and seam enumeration; elimination is the per-item check after arrival, and Fig 9 :42 says *"narrowing gets you close, the ground decides"*.

**Falsifier Q2:** show that a source assignment survives when the upstream checks are removed, i.e., that the pooled comparison alone returns the same source with the same failure conditions. Then the order is decorative.

## Q3 — Are two groups with the same score but different item meaning isomers?

Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."* Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."* Fig 12 :95-96: *"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."* Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Hold the atom set fixed at the item responses `{item1 ... itemk}` scored under a common metric. Two groups can present the same observed score vector while the bonds between item and construct differ (different loadings, different intercepts, different item meaning). Under Fig 12 they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The score is the flat formula that records ingredients and not arrangement (Fig 12 :14-17: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not"*).

Where the response sets are *not* the same (one group answered a different item set, or a different mode), the fixed-atom condition fails and the pair is not an isomer pair; it is a comparison across different molecules, and the remedy is Fig 6's import (a new item or a new instrument), not a re-reading of the same score.

This resolves the apparent conflict with Putnick and Bornstein 2016: *"different groups can apply different meanings to the same cognition or behavior"*. That is a bond difference over a shared response set, exactly the isomer case, not a contradiction of Fig 12.

**Falsifier Q3:** exhibit a case with identical item responses and identical bonds whose behaviours diverge, or a case with different item sets still correctly called isomeric under Fig 12. Either breaks the fixed-atom reading.

## Q4 — Comparison is not identity

Fig 12 :66-69 defines identity: *"two names, one referent"*, with the test *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."* A translated item is often treated as `≡` (two names, one referent), but the round trip in Fig 4 :30 can show that something is lost, which is the test's negative result. The correct relation is not identity; it is a candidate binding whose semantic gap must be measured. Fig 12 :92: *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."* Identity is symmetric, so a failed substitution test means the two items are not one term.

Fig 9 :30-31: *"your words are borrowed too, so describe the effect rather than the cause"*. This is why the packet asks what an observer would expect to see if the item meant the same thing, rather than asking them to accept the translation's identity claim.

**Falsifier Q4:** a round trip with zero measured gap plus a substitution test in which nothing is lost for the specific item. Then `≡` is licensed for that item and the warning does not apply there.

## Q5 — Two currencies, and why "sufficient" cannot be a single number

Fig 12 :125-126: *"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."* *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."* A statistical invariance model prices the description; a round trip, a cognitive interview, an external criterion and an independent method price the finding-out. Fig 12 :129-130: *"Neither figure predicts the other, so both are read or the choice is made blind"* and *"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."*

This is the structural reason there is no scalar "amount of sufficient evidence". Sufficiency is a conjunction of two readings in different units, held apart, plus a ground verdict. Any single index that merges fit statistics and discriminating actions is the named error.

**Falsifier Q5:** a figure line or a formal result showing the two currencies are commensurable for this question, i.e., that one can be converted into the other without loss. Then a single sufficiency index would be legitimate.

## CONCLUSION

The sources of `Δ` are a union of overlapping conditions with a dependency order that runs translation and habitat before channel before comparison. Two groups with the same response set but different item-meaning bonds are isomers under Fig 12, with the fixed-atom condition stated openly. Translation is a candidate identity whose substitution test can fail. "Sufficient" cannot be one number because description cost and action cost do not add. Four falsifiers are written above, before any conclusion was drawn.
