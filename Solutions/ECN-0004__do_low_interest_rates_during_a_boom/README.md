# Solution ECN-0004: low rates during a boom, malinvestment or demand shortfall

**Question:** Do low interest rates during a boom create harmful investment "malinvestments," or are recessions better explained by demand shortfalls and financial shocks?

## The question restated as an effect

Two candidate descriptions of a recession are offered as rivals:

- **Option A (Austrian, malinvestment).** Low policy rates during a boom distort the intertemporal price signal, cause projects that would not have been undertaken at the saving-determined rate, and the bust is the liquidation of those specific wrong projects.
- **Option B (demand shortfall and financial shock).** Recessions are better explained by a shortfall of aggregate spending and by disruptions in credit and balance sheets, with the boom's low rates being either an effect of the same conditions or a contributor among many.

What is being decided is not a single fact but an attribution. Option A leads to a per-project, intertemporal diagnosis and to a policy of allowing liquidation. Option B leads to an aggregate, channel-and-anchor diagnosis and to a policy of stabilizing spending and credit. The two options carry different anchors, different currencies, and different slots, so the first task is to find out whether they are answers to one question at all.

What an answer would look like, written down and bound as an ordinary term before the run (formula step "Direction"): a verdict that says, for a named episode and a named project slot, which condition was load-bearing, what non-updating ground settled it, and what would have had to be true for the other verdict to win. A general verdict that one school is right about all recessions is *not* an admissible answer shape under the figure census, because it aggregates across slots (Fig 1 :19, :22) and flattens a union into a partition (Fig 9 :54-58).

**Residual in effect terms:** the unexplained clustered failure of particular investments following a period of low rates and apparent prosperity, where the comparator needed to classify a project as wrong (the rate that would have prevailed without the policy) is not observed, and where the aggregate reading of the same episode is taken from a different instrument. The residual is the gap between what each frame predicts and what the ground returns, per slot.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with either school.
- No bulk-send plan.

Three reasons in the figures' own words:

1. **A room does not supply an anchor.** Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." A survey of economists, however large, is a reading of a shared evidence pool, not a measurement of the residual.
2. **Selecting for receptiveness manufactures the disqualifying correlation.** Fig 2 :40: "disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool". Fig 2 :42: "Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."
3. **Sending the theory before the check spends the reader's independence.** Fig 10 :52: "Bring a characterised residual, never the source." Fig 10 :42: "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."

Legally: scraping personal emails for unsolicited bulk outreach breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules in many jurisdictions. The CSV uses institutional submission and contact pages only.
