# The Tether's answer: weak interactions, strong interactions, ecological robustness

**Mode: `specified` on the ecological claims, `general` on the structural metalaws. Not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a measurement. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 6), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

The question asks whether a web becomes more robust when interactions are weak, or whether strong interactions can be necessary. Stated as an effect: for which species, links and loops does R = |Γ(b,a) - o'| stay small when a perturbation arrives, and which arrangements of strengths and signs keep it there? R is indexed per slot, never averaged (Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."). The anchor is a perturbation experiment (Fig 2 :29 "A measurement, an experiment, a counter, a market, a patient outcome."), not an eigenvalue computed from an assumed matrix.

## 2. The shape

1. **It is a union, not a binary.** Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." The two conditions are C1, many weak links that damp oscillations and lower loop weight, and C2, a few strong links that are load-bearing and, in tightly coupled predator-prey pairs, can make a large complex web stable at all. They are not degrees of one failure; a web failing only C1 and a web failing only C2 need different instruments.

2. **Strength does not carry valence; the residual does.** Fig 11 :49 "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries." The same strong link stabilises one perturbation and destabilises another. The question's "or" assumes a property of strength that the figures deny. What decides is the sign and placement of the loop and which residual is being defended.

3. **An all-weak web is the flat case.** Fig 12 :101 "A bond that costs nothing is a bond that carries nothing, which is the flat case." If every strength goes to zero there is no coupling and no network to call robust. "Weaker is always better" fails at its own limit. The observed pattern is many weak interactions and a few strong ones, not all-weak.

4. **Loop sign and placement decide closure.** Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Weak links matter because of where they sit in a long loop, not merely because they are small. The control-theory import corroborates this from outside ecology: weak loop gain stabilises already-stable components, while large loop gain can be needed to stabilise an unstable one. Both poles can be necessary.

5. **A few strong links can be load-bearing.** Fig 13 :48 "And the set is minimal: remove one and something stops." Remove or weaken such a link and a function stops. This is C2, and it is why uniform weakening is not a safe general intervention.

6. **Average scores destroy the diagnostic.** Fig 10 :26 "stale percepts, dropped transients, saturated metrics, pooled readings" names the failure mode. A single web-level robustness number is a pooled reading; the useful question is which named slot is losing contact.

7. **The specific rule for a named web is a `gap`.** Fig 9 :32 "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself." The framework returns the shape above and stops. It does not return a universal strength threshold, a best connectance value, or a ranking of links by goodness.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that weak interactions are always stabilising. McCann et al. (1998) never claimed all-weak; their own data summary is "many weak interactions and a few strong interactions".
- Not that strong interactions are always destabilising. Allesina and Tang (2012) find predator-prey pairs can be stabilising when tightly coupled.
- Not that a leading eigenvalue from an assumed interaction matrix is the ground. It is a frame-internal score, a filter for which experiment to run (Fig 1 :27).
- Not a resolution of the diversity-stability debate. Diversity and interaction strength are different residuals, and the union here is about strength arrangement and loop sign.
- Not a universal management prescription. Weakening every link can remove load-bearing coupling; strengthening every link can destabilise a loop.
- Not `grounded`. No perturbation measurement pays for any of the seven points.

## 4. What would settle it: the ground

A per-slot perturbation experiment, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*, an instrument that reads green "cannot comment on the source"; the spectrometer resolved it. Here the corresponding questions are:

- **Discriminator:** an instrument that measures per-capita interaction strength in situ at the loop scale, so that a persistence reading can be attributed to a source rather than named from the projection.
- **C1 test:** does weakening the weak links in long loops reduce measured oscillation amplitude and return time as predicted?
- **C2 test:** does weakening the few strongest links remove a measured function, as predicted by the load-bearing claim?
- **Sign test:** does reversing one link's sign at fixed absolute loop gain change loop closure, as Fig 12 :121 requires?
- **Standing falsifiers from `01`:** nine are pre-registered there, including uniform rescaling that removes the union, invariant valence across a change of residual, a regulated all-zero web, and a model metric that predicts extinction at a named slot without calibration.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** The six substrate terms are a guess at compression axes, not a discovery of nature's own list; declaring them makes the choice visible, not neutral. The union shape stops where the available instruments can no longer distinguish damping from load-bearing in a given web, and the lineage is recorded so the stop is checkable. Fig 8 :39 "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence." Fig 13 :134 "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,". What is open is not a hidden answer but a missing discriminator.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not pick a side in the weak-versus-strong debate, because the "or" is the wrong shape. It says the question is a union of two conditions: many weak links damp consumer-resource oscillations and lower the weight of long loops, while a few strong links are load-bearing and, when predator-prey pairs are tightly coupled, can be what makes a complex web persist. Strength carries no valence on its own, the same link can stabilise one perturbation and destabilise another, and an all-weak web is the flat case with no coupling at all. What decides is the sign and placement of the loop, measured per species and link against a real perturbation, not a single web-level score or an eigenvalue from an assumed matrix. The framework returns that shape and its falsifiers, then stops. Which strengths stabilise your web is something only the ground can name.
