# Two routes to one substance, and the order of the evidence

Supporting derivation for ORI-0099. **Mode: `general` on the structural claims, `specified` on the trace-class prediction.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks what could distinguish route A (origin elsewhere, arrival here) from route B (Earth-based origin) when both produce the same present artefact. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) are the two routes an isomer pair, an identical substance under different names, or something else under Fig 12's definitions; and (2) in what dependency order must the candidate evidence be read, so that a filter is never mistaken for a witness.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 Are the two routes isomers? NO, they are different routes to a functionally identical substance

Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance."

Two readings are possible and must be separated.

- **Wrong reading:** treat "arrival" and "in-situ origin" as two isomers over a shared set of biological atoms, so that their difference is a bond rearrangement readable in the artefact. This fails. As the record notes for the QMGR case, isomerism requires a shared atom set with different bonds. Here the two hypotheses share the *outcome*, not a composed molecule with a different internal arrangement. There is no pair of molecules whose ingredient lists match and whose bonds differ in a way that maps onto "arrived" versus "originated here." Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond." The artefact's notation does not record the route bond at all.
- **Correct reading:** Fig 13 :121 is about routes, not isomers. Two chains built from different links may compose functionally identical terms. The biosphere under A and under B is functionally identical in the relevant reading. So "arrival" and "in-situ origin" are two histories of one substance, and the distinction lives in the lineage, not in the molecule. Fig 13 :128-129: "the order is not recoverable from the artefact. It is recoverable from the lineage."

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a molecular character were found whose bond arrangement differs systematically between the two routes and is readable in the artefact, the two would be isomers in the strict sense and the lineage requirement would be weakened. No candidate in the opened sources meets this; each proposed signature depends on a historical record or an external comparison, not on an internal bond difference.

### Q2 Identity is a symmetric bond, so "same biochemistry" cannot carry order

Operators :24-27: identity (≡) means "two names, one referent," symmetric, with the test "substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."

Earth life's biochemistry under A and under B is the same referent under two names. Substituting one origin story for the other loses nothing in the reading, which is the test for identity. That is why a shared genetic code is not evidence for arrival: "substitute one for the other, is anything lost? No" (Fig 12 :69). Conversely a route discriminator must use an **ordered** bond: sequence (→), subtraction (−), or comparison (⋛). A trace is ordered in time (came before or after arrival), a missing condition is a subtraction ("what you failed to reproduce"), and a planetary comparison is a threshold relation between two quantities (biosignature here versus biosignature there). The shape of the answer is therefore an ordered relation, not a symmetric one.

**Falsifier:** show that a symmetric bond can carry route information, which would contradict the Operators table's symmetry test. Not attempted.

### Q3 Subtraction and negation are not the same move

Fig 11 :28-30 names "what you failed to reproduce" as a silent failure. That is a subtraction (−): "A with B removed or absent" (Operators :43). It is not a negation (¬). Operators :73-74: "− is A with B removed or absent, which is the absence of an operand. Negation is the refusal of a claim. A vocabulary with − and without ¬ can say what is missing from an arrangement and cannot say that an arrangement is wrong."

This matters for the question. A missing metabolic capability in a putative immigrant is a subtraction; it narrows a trace class but does not by itself refuse an origin claim. To refuse "life originated here" we need negation, a settled term that says the claim is false, and that requires the ground to pay for it. Fig 5 :54-55: "A term that refuses another pays the same bargain... What it removes is not a term but a term's standing to be composed over." The held term "same origin" must remain refusable: "a vocabulary without it can only accumulate" (Operators :58).

**Falsifier:** a derivation that establishes an Earth origin from the absence of a trace without a settled refusal. That would be an accumulation, not a verdict, and this file would be wrong to allow it. It is not allowed.

### Q4 The dependency order of the evidence

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance." Ordered as follows, with the bond that governs each step:

| step | what it establishes | bond | what would refute the step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read the artefact: what is actually observed, per character slot | none (perception) | a character read only through an aggregate score (Fig 1 :22) |
| 2 | State what counts as progress: a route-dependent reading | ⋛ comparison between A and B values | an objective stated as "more evidence for panspermia" |
| 3 | Enumerate the habitat of the residual from the world | none (enumeration, Fig 11 :43) | a discriminator chosen before enumeration (Fig 9 :27-28) |
| 4 | Split arrival modes: unmaintained versus maintained | ¬ (refusal of the conflation) | a single mode covering both (F3) |
| 5 | Read trace classes: what failed to reproduce, what was brought | − and → | a trace class with no readable residue (F2) |
| 6 | Ground the character with a source-discriminating instrument or lineage | ground, not a bond | an instrument sharing the artefact's evidence pool (F5) |
| 7 | Only then compose a verdict over the settled characters | → sequence | a character settled by agreement rather than by the ground |

Effort at step 5 without step 3 is the failure Fig 3 :20 names: "effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it." Most published transport work is at step 3's measurements; the break is at steps 2 and 4.

**Falsifier for the order:** a published route determination that skips step 3 or step 6 and is nevertheless settled by the ground. Then the order is a preference, not a dependency, and this derivation is retracted.

### Q5 The round trip on this derivation

Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."

Sent up (x): Fig 12 :117 (isomer definition), Fig 11 :28-30 and :66-70 (substitution, two failure modes), Fig 13 :121 and :128-129 (routes, lineage), Operators :43/:58 (subtraction and negation).
Abstracted (T_A): "two routes give one substance; look at the lineage, not the molecule; a missing trace is not a refusal."
Concretised (T_E): "look for isotope ratios, chirality, a genome message, or adjacent biosignatures."

The gap is nonzero. The abstraction loses (i) the distinction between subtraction and negation, so a missing trace becomes a verdict; (ii) the unmaintained versus maintained arrival split, so two different predictions collapse to one; (iii) the per-slot requirement, so candidates become an aggregate score. The honest form is a lossy condensation with a measured gap, recorded qualitatively in `11_audit.md`.

## CONCLUSION

The two routes are not isomers in Fig 12's strict sense; they are different routes to a functionally identical substance (Fig 13 :121), so the difference is carried by the lineage, not the artefact (Fig 13 :128-129). Symmetry analysis shows why "same biochemistry" has zero verdict weight: identity is symmetric and loses nothing under substitution. The admissible discriminators use ordered bonds: subtraction for "what failed to reproduce," sequence for "what was brought and when," comparison for adjacent instances, and negation only when a term is refused by the ground. The evidence must be read in dependency order, with the objective and arrival-mode split as the likely broken middle links. All claims are `general` or `specified`, none `grounded`.
