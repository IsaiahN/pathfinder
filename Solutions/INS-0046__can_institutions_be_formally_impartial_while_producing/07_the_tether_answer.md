# The Tether's answer: formal impartiality and unequal outcomes

**Mode: `specified` on the structure, `general` on the shape. Not `grounded`.** No institution was measured. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

The question puts a procedure's self-description and a distribution's measurement into one frame: "can an institution be impartial in form while unequal in effect?" The failure is a census failure. Three quantities are present, not two: a symmetric rule (D1), an asymmetric habitat that delivers cases with unequal starting positions (D2), and a per-slot outcome distribution (D3). Fig 9 :5 "are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search." Fig 8 :53 "Residual first, frame second."

## 2. The answer

1. **Yes, the two properties can co-exist, because they do not range against the same anchor.** "Formally impartial" is a convention about the form of a decision rule: swap the group label and the decision is unchanged. It is a symmetric bond in the notation (Fig 12 :59 "swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."). "Systematically unequal" is an ordered comparison of measured group-level quantities (Fig 12 :85 "a threshold relation between two quantities"). One is a swap test; the other is a magnitude comparison.

2. **A declared impartiality is not a ground fact.** Fig 10 :18 "a declared meaning is not a grounded one"; Fig 10 :49 "Declaring a meaning does not ground it." The contradiction in the question appears only when the convention is promoted to a verdict about the distribution, at which point Fig 10 :47 "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one" applies and the claim was never a convention anymore.

3. **The habitat is the third term that makes the co-existence mechanical.** Fig 11 :40 "An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves." Making a rule blind to group labels changes the procedure in isolation. If the same actors still arrive with the same starting positions, the distribution does not move. Fig 11 :39 "capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone." Fig 11 :61 "Introduction is available; exclusion is not."

4. **A global fairness score hides the live slot.** Fig 1 :13 "a global R near zero with one live slot residual is a legal state"; Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."; Fig 1 :22 "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average." An institution reporting near-zero unfairness while one group's slot carries a live residual is not anomalous under Fig 1; it is the expected result of averaging.

5. **The institution cannot score itself.** Fig 1 :27 "They are frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces." The outcome distribution, measured by an outside counter, is the ground. The legal framing "discrimination" is a binding from another frame, legitimate only with provenance (Fig 8 :50 "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them.").

6. **The source needs an instrument, not an argument.** *The Hard Problem is About Source*: "If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information (it cannot define consciousness in full)." A disparity reading does not identify whether the source is the rule, the habitat or a confounder. Audit studies and confounder-controlled statistics are the discriminating instruments.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that any named institution is unjust. No institution was measured.
- Not that unequal outcomes are always caused by bias, or that formal impartiality is worthless. Those are ground and value questions.
- Not a normative "ought". No figure line governs it (declared `gap` in `06` Q6).
- Not `grounded`. No measurement settles `left(R,φ)` here.
- Not that a single fairness score proves impartiality. Fig 1 :22 says the opposite.
- Not an isomer claim between the rule and the distribution as posed (they share no atom set; see `06` Q3).
- Not a ranking of institutions or people by likely agreement.

## 4. What would settle it - the ground

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument. The corresponding questions are:

- **Per-slot test:** enumerate who reaches the rule, with what starting positions, and measure the outcome residual per group. If every slot is at or near zero, the inequality claim has no live residual.
- **Contact test:** change only the habitat contact while holding the rule's form fixed. If the distribution moves, F5 is confirmed; if it does not, the habitat is not the source here.
- **Form test:** change only the rule's form while holding contact fixed. If the distribution moves substantially, Fig 11 :40 is refuted for this domain and the shape must be re-censused.
- **Anchor test:** push on the outcomes and see whether the institution's impartiality rating moves. If it does, it was never an anchor (Fig 2 :29-30).
- **Standing refuters** from `01` section 5: any one of the five fires, the shape is withdrawn or re-censused.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** The three-term split is a structural result about what can be composed. Whether a specific institution's disparity comes from its rule or its habitat is below the union horizon until an instrument is applied. The stop is recorded so a later reader can locate the error: "Indiscernibility is not identity" (canonical figures README). A later ground reading, not agreement, is what moves this to `grounded`.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not say that an institution with an impartial rule cannot produce unequal outcomes. It says those are two different quantities with two different anchors, and that a third thing, the habitat of people who reach the rule with unequal starting positions, connects them. A formal rule is a symmetric convention: swap the group label and the decision should not change. An outcome distribution is a measured, ordered comparison across groups. A convention that does not change contact does not change the distribution, and a single fairness score near zero can sit beside one group's live residual without any contradiction. The contradiction appears only when the institution uses its impartial rule as proof that its outcomes are fair. At that point it has turned a convention into a verdict. What the framework offers is the split, the per-slot instrument list, and four refuters written in advance. It does not name a guilty institution, and it does not decide the normative question of what an institution ought to do about the gap.
