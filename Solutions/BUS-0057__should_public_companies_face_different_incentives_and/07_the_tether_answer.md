# The Tether's answer on public versus private-equity-owned versus founder-controlled firms

**Mode: `specified` on the design shape, `general` on the structural laws, `gap` on the specific rule content. Not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a result. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 6), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

The question asks whether public companies should face different incentives and obligations from PE-owned or founder-controlled firms. Restated as an effect: why does the gap between what a governance regime predicts and what its ground returns differ by ownership slot, and what design rule should bind incentives and obligations to each slot? R is indexed per form and per firm, never aggregated (Fig 1 :19). The anchor is realized outcomes per form, named in Fig 2 :29, not governance ratings or internal metrics.

The question is a **union compound**, not one hard problem (Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones"*). Incentives and obligations are two conditions; the three forms differ in several conditions at once; and each form has both a contact condition and a reach condition. A case in two conditions is not a harder case of either (Fig 9 :57).

## 2. The shape

1. **Index the residual per ownership form, never a sector average.** Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."* A single "governance quality" score pooled across forms throws away the signal that says which form is losing contact.

2. **Keep one governance kernel; re-bind actors, currency and anchor per form.** Fig 4 :19-20: *"The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked, whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."* The loop is the same in a public firm, a PE-backed firm and a founder firm. What changes is the cast (dispersed shareholders, sponsor, founder), the currency (market return, fund exit, long-horizon control value) and the anchor (which outcome settles the residual in that form). So the answer is neither "one identical rule for all" nor "a different kind of governance for each": it is one kernel, re-bound per slot.

3. **The three forms are isomers, not points on a quality scale.** Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* They share capital, managers, workers, boards and assets, and differ in the ownership bond. That is why the same formal rule can leave a categorically different residual in each form. Fig 11 :51: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."* Concentrated ownership is a benefactor when the residual is shirking and a malfactor when it is minority expropriation.

4. **Unbundle before choosing a lever.** Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Separate incentives from obligations, separate the three forms, and separate contact failure from reach failure. A single lever, "same code for all" or "bespoke code for each", clears only the region where one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing.

5. **Keep the convention a convention.** Incentives and obligations are authored by the regulator seat (Fig 10 :45-46), but *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"* (Fig 10 :47). When an obligation becomes a target firms optimize, it stops being checkable against a ground. This is the figure-grounded form of Goodhart's law (verified as a low-overlap world source).

6. **Keep the channel to the ground reachable and renew reach from differing closures.** Fig 10 :24: *"the ground does not decay, the channel does"*; Fig 6 :23: *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."* A governance regime stays effective by keeping outcome readings uncorrupted and by importing from frames whose closures differ, not by composing harder inside one ownership form.

7. **Stop at the shape.** Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."* The framework does not name which concrete incentive or obligation belongs to which form. That is a `gap`, not a silence: the source-discriminating instrument that would settle it is not held.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not a `grounded` answer. No measurement here settles which form should be treated differently, or how.
- Not a ranking of public, PE or founder firms. The figures refuse a standing list of good actors (Fig 11 :51).
- Not "one uniform code is always right" and not "every form needs a wholly different code". Both are names where the figures return a shape.
- Not a specific policy: no board-composition rule, no disclosure duty, no incentive scheme is named.
- Not a theory of everything about corporate law. The regulation question stays a compound with its own loop.
- Not a claim that the world sources corroborate the verdict independently. They are one governance evidence pool plus two low-overlap witnesses for the mechanism shape, and agreement from a shared pool is one witness (Fig 2 :40).

## 4. What would settle it - the ground

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: an instrument that reads green *"cannot comment on the source"*; the spectrometer resolved it. Here:

- **Per-form residual measurement:** measure R = |Γ(b,a) - o'| separately for public, PE and founder firms on the same governance choice, with outcomes that cannot be gamed by the choice itself. If the residuals are identical after controlling for habitat, ownership form carries no live slot mass and the shape fails at F1 and F2.
- **Valence flip test:** find a documented change of residual in which a governance capability changes sign from benefactor to malfactor. If none can be found, F3 weakens to `general`.
- **Convention corruption test:** check whether a governance metric retains an unchanged relationship to an independently measured ground after being made a reward target. Under F8 it should not.
- **Unbundling test:** apply one lever to both conditions and all three forms and see whether a categorically different residue remains. If none remains, F6 is wrong.
- **Standing pre-registered falsifiers:** the eight in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` section 4.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** The governance vocabulary is *"a human's careful guess"*, and declaring it makes the choice visible, not neutral. Another institutional frame need not share the three-form taxonomy. The answer stops at the limit of the available instruments and records the lineage so the stop is checkable. *"Indiscernibility is not identity."* Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence"*, and Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."*

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not pick a winning ownership form and does not name a specific governance rule. It says that the governance residual must be measured per ownership form and per firm, that one kernel runs across public, private-equity-owned and founder-controlled firms with only the actors, currency and anchor changing, that the three forms are isomers (same atoms, different ownership bond, different substance), and that incentives and obligations form a union that must be unbundled before any lever is chosen. It adds that governance rules are conventions that stop being checkable when they become targets, and that the channel to the ground must be kept reachable. What it offers is the shape any answer must have, falsifiers written in advance, and the instruments that would close it. The specific rule for each form stays open until the ground settles it.
