## Q1 - Are two startups with the same resources isomers?

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :124)
> **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."** (Fig 12 :5)

Two startups holding an identical list of assets, cash and advisors have the same atoms. If one composes those atoms as a repeated pitch sequence and the other as a reconfigurable network of customer contacts, they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with the difference invisible in the resource list. This is the figure-grounded version of "it is not what you have, it is what you do with it", but stronger: the arrangement is itself a substance and carries information the ingredients do not.

If the startups share only a pattern (both use `support → routine → market`) while differing in which atoms occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: they are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124). The pattern fixes the kind, not the outcome. This is what a programme catalogue records when it lists duration, cohort size and sponsorship but not the bonds at work inside each startup.

The distinction is testable. A resource-list comparison that finds no difference between two startups cannot distinguish isomer from functional group; the bond has to be recorded. Hallen et al.'s *"broad, intensive, and paced consultation"* is a bond description (which contacts, when, in what order), not an atom description (how much money, how many mentors). Cohen, Bingham and Hallen's three design choices, spacing versus concentrating consultation, privacy versus transparency between peers, and tailoring versus standardising, are all bonds, and their results show that the same ingredients under different bonds perform differently.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two startups with identical atoms and identical recorded bonds nevertheless diverge in market outcome with no difference in habitat contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D2), which moves the explanation out of D3.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated for the legality of the reading (Fig 12 and Fig 11 :50, different figures), single-source for the business application. The world corroboration is the accelerator design studies, which are the same topical evidence pool as the rest of the support literature.

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20-21)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)

Order for a startup facing a changed market:

1. **Perception.** Can it read the customer's response at all? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests.
2. **Vocabulary.** Does Γ have terms for what the changed situation affords, and can it say what would count as progress there? This is the middle link. Fig 3 :26: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."*
3. **Objective.** Can it state a comparison that would count as getting somewhere? Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot state a goal.
4. **Planning.** A route to that objective under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next startup and the next programme.

The three conditions map onto this chain:

- **C1 (contact)** is mostly a failure of links 1 and 2. The readings are stale, saturated or pooled into a cohort average.
- **C2 (reach)** is mostly a failure of link 5: the startup names what worked but never imports a new atom from a closure that differs.
- **C3 (unauthored verification)** is a failure of the measurement of links 1 through 5: the comparison pool was chosen by a participant, so the correlation is manufactured before the outcome is read.

That mapping is why a single lever cannot clear all three (Fig 9 :55). A cheque, a deck review and a randomized comparison are three different instruments at three different links.

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* The market-facing link (1) has revenue and retention data; link 5 has almost none. So budget piles up at links 1 and 4 while the break sits at 2 and 5. Cohen, Bingham and Hallen's finding that founders *"systematically satisfice prematurely"* and need to *"reignit[e] search for problems that they already view as solved"* is a report from link 2 and link 5.

**Falsifier:** a startup whose chain demonstrably works at links 1, 2 and 3 and fails only at link 4. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim of F7 in the primary audit, though not the dependency ordering.

## Q3 - What is the valve? Catalyst versus spent part

> **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** (Fig 12 :119)
> **"An actor’s valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."** (Fig 11 :49)
> **"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."** (Fig 11 :51)

A mentor who regenerates across cohorts is a catalyst; a mentor whose advice was spent on one business model and is then replayed is a spent part. The programme's own catalogue does not record which. The meta-analysis reports that accelerators *"are most effective when tailored to the specific needs of ventures"* and that benefits are larger for *"smaller start-ups with high growth potential"*. That is valence relative to the residual: the same input pays in one slot and not another.

Innovation Growth Lab's structured-accountability trial supplies the sharper instance. The treatment increased commitment and perceived support, but the quantitative result was that *"these types of supervisory structures are not a good use of time and resources for new venture performance."* Supervision is a catalyst in the sentiment reading and a spent part in the performance reading, and nothing in the programme description recorded which it would be.

**Falsifier:** a support input whose measured valence is invariant across a documented change of the startup's residual. If no such change of valence can be found, the valve claim weakens to `general`.

## Q4 - Competing explanations, and what each can and cannot reach

| candidate explanation | what it names | figure-line bearing | what it cannot reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Resource-based view** (Barney 1991, quoted inside the meta-analysis: valuable, rare, inimitable, non-substitutable resources) | the startup's Γ, its library of hard-to-copy atoms | Γ is a real operand; Fig 6 :21 says minting cannot add an atom | Why the same Γ pays in one habitat and not another. No contact variable, so it conflicts with Fig 11 :40 until re-mapped as a closure |
| **Learning / dynamic capabilities** (Teece-style reconfiguration; Cohen et al.'s consultation) | the operator that renews reach | close to C2 and Fig 6 :23 | It is a binding from the entrepreneurship frame, not a derivation. It does not say what would refute it, and it does not separate C1 from C3 |
| **Sorting and signaling** (Hallen et al.; the meta-analysis's selection bias) | the authored cohort and the signal it sends | Fig 10 :73-75, Fig 2 :40, Seat Map :163 | It explains the correlation but not the residual movement; it is not a causal account of the supported slot |
| **Deadweight / picking winners** (Lenihan 2004; Irish and UK evaluation guidance) | the zero-support bin | Fig 5 :21, :29-30 | It detects the failure and cannot name which input would restore contact; that is a `gap` at the instrument boundary |
| **Programme design** (duration, cohort size, sponsorship, tailoring; meta-analysis moderators) | bonds and arrangements inside D3 | Fig 12 :117, :124; arrangement is the substance | A catalogue of atoms and moderators cannot name the bond that moved a particular slot; it records half of itself |
| **Ecosystem / habitat structure** (Stam, cited in the meta-analysis) | D2 conditions and relations | Fig 11 :46, :73; habitat updates, ground settles | It is the habitat, not the ground; it cannot by itself say what pays per slot |
| **Luck / information asymmetry** | an unearned early reading that persists while unfuted | Fig 8 :40: *"Underivable-in-principle and unreachable-at-this-budget are different facts"* | Cannot be excluded from inside a frame; Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* Honest `gap`, not a rival verdict |
| **Causal inference / average treatment effect** (Imbens and Rubin; Holland) | the population-level estimate | Fig 13 :134-135; Fig 9 :60 | It cannot settle a single startup's counterfactual; the average is a filter, not a witness |

No candidate is discarded by prestige or familiarity. Each is ranged against the same residual, in the order Fig 8 :53 requires. The point is not to pick one; it is to notice that the first six are all in one topical evidence pool (Fig 2 :31) and that the two low-overlap fields, causal inference and ecology, corroborate the method and the mechanism shape, not the startup-level fact.

## Q5 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- the resource-based emphasis on things hard to copy, as the Γ operand under a closure that has not met a differing closure;
- the learning and dynamic-capabilities emphasis on reconfiguration, as C2 and Fig 6 :23;
- the sorting and signaling emphasis, as C3 and Fig 10 :73-75;
- the deadweight emphasis on what would have happened anyway, as F1 and Fig 5 :29-30;
- the programme-design emphasis on duration and tailoring, as Fig 12 :117 and :124;
- the ecosystem emphasis on habitat, as D2.

It cannot generate the *name* of the specific startup or input that will work next. That is the `gap` recorded in F10 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 (*"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*) and Fig 8 :39 apply. The source essay states why: an instrument that reads a result *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"the answer is unreached until a better instrument exists"*.

## CONCLUSION

Two startups with the same atoms but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117; two startups sharing only a `support → routine → market` pattern are members of one functional group under Fig 12 :124. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the middle link (vocabulary and objective) and the carry link the usual breaks, and with verification as a separate failure of the comparison pool. Support inputs are catalysts or spent parts depending on the residual, and nothing in the programme catalogue records which. The standard business explanations are mostly bindings of one topical pool and are generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim has a written falsifier; the order and valve claims have one; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
