**Question:** Should governments and central banks target a low positive inflation rate, zero inflation, or a different goal?

**Source:** economics questions.txt line 27

**Mode:** `general` on the structural shape, `specified` on the conditional predictions about which target fits which residual. **Status:** done

**Registry status:** pending

## What is being decided, in effect terms

Three options are on the table. A low positive inflation target (2 percent, the current majority convention) leaves a positive gradient in the price level and headroom for nominal rate cuts. A zero inflation target removes that gradient. A different goal (a price-level path, a nominal-income path, an average-inflation rule) changes which quantity is compared and whether past misses are made up. What must be decided is not the number by itself but the shape a target has to have for the ground to settle it: which observable is the right slot, whether the target is an anchor or a convention, and what residual each option is fitted to.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with a target.
- No bulk-send plan.

Three reasons, in the figures' own words:

1. **Consensus is not evidence.** Fig 2 :41 says *"efficient for a convention: agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it"*, while Fig 2 :40 says *"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"*. The near-universal 2 percent target is a convention, so it cannot be cited as proof that 2 percent is correct.
2. **A verifier must not hold the interior.** Fig 10 :42: *"A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it."* The first contact carries the residual and its refuter, never the framework.
3. **Selecting for receptiveness manufactures the disqualifying correlation.** Fig 2 :42: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."* The cost of a wrong monetary regime is large, so independence, not agreement, is what must be bought.

Legally: unsolicited bulk email breaches GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules in many jurisdictions. Public institutional submission and opt-in channels avoid that failure as well as the structural one.
