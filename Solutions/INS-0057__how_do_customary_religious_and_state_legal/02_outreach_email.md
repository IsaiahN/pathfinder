**Subject:** One checkable claim about plural legal orders, before any theory

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The residual.** Customary, religious, and state orders issue rules for the same conduct and the same people. The effect question is: when several normative frames range against one conduct-ground, what decides which rule governs a given case, what happens where their claims overlap, and what would have to change for the governing rule to change?
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`).** Four structural claims, each with a failure condition:
>
> 1. The anchor is the decision actually enforced and the behaviour actually changed, not any order's own account of its authority. A statute moves when a legislature acts, so it is a frame, not a ground.
> 2. Agreement between two orders is evidence only in proportion to their independence. Orders drawing on one shared moral premise are one witness with two names, not two witnesses.
> 3. The relation is a union, not a partition. A person can be under all three at once, and the intersections are the cases that matter.
> 4. Influence is a crossing with a provenance record. A rule migrating into another order moves that order's wall only if the closures differ and the rule adds something not already there. A convention stops being a convention the moment it decides an outcome.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** I am not ranking the three orders, not saying which one should govern, not claiming that a legal transplant always succeeds, and not claiming that any of this is settled by measurement. The figures return a shape, not a name. A ground reading, meaning court records, enforcement data, and ethnography, is what would name a winner in a particular place. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency, not a test of the claim.
>
> **What would refute it (written before this message).**
> 1. The enforced outcome changes when a frame merely restates its claim, with no change in conduct or enforcement.
> 2. Two orders with measured low overlap agree, yet their agreement predicts the enforced outcome no better than one order alone.
> 3. A convention demonstrably decides enforced outcomes while remaining checkable, non-authoritative, and refusing-able.
> 4. A jurisdiction shows a genuine partition of the field with empty intersections over a defined period.
>
> If any of these holds, I will scope or withdraw the relevant claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figures with line numbers (local extract, path in `10_source_ledger.csv`): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Figures README, canonical usage rule: https://github.com/IsaiahN/tether/blob/general-agent/figures/README.md
> - Legal pluralism topic guide, GSDRC, University of Birmingham: https://gsdrc.org/topic-guides/safety-security-and-justice/themes/legal-pluralism/
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a pluralism case you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and label the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
