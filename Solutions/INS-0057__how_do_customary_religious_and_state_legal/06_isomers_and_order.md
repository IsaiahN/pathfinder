# Union, dependency order, and isomers: supporting derivation for INS-0057

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The primary census says the three orders stand in a union and that their relation to an enforcement ground is the thing to explain. Two questions are owed: (1) what is the structure of that union, and what is the dependency order for investigating it; (2) are customary, religious, and state orders **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, or something weaker?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (carried in, not redone)

Atoms in play, read off the habitat rather than composed: a norm, an authority that issues it, a sanction that backs it, a membership that binds it, a record of decisions, and the enforcement ground. Bonds in play: identity (two names, one referent), sequence, production, comparison, and negation. No new primitive is available without an import from another frame, and none is needed.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Union or partition, and what kind of compound?

- Fig 9 :54 "When the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :57 "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- The GSDRC guide, independently: "hybrid or mixed legal environments where state, local and non-state actors are linked and the lines between them are blurred."

⇒ The residuum after unbundling is not a smaller mixture. It is a **specific compound**: the case in two or three conditions, where the second condition still binds after the first is cleared. A single lever (for example, recognising custom in statute) clears the one-condition region and leaves the intersection standing.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** a jurisdiction with a demonstrated empty intersection between all three orders over a defined period would scope this to a subset. Recorded as a scope condition, not smoothed away.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

Fig 3 lists five links: perception, vocabulary, objective, planning, learn and carry. Mapped to this question:

1. **Perception:** can we tell which rule is actually being applied to which slot, and distinguish it from the last case? Instrument: the enforcement record.
2. **Vocabulary:** do we have terms for what each order affords (what it can move, open, join, block)? Without these, "influence" is a vague noun.
3. **Objective:** can we say what would count as progress, in our own terms? Here: predicting the governing rule per slot, not aggregating a harmony score.
4. **Planning:** a route to that objective under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry:** name what worked, verify it, carry it to the next case.

Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not." Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at." Fig 3 :23 "A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can."

⇒ Most pluralism effort lands at link 4 (designing harmonisation schemes) while link 2 (terms for what an order affords) has no instrument. Effort spent above that break cannot arrive. This is a `specified` structural claim; measuring which link actually break in a given field needs a ground reading.

**Falsifier:** show a working instrument at link 2 already in routine use in legal pluralism research, with published per-slot affordance terms. The link-local claim would then need re-census.

### Q3 - Are the three orders isomers, or a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117 "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

As usually described, the three orders do **not** share all atoms. Customary order binds by community membership and custom; religious order by revealed or interpreted authority; state order by enacted rule and official sanction. Their atom sets differ, so under Fig 12's definition they are not isomers. They share a **functional group**: each is a frame with a library, a currency, and a record, and the qualifier (custom, religion, state) says which member.

The stronger form is testable: write all three over the full shared atom set {norm, authority, sanction, membership, record, ground}, differing only in which bond connects the norm to the authority and the sanction to the ground. Then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. That is the claim worth taking to an expert.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one of the three cannot be written without an atom that the other two require (for example, a religious order with no membership relation, or a state order with no record), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead. Triangulated for the definition (Fig 12 plus the Operators table); unmeasured for the physics of any actual legal system.

### Q4 - What is the order of a transplant?

- Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."
- Fig 4 :25 "A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion."

A rule settled in one order (it has paid against that order's ground) can become an atom for a receiving order. That is recursion, not the invention of a seventh primitive. But Fig 4 :25 applies: if the rule is carried as a recording of one occasion rather than as a method that can be reapplied, the receiving order repeats a past success and cannot produce a new one. This is why Teubner's "legal irritation" is the more faithful description of real transplants: the imported rule is remade by the receiving frame.

**Falsifier:** a transplant that changes the receiving order's decisions without any change in its closure would move the wall without contact with a differing frame, contradicting Fig 6 :23.

### Q5 - Can an account of coexistence carry a refusal?

Operators table :58 "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate." Fig 12 amendment: "A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate."

A pluralist account whose entire vocabulary is nouns (custom, religion, state, recognition, harmony) can record that an order exists and cannot record that it is wrong, or that a recognition failed. It can be corrected and cannot carry the correction. Fig 5 amendment names the required fifth bin: "refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so". F1 and F5 in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` are exactly such refusals: state law is refused as the ground, and a recognised convention is refused the status of an unrevisable convention.

**Falsifier:** exhibit an account of coexistence that records a failed recognition as a change of state without a negation operator, and that can then refuse its own earlier reading. If such an account exists, the negation requirement is too strong.

## CONCLUSION

The union is a compound whose intersections, not its separate regions, carry the interesting cases. The dependency order runs from a working enforcement instrument to affordance terms before any harmonisation plan. The three orders are a **functional group** as usually described, and a real **isomer** claim only under the full shared atom set, with a stated falsifier. Transplants are recursive only when the settled rule becomes a reapplicable atom, not a recording. And a vocabulary that cannot refuse cannot carry its own corrections.

This rescues the structural content without over-asserting: no new primitive, no ranking of orders, and no claim about which arrangement actually settles a dispute.
