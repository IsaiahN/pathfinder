# Union versus partition, order of repair, and the analogues that carry it

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The question asks whether a corrective history can avoid treating a community as internally uniform. The residual is the gap between the record's prediction about who may count as a knower and what the ground returns in a specific slot. Two sub-questions are owed:

1. Is an excluded community a partition of separate groups, or a union of overlapping conditions (structure)?
2. In what order must description, refusal, import and settlement occur for the correction to survive the figures (order)?

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Is the exclusion a partition or a union?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."
- Fig 9 :57: "What remains is smaller and it is also categorically different: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."
- Fig 9 :5: "are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."

An exclusion in a history of knowledge is a compound: the same person may be excluded by gender, by institutional position, by language of publication, by the kind of knowledge they practised, and by the survival of the archive. Those conditions overlap. A lever that fixes one clears only the region where that one condition holds; it leaves the intersections standing. What remains after the lever is not a smaller version of the same exclusion, it is a specific compound. Treat it as a partition and the correction will report success while the intersections remain excluded.

**Analogue from the world literature.** The Wikipedia entry on invented tradition reports Hobsbawm and Ranger's 1983 claim that traditions which appear or claim to be old are often quite recent and sometimes invented, and records Peter Burke's criticism that all traditions change. The entry also notes the distinction between a tradition and modernity is itself often invented. This corroborates at low overlap that a tradition is not a static object with fixed boundaries. It does not decide the figure reading; it agrees with it.

**Result:** union. `specified`, falsifiable, triangulated across Fig 9 and the historiography source (low overlap).

### Q2. In what order does the repair run?

1. **Describe the residual.** Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit". Cast it in effect terms (Fig 9 :30-31). *General.*
2. **Unbundle the union.** Fig 9 :54. Do not name the tradition until the conditions are separated. *Specified.*
3. **Refuse the exclusion.** Fig 12 :140: "A vocabulary that cannot refuse can only accumulate." The claim that excluded this slot is refused with a negation. Formula step 2 owes refusal before mint. *General.*
4. **Import with provenance.** Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs." Fig 6 :25: "An import adds no atom to the world." Fig 8 :50-52: "Convergent derivation and adopted import look identical in the contents. Only the record of where each came from separates them". *General.*
5. **Settle against the ground.** Fig 2 :45: "Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it." *Grounded only when a measurement is taken; here it is not.*

**Falsifier written before the run:** if a correction that imports before it describes, or mints before it refuses, matches the ground per slot as well as one that follows the order, the order is a convention rather than a dependency and this finding falls.

### Q3. Are two corrected histories isomers of one another?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :96: "Two entries can share every ingredient and be inverses of one another."
- Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance."

Two corrective histories can cite exactly the same recovered contributors, the same archives and the same dates, and still differ categorically, because one bonds the contribution to the practice that produced it and the other flattens it into an entry in a list. The difference is invisible in a bibliography and decisive in what a reader can do with it. Fig 12 :96 strengthens this: identical ingredients can be inverses, so a shared citation list is no evidence of agreement.

The converse also holds. Fig 13 :121 says different routes can arrive at the same substance, so two histories built from different links can compose functionally equal corrections. Corroboration requires low overlap in the routes, not similarity in the outputs (Fig 8 :47: "Independence is one quantity read twice.").

**Result:** the isomer claim is `specified` and structural. The specific case needs a ground: pick two histories with identical ingredient sets and test whether their bonds predict different behaviour in a later reader or a curriculum.

### Q4. What does the membrane carry between the slot and the tradition?

- Fig 4 :22: "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."
- Fig 4 :30: "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."

A correction that works at the level of the slot is a method: enumerate contacts, describe the residual, refuse, import with provenance. That method can cross upward to the tradition level. What must not cross upward is the recording of one successful correction, which becomes a canonical list and produces a system that repeats a past success and cannot produce a new one. Every resort to the tradition level needs the round trip: send the method up, bring a head start down, and measure what the coarser description dropped.

**Result:** `general`, with the round-trip gap enumerated in `11_audit.md`.

## CONCLUSION

The exclusion is a union, the repair has a dependency order, and two corrected histories with identical citation lists can be different substances because the bond is not recoverable from the ingredients. The correction is possible without making the community uniform precisely when the historian enumerates the habitat, describes the residual first, refuses the exclusion, imports with provenance, and subjects the result to a ground. In that form the tradition is not a static object; it is the set of arrangements over contributions, and no single history exhausts it.
