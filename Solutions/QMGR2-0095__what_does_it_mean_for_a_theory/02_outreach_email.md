# Outreach draft - check the separator before you decide about the framework

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover note attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** nothing in this email asks the recipient to trust the framework. It asks them to check one separable claim. If that claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.

---

**Subject:** What would count as a failure of a statistical prediction?

> Hi [name],
>
> I am working on a narrow methods question and would value a check from someone who actually designs tests. I am not asking you to read any framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the claim below fails, there is nothing further to look at.
>
> **The question in effect terms.** A deterministic universal is falsified by one counter-instance. A statistical theory predicts a distribution, so almost every individual outcome has positive probability under it. Two answers are on offer. Answer A says this makes the theory unfalsifiable, at most disconfirmable to a high degree. Answer B says any sufficiently improbable outcome refutes it. I think both are wrong because they ask the wrong object.
>
> **The shape claim (`specified`, not `grounded`).** "Falsifiable" for a statistical theory is not a property of the distribution alone. It is a four-condition separator:
> 1. the theory stakes a **low-probability refusal region** that it commits to accept before the run, indexed per observable slot rather than collapsed into one global fit number;
> 2. the data come from an **anchor that does not update** on what the theory predicts, read through channels that have not decayed or been pooled;
> 3. the refusal is checked by **independent instruments or datasets**, not by a single evidence pool speaking twice;
> 4. a non-refusal is reported as **unreached at this budget**, never as verification, and the significance threshold is recorded as a convention that fixes the decision budget, not as a fact about the world.
>
> If any one of the four is absent, the claim is not falsifiable in the operative sense. They are a union of conditions, so one lever does not clear the bundle.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that p < 0.05 makes a theory false, that a Bayes factor above some value makes it true, that frequentist testing is correct and Bayesian inference is not, or that any specific alpha is returned by the framework. No number of that kind is claimed. The framework returns a shape, not a name. Those numbers are conventions for the decision budget, and a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim (written before this run):**
> 1. a statistical theory with no bound refusal region that still gets tested and refuted in ordinary practice, with no ad hoc rescue;
> 2. a demonstration that a single global goodness-of-fit statistic preserves the per-slot signal, so the indexing condition is unnecessary;
> 3. a case where two instruments drawing on the same evidence pool legitimately corroborate a verdict about the world;
> 4. a case where failing to reject is properly reported as verification of the theory, rather than as a statement about the budget.
>
> If any of these holds, the separator is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - [Philosophy of Statistics, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/statistics)
> - [The ASA Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose](https://amstat.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108)
> - [Degrees of riskiness, falsifiability, and truthlikeness (arXiv:2107.03772)](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.03772v1)
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the figures and the formula are public. Run them on a test you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> - [your name]
