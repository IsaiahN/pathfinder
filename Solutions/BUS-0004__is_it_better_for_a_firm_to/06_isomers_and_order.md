# Related scope as isomers, functional groups, and dependency order

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks which scope wins. In effect terms the residual is the vector of slot-residuals R = |Γ(b,a) - o′| for each product-market slot, and "better" is the persistence of a low R across slots and time. Two supporting derivations are owed: (1) what kind of composition related diversification is, and (2) what order the conditions bind in, given that the question is a union.

Atoms held in the business frame (as bindings, not derived primitives): a capability set, product lines, customer channels, and a ground of realized return. ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is related diversification an isomer?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- A focused firm's core product and a related new product, built from the same capability set, share atoms. They differ in the arrangement of those atoms into a product and a market. Under Fig 12 :117 they are isomers: same capabilities, different bond, different market substance.
- This is stronger than the usual "economies of scope" statement because it is a claim about structure, not just cost. Fig 13 :115: *"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."* The capability set is finite; the arrangements over it are not. A related new product is a new arrangement over an unchanged set.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the new product requires a capability the firm did not already hold, the isomer claim fails and the move is an import, not a rearrangement.

### Q2 - Is a core competence a functional group?

- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Prahalad and Hamel's core competence ("collaborative learning, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technologies") is a substructure whose presence fixes which new businesses are available to the firm. It says *what kind* of diversification is reachable; the particular product says *which member*. This is the figure's functional group at the firm level.
- **Falsifier:** if a firm's shared competence does not constrain which new businesses it can enter, the functional-group reading is wrong and the competence is just a label.

### Q3 - What order do the conditions bind in?

- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- The dependency chain for scope: perception (what is in the new market) → vocabulary (what it affords) → objective (what counts as progress there) → planning (a route) → learn and carry (name what worked and settle it).
- A firm that diversifies before it has vocabulary and an objective for the new slot is spending effort above the break (Fig 3 :29-30). Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- **Falsifier:** a firm shown to have explicit terms and objectives for a new slot yet failing for a reason traceable to perception, which would relocate the break.

### Q4 - Union, not partition

- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
- Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either."*
- The two conditions are C1 (a maintained channel to a paying ground) and C2 (renewed reach through a differing closure). A specialist that loses its market is failing C2 while keeping C1; a diversified firm hiding a loss in a pooled average is failing C1 in a unit while keeping C2. A single lever clears only the region where one condition holds and leaves the intersection standing.
- **Falsifier:** one intervention restoring both conditions with no categorically different residue, which would make the union a partition.

### Q5 - Recursion and the second-order claim

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- A business unit that is settled against the ground (it survives and pays) becomes an atom for the next level: the portfolio. The question of whether the whole firm is better off diversified is a second-order composition over settled first-order units, and is legal only because those units settled. This is recursion (Fig 12 :103), not import.
- **Falsifier:** a settled business unit shown unable to serve as an atom for a portfolio-level composition, which would break the recursion.

## CONCLUSION

Related diversification is an isomer claim (same capabilities, different arrangement, different market substance), and a core competence is a functional group that says which new members are reachable. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the vocabulary/objective link the usual break. The whole question is a union of two conditions, not a partition, so no single scope lever clears it. The portfolio-level verdict is a second-order composition over settled business units, licensed by Fig 12's settled-molecule rule and requiring no new primitive.

This framing keeps what is defensible in both the strategy and IO literatures without letting either hand over the verdict: economies of scope and core competence are bindings onto held atoms, recorded as imports with no source gate until one is supplied; the diversification discount is instrument-dependent, so it is a filter for the budget, not a witness for the verdict.
