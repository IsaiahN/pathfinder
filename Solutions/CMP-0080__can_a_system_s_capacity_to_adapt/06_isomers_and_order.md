# Isomers and order: what "structure" can and cannot carry

**Mode: `specified` on the per-domain derivation, `general` on the structural claims. Not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The word "structure" in the question is doing two jobs: (1) an inventory of atoms and bonds inside the system, and (2) the arrangement that a crisis will test. The derivation must decide which of these can carry a pre-crisis prediction, and in what order. It must also say whether the three attributes in the closest world literature (resilience, adaptability, transformability) are the same thing arranged differently or different things.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What order does the prediction chain have, and where does it break?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Each link consumes what the one above produces (Fig 3 :20-21).
- Fig 3 :25-26: perception has obvious tests, planning has obvious tests, but the link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments.
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- ⇒ For this question the break is at the affordance/objective link, not at the structure inventory. A team can count components, redundancy and diversity (above the break) while having no term for what a specific crisis affords or what would count as adaptation to it. Effort spent above the break cannot rescue the link below it (Fig 3 :19).
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit an instrument that measures the affordance/objective link directly across systems, and show that the prediction fails for a reason above that link. If the link is intact where prediction fails, Q1 is wrong. Status `specified`, single-source on Fig 3; world corroboration from early-warning-signal work is about the channel (F5), not the affordance link.

### Q2 - Are resilience, adaptability and transformability isomers?

The closest world source (Walker, Holling, Carpenter and Kinzig 2004) defines three attributes:

- resilience: capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganize while retaining the same function, structure, identity and feedbacks;
- adaptability: capacity of actors in the system to influence resilience;
- transformability: capacity to create a fundamentally new system when the existing one becomes untenable, by introducing new state variables.

Under the figure's definition:

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- **Resilience versus adaptability** are **isomers** over a fixed atom set: same held atoms (states, relations, actors), different bond. Resilience composes the state with a threshold comparison (`⋛`) that keeps it in the basin; adaptability composes the actors with a production bond (`⇒`) onto the resilience. Same ingredients, different arrangement, different behaviour. The flat ingredient list cannot tell them apart.
- **Transformability is not an isomer.** It introduces new state variables, so it changes the atom set. *They're Scanning Books*: *"The real import is not a new term in the existing space. It is a new dimension of the space."* Fig 6 :14: *"the only operator that moves the wall"* (import). Fig 13 :43-44 notes that a continuum was removed from the six for failing indivisibility: *"A generating set must not contain what its own members produce."* Adding a dimension is an import with a source gate and a shadow test, not a re-arrangement (Fig 8 :50-52).
- Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* All three attributes share the functional group "system in contact with a changing ground"; the qualifier (which bond connects the actors to it) decides which member.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** show that transformability can be written over the original atom set with no new state variable and no import, or that resilience and adaptability require different atoms. Either breaks the isomer/import split. Status `specified`, triangulated across Fig 12 :117, Fig 6 :14, and the Walker et al. definitions (low overlap between the bond rule and the ecology framing). A world-source conflict was checked: Walker et al. call all three "attributes," but the figure's criterion is bond and atom-set difference, not the word used, so the conflict dissolved at re-census without flipping the verdict.

### Q3 - What kind of comparison is a pre-crisis capacity reading?

- Fig 12 :87: *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* defines comparison `⋛`. Fig 12 :89: *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present?"*
- The world source gives the threshold directly: Walker et al. define *precariousness* as *"how close the current state of the system is to a limit or 'threshold.'"* That is a `⋛` between current state and a limit.
- Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond."* A capacity judgment is a goal comparison, not a sequence and not a stock.
- ⇒ The operational pre-crisis reading is a threshold comparison of the current trajectory against a limit, measured through a channel that must stay reachable (Fig 10 :24). It is **not** a structural inventory. Early-warning signals (critical slowing down, rising autocorrelation and variance) are exactly a trajectory reading, but the literature also records that *"Regime shifts in ecological systems can occur with no warning"* (Wikipedia "Critical transition", citing Hastings and Wysham 2010), so the comparison has a false-negative mode.
- **Live type question, named not hidden:** the Operators table restricts `⋛` to *"two quantities."* Whether a structural description plus a current trajectory counts as a quantity at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. Recorded as a strain in `01` section 7 and `11_audit.md`.
- **Falsifier:** a case where a capacity judgment is shown to be a sequence or a simple presence/absence (`+`) rather than a threshold, against a measured ground. If so, Q3 is wrong.

### Q4 - What do the four structures of a compositional system say about capacity?

Fig 12 names four structures (lines 116-124):

1. **isomer** (Q2 above): same atoms, different bond, different behaviour. Component counts cannot distinguish a system that will adapt from one that will not.
2. **catalyst**: Fig 12 :119 - *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* Structural inventories routinely omit whether a part renews. This is what "adaptive capacity" is usually trying to name, and an inventory cannot see it. Kirschner and Gerhart 1998 describe exactly this property in biological systems (versatile proteins, weak linkage, compartmentation, redundancy, exploratory behaviour): *"reduce the interdependence of components and confer robustness and flexibility on processes."*
3. **cycle**: Fig 12 :121 - *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Fig 13 :31-32: a remainder compounding through a chain that amplifies is chaos, and discarded detail reappears as noise. The same network with the opposite loop sign has different capacity, and an ingredient list cannot carry the sign.
4. **functional group**: Fig 12 :123 - the substructure whose presence fixes the outcome; the qualifier says which member. "Having a network" fixes the kind, not the member.

- **Falsifier for Q4:** two systems whose atom sets, bond types and loop signs are all identical, whose parts are all catalysts rather than spent, and whose habitats are identical, yet which adapt differently under the same measured residual. Any such case breaks the structural-shape claim without touching the `gap` in F4 of `01`.

## CONCLUSION

The order is: perceive the structure per slot; acquire terms for what a situation affords (the broken link); bind the goal as a threshold comparison; then judge. Resilience and adaptability are **isomers** over a fixed atom set; transformability is an **import of a new dimension**, not an isomer. A pre-crisis capacity reading is a `⋛` on a trajectory against a limit, which is a channel reading with a known false-negative mode, not a structural inventory. The four structures (isomer, catalyst, cycle, functional group) explain why a component count cannot carry the prediction: the bond, the renewal status, the loop sign and the qualifier are "the half nothing was recording" (Fig 12 :5). What survives is a structural shape plus a refuter. The literal capacity number stays `gap`.
