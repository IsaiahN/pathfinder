# Isomers, functional groups, and order in the preservation claim

**Mode: `specified` on the ecological application, `general` on the structural distinctions.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two communities can give the same function reading while their species identities differ. Are they the same thing in different spellings (isomers under a symmetric bond), members of one kind (a functional group), or genuinely different substances that happen to agree at one instrument? And what does the order of turnover tell us that a single snapshot cannot? These are owed because `01` F4 says the arrangement carries preservation but does not yet say what kind of arrangement it is.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: the six terms of Fig 13 (space, time, energy as states; spectrum, gradient, network as structures) are the generic substrate; the ecological binding adds functional groups, effect traits, response traits and conditions as *available vocabulary*, not as new primitives. Bonds held: all seven plus negation (Operators table). No new primitive is available without import. The anchor is the per-function measured flux.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are two communities that preserve the same function isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*
- Fig 13 :121: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."*

Two cases must be kept apart.

**Case A, same species set, different interaction wiring.** Atoms are identical; the bond differs. Under Fig 12 :117 these are isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The function reading can be the same at one instrument while behaviour under the next disturbance differs. This is exactly the metamerism case in `05` section 3: the reading does not identify the source.

**Case B, different species, same functional groups.** The atom sets are not identical, so the strict definition of isomer does not apply. What they share is a **functional group**: Fig 12 :123 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The members are exchangeable at the level that pays for the function. Calling this "isomerism" would be a category error; it is membership in one arrangement kind with different qualifiers.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a community can be shown to preserve a function through turnover while sharing neither the atom set nor the functional-group substructure with the comparator, and no stable dominant or outside subsidy accounts for it, both readings fail and the arrangement claim in `01` F4 must be withdrawn.

### Q2 — Which bond governs "identity change"?

- Fig 12 :59: *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."* This identifies a symmetric bond.
- Fig 12 :74: *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."* This identifies a sequence.
- Operators table :34: *"the only bond a flat chain can express, so every term today is a sequence whether or not it should be"*

If two species contribute the same effect and swapping them changes nothing at the function reading, the operative bond is symmetric (+, ∥, or ≡) at that level. If order matters, for example a nitrogen fixer must establish before a later successional species can use the soil, the operative bond is → sequence. A flat species list records both as the same string, which is the flat-form failure of Fig 12 :14-16: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not."*

Three consequences:

1. A diversity list cannot distinguish symmetric substitution from ordered succession. That is why richness is a filter, not a witness (`01` F9).
2. "Preserved under turnover" where members swap symmetrically is a permutation within one term (Fig 12 :92: *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."*). Under a symmetric bond, k operands have k! spellings and one term, so the change of identity is not a change of the paying arrangement.
3. Where order matters, turnover is a different chain, and Fig 12 :74's test applies. Two chains built from different links may arrive at the same function (Fig 13 :121), but the lineage matters because the next residual differs.

### Q3 — Catalysts, cycles, and what actually persists

- Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*
- Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*

A species or functional group that persists through repeated use without being consumed is a catalyst in the figure's sense. A nutrient that returns through decomposition is a cycle. These are different structures and they answer different versions of "preserve": catalyst-like persistence keeps the *actor*, cycle-like persistence keeps the *flux* while actors turn over. A diversity metric conflates them. The relevant discriminator, written as an instrument condition, is whether the function reading survives removal of the actor (cycle-like) or fails with it (catalyst-like). This is one of the source-discriminating measurements owed in `07` and `08`.

### Q4 — Dependency order of the preservation question

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

Order: perceive function per slot → acquire terms for what each species affords to that function → state the threshold that counts as preserved → choose an intervention under incomplete information → carry the result as a method. The likely break is at link 2, the affordance vocabulary. Ecology has excellent instruments for biomass and adequate ones for several fluxes, but response-trait and interaction metrics that would discriminate among candidate sources are unevenly available; the mirror of this is Eisenhauer et al. 2023's warning that assessments are often snapshots at a scale that cannot resolve the relevant process (`04`). A reading taken below that break is not a weak reading of preservation; it is a reading of nothing (Fig 3 :22-23).

### Q5 — What the low-overlap analogues actually support

- **Engineering, N-version programming:** *"multiple functionally equivalent programs are independently generated from the same initial specifications."* This is Case B in Q1: different members, same functional specification, preservation when one fails depends on independence of failure modes. The same page records Knight and Leveson's result that independence *"failed statistically"*, which is the Fig 2 :40 shared-pool failure in another field. The analogue supports the shape conditionally, not unconditionally.
- **Cybernetics, requisite variety:** *"only variety can destroy variety"* and the observer's discrimination must be specified. This supports F4 (response variety) and F7 (instrument discrimination), and it adds a limit: a count of species is not a count of distinguishable responses unless an observer's powers of discrimination are declared. A "redundant" species is redundant only relative to a named function and a named disturbance set.
- **Chemistry, from the figures themselves:** Fig 12 :97-98 names silver fulminate and silver cyanate as having the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into notation. The ecological analogue is the same: two communities with the same richness can have different properties because the bond is not recoverable from the ingredient list.

## CONCLUSION

Two communities preserving the same function are **not** generally isomers. If they hold the same atoms and differ only in bond wiring, they are isomers (Fig 12 :117). If they hold different species but the same functional group, they are members of one arrangement kind (Fig 12 :123), and the members are exchangeable at the level that pays for the function. The operative bond is symmetric when members swap without changing the reading and ordered when succession matters. What persists is either a catalyst-like actor or a cycle-like flux, and the discriminator is an instrument, not a term. The dependency chain most likely breaks at the affordance vocabulary, not at the richness measurement. None of this adds a primitive, and none of it names a species; the falsifier in Q1 is written before this conclusion.
