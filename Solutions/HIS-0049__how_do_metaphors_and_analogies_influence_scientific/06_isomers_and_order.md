# Isomers and order: structure, mapping order, and three analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit finds that an analogy changes classification by selecting the recorded bond, and changes explanation by mapping relations. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) what is the exact structural test that separates a real reclassification from a relabelling, and (2) what is the order of the mapping, and what do the standard historical analogues show about it?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this supporting derivation: a target residual R, a source frame, a bond between them, and a ground that pays or refuses. Bonds held: the seven of Fig 12 plus negation. The analogy itself is `→` (sequence), not `≡` (identity), because the operands are not interchangeable. No new primitive is available without import.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - The structural test: is a new classification a new substance or a new name?

- Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."
- Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."
- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

=> The test is not "did the name change?" It is "did the bond change, and does the behaviour differ?" A relabelling leaves both the bond and the behaviour alone. A reclassification changes the bond and the behaviour together.

- The figure's own case, Fig 12 :97-98: "This is not an analogy. Chemistry hit it first: silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation." Fig 13 :121-123 repeats the chemistry and names Berzelius 1830.
- World corroboration, different field: the Wikipedia "Isomer" history section records Wöhler's 1827 silver cyanate, its identity of composition with Liebig's silver fulminate, and its different properties, adding that the finding "challenged the prevailing chemical understanding of the time, which held that chemical compounds could be distinct only when their elemental compositions differ."

So the historical instance is a *literal* structural change, and that is why it is a better witness than any figurative analogy. It is `triangulated`: two figures (12 and 13) plus an independent history source.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a community is found to have re-sorted a kind with an unchanged bond and unchanged behaviour, only a new label, and the ground pays for the new sort, then the bond test fails and Fig 12 is the wrong domain for classification.

### Q2 - The order of the mapping is not optional

- Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."
- Fig 8 :27-28: "1: describe R, then find the frame that already predicts its shape"
- Fig 9 :29: "matching is a one-pass check, not a search"
- Fig 12 :30-32: `→` is "A then B; B needs A to have happened," with the test "swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."

=> The mapping is ordered. Describe the residual in effect terms first, then find the frame. Reversing the order does not produce a weaker analogy; it produces a different and unlicensed object, because the match was manufactured by the choice of frame.

- The SEP "Analogy and Analogical Reasoning" entry gives the field's own version: an analogical argument is plausible "because of certain known (or accepted) similarities with the source domain, despite certain known (or accepted) differences," and its guidelines include "the relevance of the similarities and differences to the conclusion must be taken into account." Relevance is the shadow test by another name.
- Fig 12 :65-67 defines `≡` as "two names, one referent," with the test "substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No." An analogy fails this test (swap the solar system and the atom and much is lost), so `→` and not `≡` is the correct glyph. Promoting an analogy to identity is what turns a useful bridge into a false claim.

**Falsifier:** a documented analogy whose source and target relations are interchangeable with no loss of prediction, and the ground pays for the identity. Then `≡` is correct and Q2 falls.

### Q3 - Three analogues, read as the question needs them

| analogue | what transfers | what fails | figure it instantiates |
|---|---|---|---|
| silver fulminate / silver cyanate (1820s chemistry) | nothing figurative: the same composition, different arrangement, different properties | it is *not* an analogy, so it cannot be used as one; its value is as the literal standard | Fig 12 :97-98, Fig 13 :121-123 |
| hydrogen atom / solar system (Rutherford, 1906; reported in the structure-mapping source) | relations: relative motion, mass, attraction, who revolves around whom | attributes: the Sun's temperature, colour, material | Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one." |
| water flow / electric circuit (hydraulic analogy, engineering and teaching) | relations: flow rate to current, pressure to voltage, constriction to resistance | fields, charge sign, quantum effects. The Wikipedia source records "negative transfer" and "misconceptions" when the source is taken too far | Fig 6 :12-13: "source gate: provenance", "shadow test: explains a gap" |

The pattern is constant: an analogy is a *partial* mapping with an explicit negative part. Hesse's terminology, reported in both SEP entries, names it: positive analogy (shared), negative analogy (different), neutral analogy (not yet known which). The neutral part is where the new hypothesis lives. The SEP models entry: "Neutral analogies play an important role in scientific research because they give rise to questions and suggest new hypotheses." That is the figures' "shadow test: explains a gap" stated in the philosophy of science.

### Q4 - What the three analogues do NOT show

- They do not show that a metaphor *is* the mechanism. In the chemistry case the structure was forced by experiment. In the other two, the analogy only transfers relations already available in the source frame. Fig 6 :28: "An import adds no atom to the world."
- They do not show that a memorable analogy becomes true by being remembered. Fig 7 :12-13: "same closure, handed along; agreement compounds, evidence does not."
- They do not settle the psychological question of why relations map more easily than attributes. That is a `gap` in the figures. The world source (Gentner 1983, reported by Wikipedia) supplies it, and it is marked secondary in `09_watson_answer.md`.

## CONCLUSION

The structural test for a real reclassification is a change of bond with a change of behaviour, not a change of name. The mapping is ordered: residual first, frame second, and the correct glyph is `→` rather than `≡`. The three analogues separate cleanly: the chemistry case is the literal standard, the atom and the circuit are partial relation transfers with named negative and neutral parts. A metaphor earns its keep at the neutral part, where it suggests a hypothesis that the shadow test can then check. None of this makes the metaphor the mechanism or adds an atom to the world.
