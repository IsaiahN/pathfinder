## DERIVATION

### Q1: Is each domain first-order?

- Fig 13 :74 **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."**
- Fig 13 :75 **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."**
- A first-order molecule is state, structure, state, one crossing.
- Domain 1: Energy, Spectrum, Time, with a gradient of entanglement supplying directedness. In the ETH literature this is an expectation-value statement for a single initial state, with no external bath.
- Domain 2: Energy, horizon structure, Time, with directed entropy flow. In the scrambling literature the directedness is the spread of information under unitary evolution.
- Fig 13 itself licenses more than one gradient: **"A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."** So two first-order gradient molecules are legal without a seventh term.
- **Status:** legality triangulated (Fig 13 plus the two first-order literatures); the exact binding of each atom remains `specified`, unmeasured.

### Q2: Are they isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123 **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As the domains are usually described they share Energy, Time and a directed structure, but their third atoms differ. On that reading they are not isomers. They are members of the same **functional group**: a directed cross-kind composition whose qualifier selects which member.
- The stronger version: write both over the full shared set `{Space, Time, Energy, Spectrum, Gradient, Network}`, differing only in which structure mediates which state pair. Then they **are** isomers under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. That is the claim worth testing.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either domain requires an atom the other does not (for example a horizon term that cannot be expressed as a composition of the six, or a spectrum without a state), the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. A second falsifier: if the two domains share all six atoms and the same bond arrangement, they are not isomers but one term, and the union reading in F1 of `01` fails.

## CONCLUSION

Both domains are **first-order derivatives** of the six-term set. Their relation is **second order**, licensed by Fig 12 :104 rather than by any new primitive. On the isomer question the ordinary description yields only the weaker functional-group claim; the stronger isomer claim holds conditionally when both are written over the full shared atom set, and it carries a pre-registered falsifier.
