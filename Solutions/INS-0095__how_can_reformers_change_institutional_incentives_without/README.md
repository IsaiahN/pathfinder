## Restating the question in effect terms

A reformer holds a term: change the formal incentive rule, and the institution's behaviour will change. The observation that generates the question is that after the change, members resist and the effective incentive returns to its prior setting. The decision is between (A) pushing harder on the formal rule and (B) changing the conditions under which the rule is grounded. A leads to more coherent drift toward whoever already holds influence. B leads to a checkable shape for a reform and a refuter that can fire before any policy is named.

What an answer would look like, bound as an ordinary term before the run: a strategy is a reform strategy in this sense only if it (i) ranges against an anchor that does not move when incumbents push on it, (ii) changes what actually pays and not only what is declared, (iii) introduces rather than subtracts, (iv) declares the reformer's stake, and (v) keeps per-slot residuals rather than an averaged satisfaction score. That binding is the pre-registered shape in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md`.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by likelihood of agreeing with the claim.
- No bulk-send plan.

Three reasons, in the figures' own words. Fig 2 :35: "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." Fig 2 :40: "agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool". Seat reasoning: selecting recipients for receptiveness manufactures the correlation that makes their agreement worthless. Fig 10 :42: "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it." The first contact must carry the residual and its refuter, never the framework's interior.

Legally: unsolicited bulk outreach in many jurisdictions engages GDPR and CAN-SPAM style rules. Public opt-in and submission channels avoid that failure as well as the structural one.
