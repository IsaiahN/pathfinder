# Union, order and structure: a supporting derivation for the reform question

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The headline question asks for a strategy. Two sub-derivations are owed before a strategy can be stated: (1) is the failure one problem or a union of conditions, and (2) what is the dependency order of a reform, and which structural forms recur across cases? Without these, "resistance" stays a single blob and reform advice collapses into "communicate better".

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the institutional vocabulary: rule, sanction, monitor, comparison site, outcome measure, influence weight, informal norm, instrument. Bonds held: the seven plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. Currency: what pays, measured per slot. Anchor: the ground outcome, never agreement.

## Q1 — Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55-57: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."

The failure has two conditions: A, the formal rule was changed; B, the informal response restores the old equilibrium. A lever that clears A (a better written rule, a clearer memo) leaves B binding, because B is a separate condition with its own habitat. So the residual is a **union compound**, with its own loop and its own ground. Treating it as one hard problem is the bundling error.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single lever reliably clears both conditions across independent institutions, so that changing the rule alone changes what pays and no restoration appears, the union reading is false and the problem is one partition with two symptoms.

### Consequence for the strategy

You cannot search for "the reform" once. You run one loop for the rule (does it make the bug statable?) and one loop for the habitat (what restores the old payoff?). The two verdicts stay separate and are never averaged (Fig 1 :19).

## Q2 — What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."
- Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

Mapped to this question:

| link | the institutional version | does it usually have an instrument? |
|---|---|---|
| perception | can members tell the new rule from the old one, and tell both from what actually pays? | yes, surveys and audits exist |
| vocabulary | are there terms for what the new rule affords, what can be monitored, what can be compared? | rarely |
| objective | can the reform say what would count as progress, in its own terms? | rarely, this is the middle break |
| planning | is there a route to that objective under incomplete information? | yes, project plans exist |
| learn and carry | are successes named, verified and carried to the next institution? | partially, usually as recordings |

Effort lands above the break because that is where the measurements are. The middle links, vocabulary and objective, are the ones with no instrument, so they attract the least attention and are the likely break (Fig 3 :24-27).

**Falsifier:** instrument the objective link in an institution where reform still fails. If the failure and the residual are unchanged, the break is not at link three and this ordering is wrong.

## Q3 — Are reform cases isomers, or members of a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two reforms that use the same elements (a rule, a monitor, a sanction, a comparison site, an outcome measure) but arrange them differently are **isomers** under Fig 12's definition: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The ingredient list cannot tell them apart; the behaviour can. Reforms that share only the "rule plus sanction" substructure are members of the same **functional group**, not isomers: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

The stronger, checkable claim: across institutions, the arrangement anchored-measure / introduced-monitor / declared-stake / per-slot-residual is what fixes outcome, not the particular rule text. The weaker claim is that all such reforms merely share a functional group.

**Falsifier:** exhibit two institutions with the same five-atom arrangement and decisively different outcomes, or two with different arrangements and the same outcome once the anchor is held fixed. That kills the isomer claim and leaves only the functional-group reading.

## Q4 — What structure recurs?

Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use." Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."

- **Catalyst:** a monitor or comparison site that keeps working across uses is a catalyst. A monitor that is spent or captured after one reporting cycle is a spent part. "Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is" (Fig 12 :119). Ostrom's design principle that monitors are "accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators" is an imported instance of the regenerating case, not a figure-derived rule.
- **Cycle:** the status-quo-restoring path is a negative cycle whose output (the old payoff) re-enters as input (what members update on). A chain cannot close by construction, so a reform planned as a linear sequence cannot model resistance at all. The loop's sign decides whether it closes.
- **Negation:** a reform vocabulary with only rule-addition terms can accumulate rules and cannot refuse one. Operators :58: "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate." Repeal is a different operation from refusing a reading; conflating them is the error noted in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §6.

## Q5 — What order is the whole programme?

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."

A first-order reform loop (anchor, measure, introduce, compare, report per slot) must settle against the ground before the next institution's programme composes over it. Scaling a reform by copying the policy text before the first loop has settled sends a recording upward ("A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion", Fig 4 :25). What can cross is the method and a head start (Fig 4 :22), with the round-trip gap measured (Fig 4 :30).

## CONCLUSION

The failure is a union compound with two loops, not one hard problem. The dependency chain most likely breaks at vocabulary or objective, where no instrument exists. Reform cases are isomers when they share the five atoms and differ only in arrangement, otherwise members of one functional group. The recurring structures are a regenerating monitor (catalyst), a negative restoration cycle, and the need for negation. The programme is ordered: settle the first loop before composing over it, and scale the method rather than the recording.

This rescues what is defensible without naming a policy: the order, the union split, the isomer test and the structural roles need no new primitive and no uniqueness claim.
