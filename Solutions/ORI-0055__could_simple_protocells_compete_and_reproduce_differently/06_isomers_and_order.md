2. What is the dependency order among growth, division, competition and heredity, and which bond types carry that order?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What kind of difference is "reproduce differently"?

- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Two populations with the same ingredient list can behave differently because the arrangement differs. Katla et al. 2023 report two synthetic protocell populations differing only in that one contains a photocatalyst; one excludes the other. Adamala and Szostak 2013 report vesicles with and without an encapsulated dipeptide, and vesicles containing catalysts of different efficiency; the more efficient catalyst wins. Over the shared atom set, these are isomers in the figure's sense: same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the two populations differ in their ingredient lists, the isomer claim fails and the difference is a composition difference, not an arrangement difference. If they share ingredients and arrangement yet differ in persistence under identical conditions, the ground has not settled the difference and the valence claim in `01` F2 needs re-census.

### Q2 - Are they isomers or a functional group?

- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

As the literature writes them, two fatty-acid vesicle populations with and without a phospholipid fraction share most atoms and differ in one substructure. That substructure fixes the outcome, so the weaker and safer claim is a **functional group**: `compartment with membrane-modifying substructure`, with the qualifier selecting the member. The stronger isomer claim holds when the full atom set is shared and only the arrangement or bond differs (Q1). Both are reported; the weaker one is the one that survives without the fixed-atom condition.

### Q3 - Where does the catalyst sit?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

The Ser-His dipeptide in Adamala and Szostak 2013 is exactly this: it catalyses formation of a hydrophobic dipeptide that localises to the membrane, and it is not consumed in the net reaction. Note the figure's warning: nothing in the ingredient list records whether a part is spent or regenerating. A model that lists molecules but not their catalytic role has recorded half the system. This is a `specified` structural reading, corroborated by the chemistry, not a ground claim about prebiotic peptides.

### Q4 - Is a self-reproducing protocell a cycle?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Growth produces a filamentous vesicle; shear fragments it into daughters; daughters grow. The output re-enters as input, so the compartment is a cycle, not a chain. The sign matters: if division loses contents faster than growth accumulates them, the loop is open and the lineage dies. Chen et al. 2004 note that a chain cannot close in this way without a mechanism for division, and that osmotic swelling makes division energetically expensive; the filamentous-growth route is what makes closure physically plausible. Abil et al. 2024 show the negative case at the molecular level: without compartments, shorter parasites outcompete the full-length replicator, so the loop does not close on the functional lineage. The sign of the loop is decided by the habitat and the compartment, not by the recipe.

### Q5 - What is the bond order?

- Fig 12 :72: → is sequence, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*.
- Operators table: ⇒ is production, *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*.
- Fig 12 :87: ⋛ is comparison, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*.

The dependency chain reads:

```
variation (in compartments)
   → competition for a limiting resource (⋛, threshold)
   → differential growth (⇒, new vesicles appear)
   → heritable transmission (→, generation t then t+1)
   → trait-frequency change (the R of Fig 7 :26)
```

The arrow between variation and competition is ordered: a difference must exist before it can be selected. The arrow from growth to transmission is also ordered and is where the chain usually breaks (Fig 3 :22), because growth is easy to measure and heritability is not. The comparison ⋛ is a threshold relation between quantities, not a fusion; "compete" is not "merge". Status `specified`. One type caveat is kept open: treating a compartment-level advantage as a quantity that ⋛ can compare is a level-relativity claim (Fig 12 :104, a settled molecule becomes an atom at the next level), and whether a vesicle population counts as a quantity at that level needs an instrument, not an argument. Named, not hidden.

### Q6 - What do the two scales compose into?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- Fig 4 :20: *"Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."*

D1 (chemistry) settles at the level of a vesicle; D2 (competition) composes over it; D3 (heredity) composes over the population. Each settled level becomes an atom at the next. This is recursion, not import (Fig 12 :103-105). What does **not** cross by composition is a new heritable polymer: that is the imported atom of `01` F6, and it needs provenance.

Note the correction kept visible from `05` section 5: I do not cast amphiphiles as members of the six-term physical substrate. The invariant across scales is the loop and the cross-kind composition rule, not a list of physical primitives assigned to molecules. Fig 4 :20 governs the invariance; Fig 13 governs the physical six. Different objects, no strain.

### Q7 - Is the chemistry frame an isomer of the biological frame?

No, in the strict sense. Isomerism requires a shared atom set (Fig 12 :117). A biochemistry-free polymer protocell and a fatty-acid vesicle with RNA do not share the same atoms, so the relation is composition plus import, not isomerism. Where both are described only as "compartment with heritable advantage", they share a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123): same kind, different members. This narrower claim is all the figures license. A falsifier: exhibit a single atom set from which both are composed by rearrangement only; that would upgrade the functional-group claim to an isomer claim.

## CONCLUSION

The phrase reproduce differently is an arrangement and bond difference (isomer over a fixed atom set, or the weaker functional-group claim when the atom sets differ). The catalyst is a part that survives its own use; the self-reproducing compartment is a cycle whose sign decides whether the lineage closes; the dependency order is variation, competition, growth, transmission, trait-frequency change, with the break most likely between growth and transmission because that is the link with no easy instrument. The chain is a recursion of settled levels, with one import (a heritable polymer) that must be provenanced.
