> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know about protocell competition. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified` on the mechanism, `general` on the structure, not `grounded`):** "could simple protocells compete and reproduce differently?" is two conditions joined by "and", not one yes/no question. Competition is an ecological relation inside one generation; differential reproduction is a hereditary relation across a chain of compartments.
>
> The shape any answer must have comes from three checks:
> 1. **Variation inside.** Is there a difference among compartments that can be measured at the level of individual lineages, not just as a population average?
> 2. **Selection between with a non-updating ground.** Does the ground pay for differential persistence? Fig 11 :36 puts it plainly: where nothing maintains anything, *"Nothing was intelligent; selection ran."* No selector seat is required.
> 3. **Heritability across handoffs.** The chain advances only as a product, Fig 7 :26: *"progress per handoff: R = h² × S"*. If either heritability or the selection differential is zero, the chain runs in place. Competition alone supplies the second factor, not the first.
>
> So my answer is: **competition yes, differential reproduction conditional on a measured heritable carrier, and which chemistry actually won on early Earth is an open gap.** A laboratory flask is a substituted habitat, and a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability (Fig 11 :70). I am not claiming a lab demonstration settles abiogenesis.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. A protocell population with a heritable advantage, fixed environment and limiting resource shows no trait-frequency change across generations, measured per lineage.
> 2. Two chemically identical populations drift with no measurement that discriminates which persists, so the ground does not settle the outcome.
> 3. A new heritable atom is derived purely by composition inside a closed chemical closure, with no import.
> 4. A population-average growth curve fully predicts which lineage wins and which trait fixes, with no per-lineage information.
> 5. One lever clears both the competition and the heredity condition at once, so the question is a partition rather than a union.
