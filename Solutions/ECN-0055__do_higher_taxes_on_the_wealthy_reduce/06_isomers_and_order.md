# Union structure, dependency order, and the isomer analogue

**Mode: `general` on the structural claims, `specified` on the slot-level claim, `gap` on the direction. Not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

R is the unassigned change in the composition of effort and capital at the top of the distribution when the top marginal rate rises. Two further questions are owed: (1) what is the *structure* of the thing being asked about, a partition or a union, and in what order must its links be examined; (2) are the two named outcomes two stocks, or two arrangements of the same atoms (an isomer claim)?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available in the census: effort, capital, a rule that can be captured, an enforcement instrument, and a governance habitat. Bonds available: the seven typed bonds plus negation. No new primitive is held without an import, and any import carries a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 6 :12-13, :24). The four domains D1 to D4 are set out in `01` §1 and are not repeated here.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Union or partition?

- Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*
- Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*
- The channels overlap: labor supply, avoidance, bargaining, and rent-sector entry all respond to the same rate change, and several can be chosen by the same actor in the same period. A lever that suppresses one leaves the others binding. This is the structural definition of a union.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a taxon in which each top earner is in exactly one channel and the channels have a common currency. If that is shown, the union collapses to a partition and this derivation is withdrawn.
- Verdict: **union** (`general`, triangulated across Fig 9 and Fig 1).

### Q2 — What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- Ordered links for this question: **D4 instrument** (can we tell real effort from rent capture?) → **D3 habitat** (what governance and enforcement conditions are in contact with the residual?) → **D1/D2 per-slot readings** (what did this firm or this executive do?) → aggregate statement, last and lossy.
- The middle link is the instrument. Fig 3 :24-26 says the link between perception and objective attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments, and Fig 3 :19 says *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* More pooled elasticity estimates land above the break.
- **Falsifier:** show an instrument already in use that returns separate readings for the four channels on the same rate change. Then the link is not broken and this ordering is unnecessary (though still correct).
- Verdict: **instrument first** (`general`, triangulated across Fig 3 and Fig 9 :27-28).

### Q3 — Are productive investment and rent-seeking isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :123: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- Over a fixed atom set `{effort, capital, capturable rule}`, the same atoms under a production bond (effort produces new output) and under a capture bond (effort transfers existing output) are two different substances with the same ingredient list. Their currencies differ, which is the behavioural difference the bond carries.
- World analogue, low overlap: the Tullock paradox in the rent-seeking literature (a rent worth a billion dollars may be pursued with about one percent of that), and Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva's *"pay for luck is quantitatively more important when top tax rates are low."* Both are the same atoms under a different bond and price.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either activity requires an atom the other does not (a capturable privilege for rent-seeking, a capital good for investment), the fixed-atom condition fails, the relation is composition plus import, and the isomer claim falls. The functional-group reading then stands: both are members of the `effort → return` group with different qualifiers.
- Verdict: **isomer only under the fixed-atom condition** (`specified`; single-source on the figure definition, with the world analogue as corroboration). The live constraint is named, not hidden.

### Q4 — What is the order between the rule stratum and the slot?

- Fig 13 :74: *"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."* The six-term substrate governs physical composition; this question is about social arrangements, so the direct mapping is a strain and is **not** attempted. That is a re-census result, not a forced fit: Fig 13 governs the minimum viable set, not the taxonomy of tax responses. (The same re-census pattern appears in the exemplar's F1 downgrade.)
- Fig 10 :71: *"The room composition is a function of the question."* Fig 10 :72: *"When the question changes, the room should change. A room that stays fixed while the question moves is the failure; the correlation is only its symptom."*
- The rule stratum is therefore upstream of the slot in the sense that it sets the habitat, but it is not a fixed background: a rate change is itself a rule change, so the room and the question move together. Historical slot responses are recordings of one room, not structural constants.
- World corroboration, genuinely low overlap: the Lucas critique (*"any change in policy will systematically alter the structure of econometric models"*) and Goodhart's law (*"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"*). These rhyme with Fig 10 :47 and :72 from different fields; they do not replace the figure lines.
- **Falsifier:** demonstrate a slot response that is invariant across two rule regimes measured with the same instrument.
- Verdict: **rule and slot co-move** (`general`, triangulated across Fig 10 and the two world sources, with the Fig 13 mapping explicitly refused).

### Q5 — Which operator describes the answer to "mainly X"?

- Operators table :53-57 defines negation as *"this does not hold"*, with the test *"is the point that it is false, rather than absent? Yes."* A refutation is a negation; *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*.
- Subtraction (`−`) is *"A with B removed or absent"* (Fig 12 :80-82): absence of an operand. The answer to "mainly investment or mainly rent-seeking" is not "investment minus rent-seeking"; it is a refusal of the comparison because the currencies differ and the channels overlap. That is `¬`, not `−`.
- Fig 5 :53: *"refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so"* (the fifth kind). The held term here is the scalar "mainly".
- **Falsifier:** show that the two outcomes share a currency and that one is literally the absence of the other.
- Verdict: **the comparison is refused by negation** (`general`, single-source on the operator table, corroborated by Fig 12 :125-126).

## CONCLUSION

The question's structure is a union, not a partition, and the order of examination is instrument, habitat, slot, aggregate. The two named outcomes are isomers over a fixed atom set with a stated falsifier, not two subtractable stocks, and the scalar "mainly" is refused by negation rather than computed by subtraction. No step returns a magnitude. The Fig 13 substrate mapping was tried and refused by re-census: the figure governs the minimum viable set of states and structures, not the taxonomy of tax responses, and forcing it would be the strain the method forbids.
