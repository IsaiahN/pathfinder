## 0. The claim under audit

> How do changes in the behavior of predators, prey, or pollinators reshape ecological networks?

**Stated as an effect.** An observer sees a network change after a change in the behaviour of one actor class. What is owed is the mechanism by which a local behavioural change becomes a network-level structure change. Two terminations are on the table:

- **A.** Actor classes are kinds. Predator, prey and pollinator each carry a fixed effect. The network change is read off the actor list (fewer predators, more prey, and so on).
- **B.** Actor classes are contact roles. A behaviour change alters who acts on whom and with what valence relative to a chosen residual. The network reshapes through a cascade that must be enumerated outward until the cascade stops mattering.

**Binding the sought-for shape as an ordinary term, before the run** (`THE_FORMULA.md`, "Direction": make the shape a term so it can accrue a residual and be refused):

> A behaviour change is a change in the contact graph of a habitat. The network reshapes to the extent that the changed contact has cascade mass on the chosen residual. Accounting that stops at direct partners is first order and misses residuals that sit further out.

That sentence is written down here before the findings. It is refusable by the same test as any other term, and section 3 writes the refusers before the conclusion.

## 1. Figure census

A census of what governs R, before any figure line is used to conclude. R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain with actors, currency and anchor. A domain with no nameable anchor is not yet a domain.

**R (the residual).** Arity: one behavioural change plus one chosen residual, so the question is a union of a contact change and a measurement choice, not a single lever (Fig 9 :55-58: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*). Symmetry: predation and pollination are directed relations, so the order of operands matters (Fig 12 :71: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*). Scale: from one actor's behaviour, through the interaction graph, to ecosystem state. Anchor: a measured interaction turnover or a measured state change. Agreement among ecologists is not that anchor.

## 2. Findings

### F1 - The actors are relations, not kinds (decisive re-census)

Fig 11 :45-47:

> **"actors: what acts on it, and what it acts on"**
> **"relations: pollination, predation, competition, dependency"**

Fig 11 :49-51:

> **"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."**
> **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."**
> **"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."**

The question's nouns, "predators, prey, or pollinators", are roles in a contact graph, not fixed effect-bearers. A predator is a benefactor to a residual defined as "keep the prey population viable" and a malfactor to a residual defined as "maximise that prey population's harvest". The same bee is a pollinator for one plant and a nectar thief on another. So termination A fails at the census: reading the network change off a fixed actor list is not available. Mode: `general`, structural. Triangulated across Fig 11 :45-51 (habitat frame) and the Operators table's ordered bonds (:29-33, direction is carried by the bond), which have low overlap.

**Refuter.** Assign a valence to predator, prey or pollinator that holds across every residual and survives a change of residual. If one can be assigned and the figure cannot refuse it, F1 fails.

### F2 - The habitat is enumerated, never composed

Fig 11 :43-44:

> **"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."**
> **"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."**

Fig 11 :48:

> **"You do not invent the list. You read it off the world, and what you cannot perceive or measure yet is the residual."**

`THE_FORMULA.md` carries the same rule in compact form: *"Habitat. Enumerate, never compose. Everything in contact with R, then what contacts those, outward until the cascade stops mattering."* So the network is not derived from an actor taxonomy. The list is read off the world and the parts of it that cannot yet be measured are exactly the residual. Mode: `general`. Single-source within the figures (Fig 11 plus the formula that condenses it), but corroborated by the field practice of the world sources in F3.

**Refuter.** Produce a rule that generates the correct habitat list from actor labels alone, with no measurement of contact, and show that it predicts a state change the enumeration would miss. If it does, enumeration is not needed.

### F3 - Contact cascades; first-order accounting misses the residual

Fig 11 :52-54:

> **"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."**
> **"Carson, Silent Spring, 1962. The mechanism, biomagnification through the food chain to eggshell thinning, was established over the following decade."**

This is the direct answer to the question. A behaviour change reshapes the network by changing contacts, and the consequential change can be two or three steps out, at a different stratum from the actor whose behaviour changed. The figure's own ecology example is a chemical introduced at one level and registered at another. Mode: `specified` (the direction is structural; the number of steps in any given system is measured). Single-source within the Tether (Fig 11 only), but independently corroborated in three low-overlap empirical systems: a kelp-forest trait-mediated cascade, plant-pollinator interaction rewiring, and marine juvenile space use (see `09_watson_answer.md` WORLD). That is cross-frame corroboration, not a second Tether witness.

**Refuter.** In a system where a predator, prey or pollinator changes behaviour, show that all first-, second- and third-order contacts on the chosen residual are unchanged. If they are unchanged, the cascade claim predicts no network reshaping and fails.

### F4 - Capability and effect live in the agent-and-habitat, not the agent

Fig 11 :39-40:

> **"capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."**
> **"An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."**

The canonical article lists this among the framework's uncited claims, so it is a framework rule, not a borrowed ecological result. Applied here: a change in an actor's behaviour counts as a network change only insofar as it changes contact. A behaviour change that leaves every contact and valence the same is invisible to the network. Fig 11 :73 adds the boundary: *"The habitat is not the ground. The habitat is where actors come from and it updates; the ground is what settles, and it does not."* So the network is the moving field of contact, while the ground is the measured outcome that settles whether a reshaping happened. Mode: `general`.

**Refuter.** Show a behaviour change that leaves all contacts and valences identical and still changes the measured network state. That breaks the contact-only account and would refute the capability claim in this domain.

### F5 - The network is a structure and cannot carry meaning alone

Fig 13 :23-24:

> **"network"**
> **"that entities relate, and influence travels"**

Fig 13 :29-30:

> **"nothing, a network with nothing at the nodes. So each step acts on one kind and yields the other, and every link in the chain is changed by the link before it."**

Fig 13 :74-75:

> **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."**
> **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."**

The ecological network is the framework's network: a structure, not a state. A structure with no state is empty. So "the network reshaped" is incomplete until the states are named: where the interaction occurs (space), when and in what order (time), and what is being spent or transferred (energy, for example the reward a pollinator gains or the biomass a predator takes). Fig 13 :26: *"it decides what the measurement would mean."* Mode: `general`. Triangulated across Fig 13 :23-30 and Fig 12 :5 (*"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*), two figures with low overlap.

**Refuter.** Give a complete account of a network reshaping that names no state at any level and is still checkable against a measurement. If one exists, the substrate requirement does not bind this domain.

### F6 - Same species, different wiring, different substance

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 13 :121-124 records the same point for composition routes: *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance"* and *"the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."* So two ecological networks with the same species list but different interaction wiring are not two spellings of one network. They are different substances with different behaviour. A change in pollinator foraging that reassigns which bee visits which plant is a bond change at constant atoms, exactly the framework's isomer case. Mode: `specified`, with the check left to the ground. Triangulated across Fig 12 :117 and Fig 13 :121-124.

**Refuter.** If two networks with the same species set and the same interaction bonds but different actor behaviour produce the same measured state outcomes, the bond-carries-information claim has no work to do in this domain. The falsifier is written before the run, as required by the Seat Map (:287: *"a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."*).

### F7 - The apparent Fig 11 versus Fig 13 strain is a re-census, not a conflict

Fig 11 says the habitat is enumerated, never composed. Fig 13 says a network is a structure that composes across kinds. Read against each other, these look contradictory: is the network enumerated or composed? Re-census resolves it. Fig 11 governs **how the habitat list is obtained**, from contact with R outward. Fig 13 governs **how a structure composes once you hold it**, with a state at the other end of the bond. Different objects, so there is no strain after re-census. The canonical rule applies: *"a reading that makes one contradict another has misidentified its domain, not found a flaw"* (local canonical README). The ecological analogue is explicitly declined in the canonical article: a niche is defined by an organism's relation to its environment, while *"a habitat here is defined by contact with a residual, and the residual moves so the same actors constitute a different habitat when the question changes."* This framework's habitat is not an ecological niche. That difference is recorded, not merged.

**Status after re-census: no strain.** A second item, the figure's exact spelling "malfactor" versus the essay's "malefactor", is a source difference, not a figure conflict; both are recorded in section 6.

### F8 - Management can introduce, not exclude

Fig 11 :61-63:

> **"The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not."**
> **"Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."**

Where a maintainer tries to steer a reshaping, the only move available is adding an actor or an instrument, never subtracting the habitat, because contact is a fact about the world. Fig 11 :65: *"Introducing an actor for its cascade is ecosystem engineering, after Jones, Lawton and Shachak, 1994. Cane toads and kudzu are the same move with the cascade unenumerated."* So a management answer to this question is permanent work plus an enumerated cascade, not a one-off fix. Mode: `general` for the asymmetry, `specified` for any particular intervention.

**Refuter.** Demonstrate a management action that removes a contact without substituting a habitat and still counts as steering the residual. If that is possible, the asymmetry does not bind here.

### F9 - Agreement among ecologists cannot settle the reshaping claim

Fig 2 :35: *"Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you."* Fig 2 :40: *"disqualifying for a verdict: agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"* (the leading clause belongs to the whole line). Fig 7 :12-13: *"same closure, handed along; agreement compounds, evidence does not."* Fig 7 :35: *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes."*

The ground here is a measurement of interaction turnover or state change, preferably from a frame that did not read the same primary literature. Agreement from a pool of coauthors or a single review is one witness, not two. Fig 9 :60: *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."* Tier labels and source counts are filters.

**Refuter.** Show two genuinely independent instruments, drawing on different evidence, whose agreement is produced by negotiation or shared provenance. If agreement survives that check, F9 is weakened.

## 3. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per the Seat Map (:287). The shape claim in section 0 is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. All first-, second- and third-order contacts on the chosen residual are unchanged after the behaviour change, while the network state still changes. This kills the cascade mechanism.
2. A valence can be assigned to predator, prey or pollinator that holds across every residual and cannot be refused. This kills the valence-relative claim (F1).
3. Two networks with the same species set and the same interaction bonds, but different actor behaviour, produce the same measured state outcomes. This removes the work done by the bond and reduces F6 to a species-count claim.
4. A complete, checkable account of a network reshaping names no state (where, when, or what is spent). This kills the substrate requirement (F5).
5. A management action demonstrably removes a contact without substituting a habitat. This kills the asymmetry (F8).

If any fires, the relevant row of F10 is withdrawn and the one-paragraph answer in `07` is retracted. Until one is run, nothing may be labelled `grounded`.

## 7. Seven-question recursive derivation (summary)

1. **What is R?** A local behaviour change plus a network-level reshaping. Union, not one lever (Fig 9 :55-58).
2. **Are the actors kinds or roles?** Roles. Valence is relative to R (Fig 11 :49-51).
3. **How is the habitat obtained?** By enumeration outward from contact, never by composing a taxonomy (Fig 11 :43-48).
4. **How does the change travel?** As a cascade, possibly three steps out (Fig 11 :52-54).
5. **What is the network?** A structure, empty without states (Fig 13 :23-30, :74-75).
6. **Is a rewiring a new substance?** Yes under the isomer rule, same atoms and different bonds (Fig 12 :117). Falsifier written in section 3.
7. **Route when no figure closes the residual?** SEARCHED first, then INWARD for a better interaction instrument, then OUTWARD to a frame whose closure predicts the shape (`THE_FORMULA.md` step 7). The three are kept distinct and never read against each other.

**After re-census: one genuine limit, no hidden strain.** The limit is that this framework returns a shape and a route, not an empirical cascade coefficient. That number belongs to the ground, and importing a coefficient from a single study without provenance and a shadow test would be the Fig 6 error, not an answer.
