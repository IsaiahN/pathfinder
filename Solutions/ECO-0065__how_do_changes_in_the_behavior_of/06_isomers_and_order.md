# Networks as molecules: isomers, cycles and order in an ecological reshaping

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The primary audit leaves two structural questions open. First, what makes two ecological nets with the same species list different things? Second, in what order does a behaviour change become a network change? This file derives the supporting structure and writes its falsifier before the conclusion.

The sought-for shape, written as an ordinary term before the run: *a network is a molecule, species are its atoms, interaction types are its bonds, a species set at constant wiring is one substance, and the same species set under a different wiring is a different substance; a behaviour change is a change of bond and the cascade is the order in which the changed bond is composed onward.*

## DOMAIN CENSUS

- **Atoms held:** species in the habitat. These are read off the world by enumeration (Fig 11 :43-44), not composed.
- **Bonds held:** the typed interactions, predation, pollination, competition, dependency (Fig 11 :47), mapped onto the framework's seven bonds. Predation and pollination are ordered: who acts on whom matters.
- **Structures held:** network (Fig 13 :24) plus the states space, time and energy.
- **Currency:** bond changes and cascade steps. **Anchor:** measured interaction turnover and state change.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Is an ecological network a molecule?

Fig 12 :46-47:

> **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**
> **"A recipe that names only its ingredients has recorded half of itself."**

Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**

A species list is a recipe that names only ingredients. The interaction graph is the arrangement. So the ecological network is a molecule in the figure's sense, and the change in behaviour is a change of bond, not a change of ingredient. This is not an analogy imposed from outside: Fig 11 :47 lists predation and pollination as *relations*, and Fig 12 says the relation carries information the operands do not. Triangulated across Fig 11 :47 and Fig 12 :46-47, two figures with low overlap.

### Q2 - Are two nets with the same species and different wiring isomers?

Fig 12 :117:

> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Fig 13 :124: **"the same ingredients under a different bond are a different substance."**

Yes, under the figure's definition. A pollinator switching from plant A to plant B while both species remain present is the same atom set with a different bond, hence a different substance with different behaviour. The species list, the "formula", cannot tell the two apart. This is why an interaction-frequency table that reports only species presence is the flat-form collapse of Fig 12 :6-8.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two networks with the same species set and the same interaction bonds but different actor behaviour produce the same measured state outcomes, the isomer claim has no work to do here and reduces to a species-count claim. If they produce different outcomes at constant bonds, the isomer claim is not what explains the difference and a change of quantity rather than bond must be named.

### Q3 - What do cycles add?

Fig 12 :121:

> **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Pollination can be a positive feedback loop (more flowers support more pollinators support more flowers); predator-prey interaction is a loop whose sign alternates. A behaviour change that flips the sign of a loop changes whether the cascade closes on itself. A chain, by contrast, cannot close by construction, so treating a network as a chain of direct effects loses the closure question entirely. Mode: `specified`, unmeasured for any particular loop.

**Falsifier:** show a network loop whose sign changes while its closure behaviour on the ground is unchanged. That would remove the work Fig 12 assigns to the sign.

### Q4 - Are there functional groups in a network?

Fig 12 :123:

> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

A hub pollinator or a keystone predator is a functional group in this sense: the substructure fixes the kind of outcome, while the species identity says which member of the kind is present. This is a useful bridge to the empirical literature, which often reports that losing a particular species has an effect far larger than its abundance predicts. It is a binding from the ecology frame onto a held figure concept, not a derivation of which species is the hub. Fig 11 :51 (*"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual"*) forbids treating the hub as a fixed good actor; which substructure fixes the outcome depends on R.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a substructure whose presence fixes an outcome across every residual. That would restore a standing list of good actors and break Fig 11 :51.

### Q5 - Where do the states enter?

Fig 13 :29: **"nothing, a network with nothing at the nodes."** Fig 13 :23-26 names network as a structure and gives the test: *"it decides what the measurement would mean."* Fig 13 :74-75: *"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."*

So a reshaping account must name the three states:

- **Space:** where the interaction occurs and whether the actor's range moved.
- **Time:** when the interaction occurs, and the order in which the cascade steps fired. Phenology is a time state, and the plant-pollinator world source shows phenology constraining turnover.
- **Energy:** what is spent or transferred, the reward a pollinator gains or the biomass a predator takes. This is the state that makes the gradient spendable (Fig 13 :22: *"a difference that can be spent"*).

A network with none of these is empty, not a complete description.

### Q6 - What is the order?

Fig 11 :44: *"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."* Fig 9 :27-28: *"describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit."* Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."*

The dependency order for this question is:

1. Name R (which outcome is at stake).
2. Enumerate direct contacts with R.
3. Enumerate what contacts those, outward until the cascade stops mattering.
4. Name the states (where, when, what is spent).
5. Compare the old and new bond arrangements as isomers.
6. Measure the state outcome; let the ground decide.

Steps 2 and 3 are enumeration, not composition (Fig 11 :43). Step 5 is composition, and it uses the bonds the enumeration discovered. Reading step 5 before steps 2 and 3 is the apophenia the canonical rule warns against: any frame will seem to fit once the species list is fixed first.

## CONCLUSION

The ecological network is a molecule: species as atoms, interactions as bonds. Two nets with the same species and different wiring are isomers, not spellings. Cycles add the question of whether a cascade closes, and the sign of the loop decides it. A hub pollinator or keystone predator is a functional group whose identity depends on R. The network needs states (space, time, energy) to carry magnitude, and the order of derivation runs from residual through enumeration to composition and finally to measurement. All of this is `specified` and ungrounded in this folder; the falsifiers above are written before the run.
