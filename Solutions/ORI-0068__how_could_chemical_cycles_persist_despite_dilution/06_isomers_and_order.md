## RESIDUAL

The audit in `01` leaves four structural questions open: what distinguishes a cycle from a chain in
the notation; what distinguishes a regenerated part from a spent one; whether two proposed settings
(wet-dry pools and alkaline vents) are isomers or members of one functional group; and what order the
persistence claim lives at. Each is answered below with its own falsifier.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Cycle versus chain in the notation

- Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it
  closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."
- The Operators table defines `→` as sequence: "A then B; B needs A to have happened." A sequence
  is an ordered bond, not a closed one. Writing a reaction network as a flat list of arrows records
  its ingredients and loses the loop that decides whether it closes.
- Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one." A
  beaker listing of species names a state; the closure relation names the route.
- ⇒ The object that persists is the closure, not the sequence. A linear sequence can produce a
  product once; only a cycle re-enters it as input.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** a flat, one-through reaction set (no product among the
  reactants that regenerate an earlier step) sustains the same steady product population as a
  matched closed set under the same measured loss rates. If that is shown, Q1 fails.

### Q2 - Regenerated part versus spent part

- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave
  differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."
- Fig 12 :40: "same two atoms, three substances" - the arrangement carries information the
  operands do not. Two cycles with identical species lists can behave oppositely across turnovers
  if in one the catalyst is regenerated and in the other it is consumed.
- ⇒ Degradation resistance is not a property of a molecule's toughness. It is a property of the
  arrangement: whether the part that survives its own use is in the loop. A flat formula cannot
  tell you which, so the lineage must be recorded.
- **Falsifier:** two otherwise identical closed networks, one labelled catalyst-regenerated and one
  catalyst-spent, show no difference in product population across repeated turnovers under a
  controlled loss schedule. If they do, Q2 fails.

### Q3 - Are wet-dry pools and alkaline vents isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is
  invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says
  what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Read at the level of the figure's own atoms, both settings instantiate the same pattern:
  `state -> structure -> state`. A gradient over space (a mineral pore or a thermal field) and a
  gradient over time (a wet-dry phase) are different terms because "which state it is bonded to is
  part of what it is" (Fig 13). They are **two members of one functional group**, not isomers as
  written, because the atoms are not the same.
- The stronger claim: if both are described over a shared atom set `{Energy, Gradient, Network,
  Space, Time}` and differ only in which structure mediates which state pair, they become a real
  isomer claim, same atoms arranged differently. That is the checkable version, and Kato 2026
  supplies a candidate experiment (coupled versus isolated reactors) without presenting it as a
  result.
- **Falsifier:** if either setting requires an atom the other does not (a vent with no wet phase, or
  a pool with no spatial gradient), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.
  This is the same discipline the exemplar applied to GR and QM.

### Q4 - What order is the persistence claim?

- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." Fig 12 :106:
  "Nothing composes over what was never admitted as a part."
- A single closed cycle, once settled against the ground, can serve as an operand for a
  habitat-level selection loop. That is second order; the cycle itself is first order.
- Fig 13 :88: "The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no
  two passes begin from the same place." So persistence across environments is not replay. Each
  turn begins from the residual the previous one left, which is why a substituted habitat changes
  what the next turn can compose.
- Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance." What carries across the order boundary is the
  closure relation and its lineage, not a recording of one successful occasion.
- **Falsifier:** show that a settled reaction closure cannot serve as an operand for selection, or
  that each new environment begins from the identical residual (which would make replay sufficient).
  If either fires, Q4 fails.

## CONCLUSION

The cycle is a structural closure and the catalyst is an arrangement property; both are legible only
from the lineage, not from an ingredient list. The two candidate settings are members of one
functional group as usually described, and become a genuine isomer claim only under a shared atom
set with a stated falsifier. Persistence itself is second order: a settled first-order cycle becomes
an atom for selection in a habitat, and the residual of each turn is what the next turn composes
against. Nothing here requires a new primitive, and nothing here is grounded.
