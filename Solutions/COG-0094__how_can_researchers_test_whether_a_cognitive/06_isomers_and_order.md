# Bonds, order and habitat: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question says a cognitive pattern is "caused by culture, ecology, institutions, or their interaction." Three things are owed: what kind of bond "caused by" names; whether "interaction" is a fourth cause or a compound; and what order a test must follow. A fourth question follows from the habitat: what a laboratory test actually substitutes.

## Q1 — What bond does "caused by" name?

- The Operators table defines production as *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* with the test *"is there a B before A fires? No."* (Operators :36-39). It defines sequence as *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* with *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."* (Fig 12 :71-74). Both are **ordered**: swapping operands changes the claim.
- The symmetric bond `+` means *"both hold at once"* (Fig 12 :57). That is coexistence, not causation. A correlation between a cultural practice and a cognitive pattern written with `+` records that both are present and records nothing about production.
- So an attribution must be written with an ordered bond, and which one (production versus sequence) is an empirical question the figures do not decide for this case. Written as `+`, the claim is a flat formula: Fig 12 :5, *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** show a case where swapping cause and pattern leaves the attribution intact and the intervention unchanged. Then the bond is symmetric and the ordered reading is refused.

**Verdict: `specified`, triangulated** (Operators table production test and Fig 12 sequence test, two low-overlap statements of order).

## Q2 — Are the three attributions isomers, or a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
- As the question writes them, the three attributions are `culture → pattern`, `ecology → pattern`, `institutions → pattern`. They share the pattern and the person but not the cause atom. By Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**; they are members of one **functional group** (ordered cause to pattern, with a different cause in the slot). The group fixes the kind of claim; the qualifier picks which habitat condition.
- The stronger form: if all three are written over the full shared atom set `{person, pattern, habitat, intervention}` and differ only in which habitat condition is bonded to which intervention, then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. That is the claim worth testing, because it predicts that the intervention that works depends on the arrangement, not on the size of the effect.
- The "interaction" option is not a fourth atom. It is the compound that remains when more than one condition binds (Fig 9 :55-56), and in statistics it is the lack of additivity between factors: *"When the effect of one factor is different for different levels of another factor, it cannot be detected by an OFAT experiment design. Factorial designs are required to detect such interactions."* (Wikipedia, Factorial experiment, opened 2026-11-22). Fig 12 :104 permits the compound to become an atom at the next level: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."*
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if any one attribution can be written without an atom the others require (for example ecology with no habitat condition, or institutions with no intervention), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. A second falsifier: if a single lever clears the intersections, the compound is not a compound.

**Verdict: `specified`, single-source for the figure definition (Fig 12), with the statistical implementation corroborating but not deciding.**

## Q3 — What is the dependency order of a test?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*

1. **Perception.** Can the pattern be told apart from what was there before? Fig 3 :5: *"the system can tell what is in front of it, and tell it apart from what was there before"*.
2. **Vocabulary.** Are there terms for what the habitat affords, in effect terms rather than imported categories?
3. **Objective.** Can the researcher say what would count as a successful attribution in their own terms?
4. **Planning.** Choose a factorial, natural-experiment, or mixed-method route under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it, carry the method to the next population.

The break is expected between 2 and 3. Fig 3 :25-26: *"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."* Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

This is the link that needs the pre-registered refuter in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` §3. Effort spent on statistics (step 4) cannot rescue a missing objective (step 3): Fig 3 :19, *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* A reading taken below the break is not a weak reading; by Fig 3 :22 it is *"a reading of nothing."*

**Falsifier:** show a working attribution design that has no effect-term vocabulary and no stated objective, and whose result survives an independent re-analysis. That would break the dependency claim.

**Verdict: `general`, single-source (Fig 3).**

## Q4 — What does a laboratory test substitute?

Fig 11 :66: *"Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."* Fig 11 :70: *"A test harness is a substituted habitat. Which is why a synthetic solve proves wiring and never capability."*

A laboratory task is a substituted habitat. It can demonstrate that a person can do the task under the lab's conditions; it cannot by itself show that the pattern in the natural habitat has the same cause. What is failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails, and what is unintentionally introduced is invisible until it acts (Fig 11 :68-69). The remedy is not to abandon the lab but to record the substitution and re-range the result against the original habitat. This is the figure-grounded reason for mixing laboratory work with ethnography and population data.

Fig 11 :52-53: *"Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."* An institutional effect three steps out is missed if only direct contact is enumerated.

**Falsifier:** show a substituted habitat that reproduces every condition the goal required, with no introduced actor and no silent gap, so that a lab result carries the natural-habitat attribution unchanged.

**Verdict: `specified`, single-source (Fig 11).** The Many Labs 2 result on heterogeneity across settings (world source) corroborates the practical consequence but is not a figure line.

## CONCLUSION

"Caused by" is an ordered bond, not a symmetric coexistence; writing it as `+` loses the causal claim. The three candidates are a functional group as written and isomers only over a shared atom set. "Interaction" is a compound, not a fourth atom, and it becomes composable at the next level only once settled. The dependency order puts effect-term vocabulary and objective before planning, and the middle link is the one without an instrument. A laboratory test substitutes a habitat, so it proves wiring and not capability in the original habitat. Every one of these is a `specified` constraint with a falsifier; none is a ground reading.
