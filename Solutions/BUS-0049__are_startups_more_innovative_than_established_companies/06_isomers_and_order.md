# Are startups and incumbents isomers? Union, order, and scale

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §8).

The scalar comparison fails (F1). Two questions are owed: (1) is the startup/incumbent difference a partition of one variable or a union of two conditions, and (2) under Fig 12's definitions, are the two forms isomers, a functional group, or neither? ## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :4 "Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."
- Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- The essay analogue: "Perhaps your problem was a bundle or conflation of several problems masquerading as one? You will need to search all of those problems individually to find their answers and then combine them in a satisfactory way."
- Damanpour 1992 splits initiation from implementation by finding a stronger size effect for implementation. Wu et al. 2019 split disrupt from develop by finding that "smaller teams have tended to disrupt... whereas larger teams have tended to develop existing ones".
- ⇒ **Union.** Two conditions, each with its own ground. Not a partition of one variable. Triangulated (Fig 9 plus the essay plus two independent empirical literatures), but the essay shares the framework author, so the independent weight comes from the two empirical sources.
- **Falsifier:** a single measure that predicts both conditions with the same sign on the same ground would collapse the union to a partition.

### Q2 — Does team size scale the same machine?

- Fig 4 :19 "Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change." Fig 4 :21 "The membrane is the boundary between the sizes, and the rule about what may cross it. Not a wall: a filter with a direction."
- Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."
- The kernel of innovating is invariant with size: bet, act, observe, settle. What changes is the actors (one founder versus a hierarchy), the currency (a cheap bet versus a defended budget), and the anchor (a new market versus an installed base). So "startup" and "incumbent" are two sizes of one machine, and the transform between them is lossy.
- Fig 4 :30 "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."
- ⇒ **Round trip gap R_T is nonzero.** What survives upward from a startup's practice is a method (many cheap probes). What a large firm gets when that method comes back down is a head start, not the recording of one particular startup's success. Copying a startup's visible artifacts without the method is the replay failure Fig 4 :25 names.
- **Falsifier:** show that a specific startup's artifact, replayed inside an incumbent, produces the same new result without the method. Then the transform is not lossy and R_T is zero.

### Q3 — Isomers or functional group?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- As the two forms are usually written, startup `{many cheap bets, loose process, new market}` and incumbent `{few large bets, tight process, installed base}` share almost no atoms, so the strict isomer reading fails; they are two members of a functional group, both "organizations that innovate", with different qualifiers.
- The stronger form: write both over the shared organizational atom set `{search, bet, observation, settled result, budget, habitat, seven bonds}`, differing only in which bond mediates which pair. Then they are genuine isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. The difference is invisible in the word "innovative" and decisive in what each produces. This is the claim worth testing.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either form can be written without an atom the other requires, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. This is the same test used in the exemplar for GR and QM.
- Additional strain: Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." A count of patents records ingredients and not the bond, so no patent count can by itself distinguish the isomers. This is the metamerism point of `05` §5 in structural form.

### Q4 — What is the order of the conditions?

- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces"
- Initiation precedes implementation in the life of an innovation; implementation consumes what initiation produces. The two conditions are not independent slots to be added. This is why a single lever, "fund the initiator more", clears the first condition and leaves the second standing.
- Fig 11 :61 "Introduction is available; exclusion is not." A habitat seat can introduce an actor to help implementation and cannot subtract the incumbent's installed base. The order is therefore not fully controllable, which is why "different constraints" rather than "different choices" is the honest wording.
- Wu et al. add the population-level order: small teams disrupt, and their work "succeed further into the future if at all", while large teams' work "comes immediately". The two conditions are ordered in time as well as in dependency.
- **Falsifier:** show that implementation can reliably precede initiation for the same innovation, with the same ground, and the dependency order is wrong.

### Q5 — What does the ecology analogue contribute, and what does it not?

- Fig 8 :53 "Residual first, frame second." The residual was described in §0 before r/K selection was brought in.
- Wikipedia's r/K article: "r-selected species are those that emphasize high growth rates, typically exploit less-crowded ecological niches, and produce many offspring, each of which has a relatively low probability of surviving to adulthood"; by contrast "In stable or predictable environments, K-selection predominates as the ability to compete successfully for limited resources is crucial".
- The mapping: r-strategy to many cheap bets in an unstable habitat; K-strategy to few large, competitive bets near carrying capacity. This corroborates the union and the habitat-relativity of capability from a closure that did not read management theory.
- Fig 11 :50 "The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds." r and K are not rankings of worth; they are relations to a habitat. So are the two organizational forms.
- **What it does not contribute:** a business number, a policy recommendation, or a derivation. The theory itself is criticised in the article that reports it, so the analogue is admitted at low weight. Round trip gap R_T is nonzero across the substrate change.

## CONCLUSION

The startup/incumbent question is a **union** of at least two ordered conditions, not a partition of one variable. The two forms are **two sizes of the same machine** under Fig 4, with a lossy transform and a nonzero R_T. Under Fig 12 they fail the strict isomer test as usually written and are a **functional group**; the stronger isomer claim requires both over a shared atom set and carries a stated falsifier. The ecology analogue is low-overlap corroboration for habitat-relative capability and carries its own round-trip gap. No scalar ranking is derived.
