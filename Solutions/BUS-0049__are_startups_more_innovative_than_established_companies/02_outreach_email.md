**Subject:** One shape claim about startup vs incumbent innovation, with its refuter

> Hello,
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a theory first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. I record your verdict on this output before showing any source, and your later agreement is never pooled as corroboration.
>
> **The residual, in effect terms.** The question "are startups more innovative?" puts two conditions on one axis: starting something new, and developing and implementing it. In the empirical literature those two conditions are not one quantity. A 1992 meta-analysis of organizational size and innovation reports that size is more strongly related to implementation than to initiation, and a 2004 replication reports that contradictory findings come from divergences in how the variables were measured. A 2019 Nature study of 65 million papers, patents and software products reports that smaller teams tend to disrupt while larger teams tend to develop, and that both are essential. The residual is a split, not a magnitude.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`).** Innovation is not one axis on which the two classes rank. The question is a union of conditions. Innovative capacity is a property of the firm and its habitat, never of the firm class alone. Structural features such as hierarchy, slack and process discipline have opposite valences for initiation versus implementation. Startups and established firms are better read as two arrangements that each do one condition well than as high and low values of one quality. No numerical ranking is claimed; that magnitude is an open ground question.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** Not that startups are always better, not that incumbents do not innovate, not that established firms are merely slow copies of startups, and not that this framework settles the empirical comparison. No measurement has been taken here. This is a shape and a route, not a result.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim, written before this message:**
> 1. a single innovation measure that the ground settles for both classes on the same basis and on which one class dominates across the relevant habitats, with a residual that trends down;
> 2. evidence that innovative capacity belongs to the firm class alone, so that moving the same people and resources between habitats leaves capability unchanged;
> 3. evidence that a structural feature has the same valence for initiation and implementation in both habitats.
>
> If any of these holds, I withdraw the shape claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures, not my summary of them, are the authority.
> - Meta-analysis of organizational size and innovation, Damanpour 1992, abstract: https://colab.ws/articles/10.1177%252F017084069201300304
> - Wu, Wang and Evans, "Large teams develop and small teams disrupt science and technology", Nature 2019: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-0941-9
> - March, "Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning", Organization Science 1991, record: https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v2y1991i1p71-87.html
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link in `04_links_and_distribution.md`. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> Thank you for your time,
> [name]
