# The Tether's answer on startups versus established companies

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the shape. Not `grounded`.** This is a shape and a route, not a result. Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §8), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

"Are startups more innovative?" is a bundle. Observed disagreements do not shrink with effort, and each measurement rule works well on a different subset. Fig 9 :57 "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either". The two conditions are initiation (new, radical, disruptive) and implementation (developing, refining, scaling). Option A collapses both into one score; Option B keeps them separate. The question is a union compound with its own loop and its own ground, not one hard question about a quality called innovativeness.

## 2. The shape

1. **The two conditions must be split before any ranking.** Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." A global innovation score throws away the only signal that says where to look. The 1992 and 2004 meta-analyses show why: the size effect is stronger for implementation than initiation, and contradictory findings track how the variables were operationalized.
2. **Capability belongs to firm plus habitat, not to firm class.** Fig 11 :39 "capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone". Fig 4 :19 "Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change." The loop of innovating is the same at both sizes; what changes is who bets, what currency pays, and what anchor settles. Wu, Wang and Evans report that smaller teams disrupt and larger teams develop, and that both are "essential to a flourishing ecology of science and technology".
3. **The same structural feature cuts both ways.** Fig 11 :49 "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries." Hierarchy, slack and process discipline help implementation and hurt initiation in the habitats studied. So "different strengths and constraints" is the accurate description; "more and less innovative" is not.
4. **The two forms are arrangements, not amounts.** Over a shared organizational atom set they are Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." As usually written they share little and are better described as Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member." Either way, the difference is in the bond, not the quantity of innovation.
5. **The numerical ranking is a gap, not a verdict.** Fig 9 :32 "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself". The figures narrow the answer's form; they return no patents-per-employee threshold. Fig 13 :134 "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence". This magnitude is reachable by a ground reading once an instrument discriminates the conditions, so it is a `gap`, not unreachable in principle.
6. **Size is a filter, not a witness.** Fig 9 :60 "Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict." Small and large tell you where to point an instrument. They do not tell you which class wins.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that startups are more innovative. That ranking is refused as an aggregation, not reversed.
- Not that incumbents do not innovate. They implement and develop; Wu et al. find that large teams build on recent, popular developments and get immediate attention.
- Not that this settles the empirical question. No measurement was taken and no patent count was read.
- Not a policy of funding one class over the other. Such a lever clears the region where one condition holds and leaves the other standing.
- Not a derivation of one organizational form from the other, and no new primitive.
- Not `grounded`. Nothing here is asserted as settled by experiment.

## 4. What would settle it, the ground

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument. From *The Hard Problem is About Source*: an instrument that reads green "cannot comment on the source"; the spectrometer, not an argument, resolved it. Here:

- **Discriminator test.** Does a proposed innovation metric separate initiation from implementation at all? A count that cannot is inadequate to the question, however precise.
- **Habitat test.** Does moving the same people and resources between a startup habitat and an incumbent habitat change the output? If not, capability is in the class and Fig 11 :39 is broken.
- **Valence test.** Does a given structural feature carry opposite signs for initiation and implementation on one ground? If it carries the same sign, the "different strengths" claim falls.
- **Isomer test.** Can both forms be written over the full shared atom set, differing only in arrangement? If one requires an atom the other lacks, the functional-group reading stands and the isomer claim is withdrawn.
- Standing falsifiers from `01`: one measure that settles both conditions for both classes with a downward-trending residual; class-only capability; feature valence independent of residual.

## 5. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable.** The magnitude is decidable by a defined metric and data. The framework's contribution is to refuse the aggregate and split the union before that metric is chosen. Fig 4 :30 "So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked." Any compression of the two conditions into one "innovation" score carries a round-trip gap R_T that must be measured, not assumed zero. Fig 2 :35 "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you." Agreement between management and science-of-science writers is one pool until a low-overlap witness is found; the ecology analogue supplies that witness at low weight. "A search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence" (Fig 8 :39), so the gap is recorded as a verdict.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> The Tether does not rank startups above established companies, and it does not claim the final empirical answer. It says the question is a bundle: starting something new and developing something existing are two conditions, not two scores on one axis, and a global innovation average throws away the only signal that says where to look. Innovative capacity belongs to the firm and its habitat, not to the firm class, so the same feature, hierarchy, slack, process discipline, helps one condition and hurts the other. The two forms are arrangements of the same organizational atoms, isomers over a shared set or at least members of one functional group, and the difference is in the arrangement, not the amount. What the framework offers is that shape, three falsifiers written in advance, and the name of the instrument that would close the magnitude: a metric that discriminates initiation from implementation and pays for both classes on one ground. The ranking stays open until that instrument is read.
