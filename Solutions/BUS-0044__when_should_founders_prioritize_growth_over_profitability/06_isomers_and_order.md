# Is "grow fast" and "grow efficiently" an isomer pair? Order, analogues and two currencies

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question offers two apparent strategies, "prioritize growth" and "prioritize profitability." Are these two members of one functional group (same structure, different atoms), two isomers (same atoms, different bond, different substance), or a false pair that should be unbundled? A second question follows: in what order must the decision loop run, and in which currencies is its price paid?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in D3 (from `01`, section 1.2): acquisition cost, retained customers, gross margin, payback period, founder labor, cash. Bonds held: the seven, including sequence `→`, production `⇒`, comparison `⋛` and subtraction `−`. Negation `¬` is available for the refusal of "growth is good." ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the two strategies isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Write the two strategies over the shared atom set `{cash, acquisition spend, retained customers, gross margin, time}`:

- Growth-first: `cash ⇒ acquisition spend ⇒ customers → margin`, with the comparison `⋛` applied to growth rate versus benchmark.
- Profit-first: `cash − acquisition spend → margin → cash`, with the comparison `⋛` applied to burn versus runway.

The two share **every atom** and differ in which bond is placed where: `⇒` (production) versus `−` (subtraction), and in what the comparison measures. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, not different degrees of one strategy. The difference is invisible in a list of inputs ("both spend money and sell things") and decisive in behaviour (one may compound, the other may starve).

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either strategy can be written without an atom the other requires, for example profit-first with no acquisition spend at all or growth-first with no margin atom, the isomer claim fails and the weaker functional-group reading stands instead. The reading is `specified`; no market measurement pays for it here.

### Q2 - What is the dependency order?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Fig 3 :24: **"Why the middle link is where chains usually break."**

Order: perception (cohort retention, cash) **→** vocabulary (payback, margin, retention, runway) **→** objective (what counts as value-creating growth) **→** planning (spend and hire) **→** learn and carry. The middle link is the one most often missing. Graham's observation that *"Half the founders I talk to don't know whether they're default alive or default dead"* is a link-1 failure with a link-2 cause: no term for the question, so no reading arrives.

**Falsifier:** a documented company whose perception, vocabulary and objective links all measure as working and whose failure is located at planning or execution. That would falsify "usually at the middle" while leaving the dependency order intact.

### Q3 - The low-overlap analogue: r-selected and K-selected strategies

This is an **analogue**, not a derivation. It is included because WATSON asks for low-overlap fields and because the ecology literature has an explicit vocabulary for growth versus efficiency under environmental constraint.

- The reference page on r/K selection states: *"In unstable or unpredictable environments, r-selection predominates due to the ability to reproduce rapidly."* It also states the K case: *"In stable or predictable environments, K-selection predominates as the ability to compete successfully for limited resources is crucial"*.
- The University of Minnesota open textbook (from OpenStax Biology 2e) says of r-selected species: *"This strategy is often employed in unpredictable or changing environments."*

Mapping in effect terms, with the map declared rather than hidden:

| ecology | startup analogue | figure it corroborates |
|---|---|---|
| unstable environment, density-independent mortality | winner-takes-all market with a closing window | Fig 7 :39, ground pressure on the room |
| r strategy: many cheap offspring, rapid reproduction | spend to acquire many users, accept low retention | Fig 12 :119, catalyst versus spent part |
| stable environment near carrying capacity | mature market with strong incumbents | Fig 11 :51, no standing list of good actors |
| K strategy: few costly offspring, high parental care | few high-value accounts, deep retention work | Fig 12 :124, functional group qualifier |

**What the analogue corroborates:** the paying strategy depends on the environment; there is no context-free winner. That matches Fig 11 :49 (*"An actor’s valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries"*) and F6.

**What the analogue does not license:** the page records that r/K theory *"lost importance in the early 1990s, when it was criticized by several empirical studies"* and was replaced by life-history theory. It is a contested heuristic, not a law. So this is a single-source, low-overlap corroboration of the **shape** (environment dependence), not evidence that a startup is an r- or K-strategist. Fig 9 :30-31 applies: *"your words are borrowed too, so describe the effect rather than the cause."*

**Falsifier for the analogue:** a market in which the same acquisition strategy wins at both the expansion and the saturation stage with no change in valence. That would break the environment-dependence claim here.

### Q4 - Why two currencies, and why "burn multiple" is only a filter

- Fig 12 :125: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."**
- Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."**

A growth plan is cheap in bits and expensive in actions. "Double the acquisition budget" is one sentence and a quarter of runway. A retention test over three cohorts is a longer description and may cost little. Conversely, a plan with a large description (a full financial model) can be cheap to settle if one cohort disproves it. Fig 12 :128-129 says the two readings come apart in both directions and summing them is the error.

"Burn multiple," the rule of 40, LTV/CAC and "default alive" are **bits**: compact descriptions of a state. The action price is the cash actually spent finding out whether the description holds. A filter can tell you what to try; it cannot tell you what paid (Fig 9 :60).

A separate, low-overlap warning comes from the measurement literature rather than from the figures. The Goodhart/Strathern rule is *"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"*. It names what happens when a bits-price is mistaken for a ground reading.

### Q5 - The ordered bond and the refusal

`→` is sequence: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :72). Detection must precede decision, so the correct composition is `measure → decide`, not `decide → measure`. Writing the reverse adds an ordering the figures do not license, the same notation error the exemplar found in its spacetime chain.

And `¬` is needed before `⇒`: the shape must first refuse the premise that growth is a good in itself. Operators table :58: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."* `¬` is a unary operator and is not one of the seven bonds (Fig 12 amendment), so the refusal does not enter the bond price. Practically: refusing "growth at all costs" costs one sentence, not a new metric.

## CONCLUSION

The two strategies are **isomers over a shared atom set** in the `specified` reading, differing in bond placement rather than ingredients, with a stated falsifier. The dependency order is `perceive → vocabulary → objective → plan → learn`, with the middle link the usual break. The r/K analogue from ecology corroborates environment dependence at low overlap but is a contested heuristic and is not promoted to evidence for any particular startup. Growth plans carry a bits-price and an action-price that do not add, so benchmark ratios are filters, never witnesses. The `¬` refusal comes before the `⇒` production of a strategy.
