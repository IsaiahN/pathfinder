## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are two overlapping layers isomers over the node set?

- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- In a multiplex, replica nodes in layer A and layer B are the same entities. The node set (the atoms) is shared; what differs is the bond arrangement (the edges). Two layers with the same node set and different edge sets are therefore Fig 12 isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance. This is why two layers can behave differently even though an adjacency-only description lists the same vertices.
- Fig 12 :96: *"Two entries can share every ingredient and be inverses of one another."* Two layers over the same nodes can carry opposite-sign effects, as the awareness/disease case shows (Granell 2013): awareness inhibits, infection enhances. Same atoms, different arrangement, opposite substance.
- Chemical analogue from the figure itself: Fig 12 :97-98, *"silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation. Liebig and Wöhler, 1824 to 1826; named isomerism by Berzelius, 1830."* This is the world-source analogue for why an aggregate adjacency matrix is not enough.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two layers with the same node set but demonstrably different edge arrangements produce identical spread outcomes for all four currencies under fixed transmission rules, the isomer claim fails and the layers are merely two spellings of one substance (Fig 12 :23, *"this bond is symmetric, so the four are one term"*). Triangulated for the figure definition; unmeasured for the network case.

### Q2 — What is the order of the composition?

- Fig 12 :30: `→` is *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, an ordered bond. Fig 13 :75: *"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."*
- The spread chain is `state(informed set) → structure(network) → state(new informed set)`, a cross-kind composition, legal under Fig 13 :74-75. Adding a layer inserts a second structure that joins only through the shared state (the nodes), not directly to the first structure. This is the figure-grounded reason inter-layer edges run between replica nodes: structures join through a state.
- Fig 11 :52-53 fixes the temporal order: first-order enumeration (direct contacts), second-order effect, residual three steps out. So intra-layer contact precedes inter-layer crossing precedes cascade. This is a dependency order, not an importance order (Fig 3 :20-21): *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."*
- Fig 13 :4-5: *"a crossing is a change of role rather than a step upward, and no member of the pair is senior to the other."* Switching layers is a change of role, not climbing to a higher kind of network. So a multiplex is not a hierarchy of layers; it is a lockstep alternation.
- **Falsifier:** if a compound's spread can be fully predicted without ordering intra-layer before inter-layer contact, the dependency-order claim fails. Single-source for the figure's chain rule; world corroboration from Radicchi 2015's intersection/remainder decomposition.

### Q3 — Which bond does overlap use?

- `+` is symmetric: *"both hold at once"* (Fig 12 :57), test *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."* (Fig 12 :59). A pair of layers with the same node set combined by `+` is one term under a symmetric bond.
- `∥` is *"either suffices; not both required"* (Fig 12 :62), test *"remove one. Does it still work? Yes."* (Fig 12 :64). A low-overlap compound in which either layer alone suffices to carry a simple contagion is a `∥` composition. This matches the Operators table warning: *"not concurrency. Two things running in parallel both matter; remove one and it breaks, which is +"* (Operators :22).
- `⋛` is *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* (Operators :50) with test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present?"* (Operators :51). An overlap coefficient compared against a percolation threshold is a `⋛` composition. A goal is a comparison (Operators :52), so stating "surplus is owed" is a comparison, not a new substance.
- Under high overlap the two layers are `≡` (identity), *"two names, one referent"* (Fig 12 :67); the union adds nothing, exactly Fig 8 :48-49's *"one frame wearing two names."* Under low overlap the bond is not identity and the union has surplus (Fig 8 :45).
- **Falsifier:** if the bond type cannot be identified by the six tests in Fig 12 :90-91 for a given measured compound, the bond classification is unearned and the claim is withdrawn to `general`.

### Q4 — Analogues and where they stop

- **Frame analogue (Fig 8):** two layers are two frames. Low overlap gives surplus; high overlap gives redundancy. This is the primary analogue and it decides.
- **Habitat analogue (Fig 11):** the union of layers is a habitat enumerated by contact, not composed. *"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."* (Fig 11 :44). *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* (Fig 11 :49). The same edge is a benefactor to information spread and a malfactor to disease control when awareness inhibits infection; mutualism and parasitism are relations, not kinds (Fig 11 :50).
- **Chemical analogue (Fig 12):** isomerism explains why the aggregate formula loses the substance. Corroborating only; it does not decide the network case.
- **Animal-behaviour analogue:** Ward and colleagues (PNAS, 2011) find *"division of labor combined with social information transfer"* in fish shoals, a low-overlap instance of the habitat-and-cascade shape.
- **Limit of analogues:** Fig 8 :53 *"Residual first, frame second."* The analogues were checked after the residual and the figure lines were stated, not used to pick the domain. Agreement from a shared pool is one witness (Fig 2 :40).

## CONCLUSION

Two layers over one node set are Fig 12 **isomers** when their edge arrangements differ: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The composition is ordered `state → structure → state`, with inter-layer crossing a change of role rather than a higher level, and the cascade following dependency order. The bond that decides the outcome is the overlap identity: `≡` under high overlap (no surplus), not-identity with a surplus path under low overlap. Each claim carries a written falsifier. Triangulated: the isomer and order claims from Fig 12, Fig 13 and Fig 3 (low overlap between the bond table and the dependency chain); the bond classification single-source for the figure tests, unmeasured for the network case.
