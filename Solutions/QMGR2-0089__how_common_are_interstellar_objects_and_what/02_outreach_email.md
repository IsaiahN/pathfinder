**Subject:** Check one shape claim about interstellar objects before you decide whether the method is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the question "how common are interstellar objects, and what can they reveal about other planetary systems?" is two overlapping conditions, not one. The count half and the reveal half have different grounds, so they must be unbundled before either is searched.
>
> 1. **Count.** The abundance residual is indexed by survey slot, not averaged into one scalar. The local density near 0.1 au^-3 comes from the characterized Pan-STARRS detection of 1I. The 2I survey has unpublished depth and area, so it cannot tighten that number. The 95 percent Poisson interval still spans orders of magnitude. "Common" is a comparison against an expected density, and that threshold is not grounded until the size-frequency distribution is constrained by more detections. So this half is unreachable at this budget: the remedy is more independent survey search, not a new kind of object.
> 2. **Reveal.** A composition reading constrains a class of parent systems, it does not name one parent star. CO and water production ratios, ammonia as a metallicity tracer, and isotope ratios narrow the class. Different formation routes can arrive at the same substance, so a reading does not determine a unique source. What is owed is the discriminator, not a name.
>
> This is a shape and a route, not a measurement. I am not claiming the number is settled or that any particular star is the parent.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that the 0.1 au^-3 density is a final answer; that the mass budget is 2 to 50 percent of stellar mass as a settled value; that 1I and 2I are chemical isomers; that 3I/ATLAS or 2I/Borisov has been traced to a specific star. Those are either budget-limited estimates or bindings from the astronomy frame, and they carry no evidentiary weight until their provenance and discriminators are shown. A framework returns a kind of thing, never the answer itself.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. a characterized survey yields a density with negligible Poisson and size-distribution uncertainty;
> 2. an invertible map from number density to ejected mass density is demonstrated, so the scaling gap is zero;
> 3. a composition vector identifies a unique parent star by measurement, not by preference;
> 4. count and source share one anchor, so they need not be unbundled.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any one holds, I will narrow or withdraw the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Interstellar Objects in the Solar System, David Jewitt, 2024 (arXiv:2407.06475): https://arxiv.org/html/2407.06475
> - The Interstellar Interlopers, Jewitt and Seligman, Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics, 2023: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-astro-071221-054221
> - On the Compositions of Interstellar Objects, Hopkins et al., 2026 (arXiv:2609.30827): https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30827
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link in the packet. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
