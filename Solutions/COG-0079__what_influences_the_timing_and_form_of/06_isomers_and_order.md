# Are agency, intention and responsibility isomers? And what order do they come in?

**Mode: `specified` on the child-facing claims, `general` on the structural claims, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks what influences the *form* of children's understanding of agency, intention and responsibility. "Form" is ambiguous between two readings: (1) the three concepts are three ingredients that get added to a mental inventory, or (2) the three concepts are the same ingredients under different typed relations. The figures decide between them. A second residual is the *order*: is the sequence a matter of chronological age alone, or is it a dependency chain whose middle link has no instrument?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting derivation)

Atoms held for this residual: agent, action, goal, outcome, belief. Bonds held: the seven plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. The ground is the independently measured social state. The habitat (caregivers, language, culture, cohort) supplies bindings and imports, not new atoms of the social-cognitive set.

## DERIVATION

### Q1. Are the three concepts distinguished by atoms or by bonds?

- Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
- Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands. A recipe that names only its ingredients has recorded half of itself."
- Fig 12 :94 "The arrangement is the substance."

The same atom pair, agent and action, can be bonded as production (the agent brings about the outcome), as comparison (the action matches or misses a goal), or as a comparison plus the refusal of the accidental reading. The atoms do not tell you which; the bond does.

### Q2. Which bond goes with which concept?

- Operators :37 "A produces B; B did not exist before" (production, `⇒`). This is agency: who made the cup fall.
- Operators :51 "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one" (comparison, `⋛`). This is intention: the outcome is compared against a goal state.
- Operators :73 "A vocabulary with − and without ¬ can say what is missing from an arrangement and cannot say that an arrangement is wrong, so it can only ever accumulate." This is responsibility: it needs the refusal of the "it was an accident" reading, not merely the absence of intent.

Fig 12 :112 "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction. A refutation is a negation, and that one is not a bond." So the three concepts are not three points on one scale of "more mental state". They are three different operator configurations over a shared set.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if each concept can be written only by adding a distinct atom (a special "responsibility concept", a special "intention concept") with no change of bond, the bond reading fails and the ingredient-list reading stands.

### Q3. Are they isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

**Strong form.** Write all three over the full shared atom set {agent, action, goal, outcome, belief}. They then have the same atoms and different bonds, so they are isomers under Fig 12 :117. Their behavioural signatures differ (who did it, whether it was meant, whether it was blameworthy), which is exactly "decisive in the behaviour".

**Weak form.** As the concepts are usually described in the literature, agency requires an agent and an outcome, intention adds a goal, and responsibility adds a counterfactual. They do not share the full atom set, so under the figure's definition they are not isomers; they share a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123), the `agent → outcome` core, with the qualifier deciding which member.

Both forms are checkable. The strong form is the real isomer claim; the weak form is the safe one. Asserting the strong form without holding the atom set fixed is the error Fig 12 :94 warns about.

**Falsifier:** if either concept requires an atom the other two do not and the full shared set cannot be written for all three, the strong isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim survives.

### Q4. What order does the dependency impose?

- Fig 3 :19 "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."
- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step"
- Fig 3 :25 "Perception has obvious failures and obvious tests. Planning has obvious failures and obvious tests. The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."
- Operators :58 "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."

The chain for each concept: perceive the event, have terms for what it affords (agent, goal, seeing), say what would count as progress (the comparison), plan the attribution, learn and carry. The middle link, having terms and an objective, is the one with no instrument. Responsibility adds a further dependency: the refusal operator `¬` must be available before "it was not an accident" can be asserted. So the predicted order is **agency (production) before intention (comparison) before responsibility (comparison plus refusal)**, with the break most often between vocabulary and objective, not at planning.

This is a dependency order, not a chronological schedule. Fig 7 :37 "Progress per handoff has two factors. Zero either and the chain runs in place:" Fig 7 :38 "the bracket's fidelity across the handoff" Fig 7 :39 "the ground's pressure on the room". A handoff occurs when the room changes (Fig 10 :72 "When the question changes, the room should change."), and two children of the same age can be at different links.

**Falsifier:** a child who passes an independently measured responsibility judgment before mastering the refusal operator; or a documented child with perception, vocabulary and objective links all measuring as working while the failure sits at planning (the second falsifies "usually at the middle" but not the dependency ordering).

### Q5. What do the world sources say about the order?

World sources are secondary and corroborate only; they never hand the verdict.

- Barone, Corradi and Gomila 2020 (Ciencia Cognitiva): development goes "from an intuitive sensitivity to others' intentions (what is that person going to do?) to the ability to develop complex explanations of the others' behaviors" and the nonverbal tasks "seem to rely on infants' capacity to detect simpler mental states, like intentions and states of seeing and not seeing." This corroborates the early-intention, late-explanation end of the order.
- Piaget 1932 (primary text read via search index): "Objective responsibility diminishes on the average" and "These two attitudes may co-exist at the same age and even in the same child" (consequence-based and intention-based judgment). This corroborates the late arrival of the comparison/refusal configuration and the coexistence of two configurations in one child, the isomer signature.
- Tomasello, Kruger and Ratner 1993 (Behavioral and Brain Sciences): "imitative learning, instructed learning, and collaborative learning – in that order." A different ordered progression in the same broad pool; it corroborates that contact-dependent learning is staged, not instantaneous.
- Elder 1998 (Child Development): "the developmental impact of a succession of life transitions or events is contingent on when they occur in a person's life." This is the life-course version of the same order claim, from a genuinely low-overlap pool.

No world source returns a specific age that would override the dependency reading. The order is a `specified` prediction, not a `grounded` schedule.

### Q6. Analogy check: what must not be imported

A tempting analogy is Piaget's stage sequence as a direct instance of Fig 3's five links. That is refused. Fig 3 governs a dependency chain of perception, vocabulary, objective, planning and learn-and-carry inside a frame; a stage sequence is a population-level description of when children pass tasks. They are different objects. The analogy is retained only as a shape match (ordered links, middle break), marked `single-source` on the figure side, and with the empirical fit left to a ground measurement. This is the same re-census discipline that in `01` retracted the mapping of the six physical terms onto social concepts (strain S1).

## CONCLUSION

Form is carried by bonds, not by an ingredient list. Under the full shared atom set the three concepts are isomers; as usually described they share a functional group and differ in the qualifier. The order is a dependency chain, agency then intention then responsibility, gated by the middle link that has no instrument and by the availability of the unary refusal operator. The strong isomer claim and the "usually at the middle" claim each have a stated falsifier. Everything here is `specified` and ungrounded; a child-level measurement with a source-discriminating instrument is needed before any part becomes `grounded`.
