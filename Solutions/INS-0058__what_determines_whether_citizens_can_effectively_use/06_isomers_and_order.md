# Union, order and isomers in the rights-use question

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question "what determines whether citizens can effectively use legal rights that formally exist?" bundles at least two conditions: a right is formally admitted, and a citizen can use it. Two questions are owed: (1) is this a partition or a union, and what does unbundling produce? (2) are two legal systems that hold the same formal rights but different procedural arrangements **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, so that the difference in effective use is carried by the bond rather than by the atoms?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Is this a partition or a union?

- Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55: "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."
- Formal existence and effective use overlap. A right can exist and be usable; it can exist and be inert. The two conditions also have different anchors: the enrolled text for the first, the realized remedy for the second (Fig 2 :29).
- ⇒ This is a **union**, not a partition. A single lever (passing more laws) clears the region where only formal existence is missing and leaves the intersection standing, because the second condition still binds (Fig 9 :55 to :56).
- **Unbundled residue:** the hard case is not "a right that is hard to use". It is a **compound of a formally admitted right and a closure that lacks the atoms to compose over it**. That is a specifically different object, and it needs its own loop and its own instrument.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a single lever applied to formal existence reliably changes effective use across all citizens and conditions, the two conditions were a partition after all and the union reading fails.

### Q2 — What order are the conditions in?

- Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces".
- Enactment is `⇒` production: the right did not exist before the act fired (Operators table :37: "A produces B; B did not exist before"). Using the right is `→` sequence: the claim must come before the remedy, and swapping them gives a different thing (Operators table :30: "A then B; B needs A to have happened").
- The order is therefore **production before sequence**: first the term is admitted, then a route is run. Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one." A statute names a state; the access conditions name the route.
- The break is between the vocabulary of affordance and the ability to say what counts as progress (Fig 3 :25), not between enactment and existence. Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
- **Falsifier:** if a route to a remedy can be run before the right is admitted and still counts as using *that right*, the production-before-sequence order is wrong.

### Q3 — Are the two systems isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**
- Take two jurisdictions with the **same formal right set** (same atoms: the same enrolled rights on paper) and a different arrangement of access: one supplies identity documents, a forum, representation and protection from retaliation; the other supplies the same words and none of those bonds.
- On the figure's definition these are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the statute formula and decisive in behaviour (one yields remedies, the other does not).
- This is stronger than saying "implementation matters". It says the effective right is a *different molecule* from the formal right, not a weaker instance of it, and that the difference is carried entirely by the arrangement. The formal text records the ingredients and not the bond, which is Fig 12's flat-formula collapse.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two systems with identical formal right sets but different procedural arrangements show **no systematic difference** in per-slot effective use, the isomer claim fails and the weaker functional-group reading stands instead (Fig 12 :124: "the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member"). A single jurisdiction where access reform changes nothing is not enough to fire this; the test is systematic across comparable systems.

### Q4 — What becomes an atom at the next level?

- Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**
- A right that a citizen has actually used, with the remedy obtained, is settled against the ground. It can therefore serve as an atom for a further claim: a pattern of successful use, a precedent, a collective entitlement, a habit of enforcement.
- A right that exists only on the books is admitted but not settled in this sense, so it cannot serve as that atom. This is why a catalogue of unused rights cannot be composed into a practice of rights. The world literature agrees but is secondary: Namati reports 75 plus changes in law and policy built from grassroots experience, that is, from settled molecules, not from proclamations.
- **Type caveat, still open.** Fig 12 :106: "Nothing composes over what was never admitted as a part." Admission is necessary; §F1 of `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` adds that it is not sufficient. Whether a used right can always serve as an atom at the next level depends on how it composes, not on what it paid. This needs an instrument, not an argument.

### Q5 — Comparison at the threshold

- The Operators table defines `⋛` as "a threshold relation between two quantities", with the test "is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."
- Comparing a formal right with an effective use is a comparison across two different levels of settlement. It is legitimate only if both have settled as terms at that level; the first is admitted, the second is ground-settled. The comparison is therefore **not symmetric in standing**, even though the bond is symmetric. Strain recorded, not hidden: the figure's symmetry refers to the bond's operands, while the standing difference refers to their provenance.

## CONCLUSION

The residual is a **union compound** that must be unbundled before it is searched (Fig 9 :54 to :56). Its dependency order is production before sequence (Fig 3 :20; Operators :30, :37), and the unmeasured break is at the affordance link (Fig 3 :27). Two jurisdictions with the same formal rights but different access arrangements are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117, same atoms, different bond, different substance, with the difference carried by the arrangement and invisible in the statute formula. A right that has actually been used becomes an atom at the next level under Fig 12 :104; a paper right does not. The isomer claim is checkable and carries a falsifier; it is `specified`, not `grounded`.
