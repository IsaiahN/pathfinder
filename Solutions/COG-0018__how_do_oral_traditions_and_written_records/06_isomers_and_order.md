### Q2 - What bonds organize a remembered past?

- Fig 12 :30: sequence →, *"A then B; B needs A to have happened."* This is chronology: the order of episodes, with the earlier one a precondition for the later one being remembered in that position.
- Fig 12 :36-37: production ⇒, *"A produces B; B did not exist before."* This is causal organization: one episode brings about another. It is not sequence, because the second operand is absent until the first fires (Operators :40: *"different from sequence: the second operand is not waiting, it is absent until the first fires"*).
- Operators :48-51: comparison ⋛, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*; *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one."* This is the comparison of two eras or two accounts against a threshold.
- Operators :58: negation ¬, *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."* This is the refused date or the rejected causal claim. A memory vocabulary without it can only accumulate legends.
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** A remembered past with the form *episode → episode* is a functional group; the specific episodes are the members.

Bartlett's "effort after meaning" is the cognitive analogue: recall fits the strange into a familiar schema, and the schema becomes "not merely something that works the organism, but something with which the organism can work" (p. 206, quoted in section 9 of the audit). Ong's "additive," "aggregative" and "redundant" characteristics of orally based thought describe a bond vocabulary weighted toward + and → rather than ⇒ and ⋛; that is a `specified`, unmeasured claim, not a figure line.

### Q3 - Are the oral and written memories isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Take the same remembered atom set: the same episodes, dates and agents, held by the same community. Write one account as a strict chronology (→ bonds), another as a causal narrative (⇒ bonds), a third as a comparison of eras (⋛ bonds). Same atoms, different bond, different substance. By Fig 12's definition these are **isomers**. The carrier is irrelevant to the isomer test; this is the F7 re-census applied.
- **Weaker reading.** If the carrier selects different atom sets (a chronicle records dates that an oral epic omits, and the epic records relations the chronicle omits), the two are **not** isomers over the full set. They share a functional group (Fig 12 :124) and differ in atoms. This is the honest weaker claim where the atom sets are not shown equal.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the two accounts can be written over the same atom set with the same bond and the same behaviour, the isomer claim fails; if no shared atom set can be found at all, the weaker functional-group reading stands instead. Neither is decided here because no account pair was measured.

### Q4 - What is the dependency order? (Fig 3)

The dependency chain for "organize the past" runs: perception of an episode (Fig 3 :4-5) → a vocabulary of bonds that can express what the episode affords (Fig 3 :6-8) → an objective, what would count as an organized past in the community's own terms (Fig 3 :9-11) → a plan for the narrative → learning and carrying it (Fig 3 :14-16). Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**

The middle link (vocabulary of bonds to objective) is where the chain usually breaks (Fig 3 :24-27): having terms for what a remembered episode affords has been taken for a dictionary when it is a grammar (Fig 12 :115). *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* (Fig 3 :27). A culture can hold many remembered episodes and have no term for progress through them; that is a link problem, not a storage problem.

### Q5 - Catalysts, cycles and functional groups in a tradition

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."** A mnemonic formula or a ritual frame is a catalyst: it survives its own use and makes the next telling cheaper. A one-occasion verbatim script is a spent part. Ong's "the remembrance of songs sung" is the catalyst reading; a fixed archive entry that must be re-read from scratch each time is the spent-part reading.

Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."** An oral tradition whose only output is the next telling is a chain that cannot close; it becomes a mirror chain (Fig 7 :36) unless a ground reading re-enters as input. Writing can supply the external input that closes the loop, but only if the text is ranged against something outside itself.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed set of remembered atoms, a chronological account, a causal account and a comparative account are isomers: same atoms, different bond, different substance (Fig 12 :117). Where the atom sets differ, the weaker claim is a shared functional group (Fig 12 :124). The carrier is not a bond; it is the membrane through which the molecule travels, and applying the isomer test to the medium instead of the arrangement is the strain corrected in Q1 and in F7 of the audit. The dependency chain breaks, if anywhere, at the bond vocabulary, and the mnemonic formula is the catalyst that makes an oral tradition regenerative.
