# Are the feedback slots isomers, and in what order do they move?

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The audit establishes that climate sensitivity should be reported as per-slot signed comparisons, and that the slots couple by contact cascades (Fig 11 :52). Three supporting questions are owed: (1) is a feedback structurally a cycle or a chain, (2) do ice, clouds, forests and oceans stand to one another as isomers or as members of a functional group, and (3) in what dependency order should a diagnostic read them, and what does a threshold compare?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Cycle or chain?

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- Fig 13 :87: **"The residual of the turn just closed is what the next turn composes against, so no two passes begin from the same place."**
- The ice-albedo case reported by Lenton et al. 2008 is a cycle: *"As sea-ice melts, it exposes a much darker ocean surface, which absorbs more radiation–amplifying the warming."* The output (warming) re-enters as input (more melt).
- The global-mean narrative "forcing → mean temperature → new ECS scalar" is a chain. A chain cannot close by construction, so it cannot represent the thing that changes.
- **Conclusion:** the structure that governs a change in sensitivity is the cycle, and the sign of the loop is the quantity to report. The scalar narrative is a chain and is structurally incapable of closing. Mode `general` for the structure, `specified` for its application to these slots.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** a documented feedback whose output re-enters as input and whose measured effect on sensitivity is fully captured by a one-pass chain with no re-entry. That would falsify the cycle reading for that slot.

### Q2 - Isomers or functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** Fig 12 also records the chemistry case: *"silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties"*.
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As the question names them, ice, clouds, forests and oceans have different actor sets and different currencies (albedo, radiative flux, carbon uptake, heat uptake). They share only the *substructure* "a state change produces a flux change that re-enters the energy budget". Under Fig 12 :123 that makes them members of one **functional group**, not isomers. The group says kind (feedback loop); the qualifier says member (ice, cloud, land, ocean).
- The stronger form: written over the full shared atom set, each slot is a `state → structure → state` molecule. Same atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers** at that level.
- This is a distinction with a consequence, not a label. If the slots are only a functional group, a single lever cannot be transferred between them. If they are isomers over a shared atom set, a method that moves one can be reused on another after re-deriving the bond, which is exactly what Fig 4 :22 permits: *"Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."*
- **Falsifier:** either theory cannot be written as `state → structure → state` at first order (kills the isomer claim and leaves the functional-group claim), or two slots are found to share all atoms and all bonds and yet differ in behaviour (kills the figure's isomer definition).

### Q3 - Dependency order

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."** Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."**
- Diagnostic order for EAR-0012: 1 perception (is the slot's flux measured? CERES, Argo, flux towers) → 2 vocabulary (does the frame have a term for the changed regime?) → 3 objective (is there a per-slot residual that stops trending down?) → 4 planning → 5 learn and carry. The middle link is where IPCC Ch7 reports the gap: *"there is currently insufficient evidence to quantify a likely range of the magnitude of future changes to current climate feedbacks."*
- Cascade order among slots is **not** a fixed temporal sequence. It is the order in which contact stops mattering (Fig 11 :44). Lenton et al. give distinct observation times: Arctic summer sea ice about 10 years (rapid), Amazon rainforest about 50 years (gradual), Atlantic thermohaline circulation about 100 years (gradual), Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets greater than 300 years (slow). A diagnostic that orders by these times is ordering by persistence of the residual, not by importance.
- A second-order coupling can invert a naive order: ice loss can change ocean circulation, which changes rainfall, which changes forest stress, which changes the carbon sink. The 2026 EGUsphere short summary on polar sea ice reports *"The same global temperature can therefore correspond to different sea-ice states, showing that climate history matters for recovery."* History is an operand of the comparison, so the order cannot be read off a single snapshot.
- **Falsifier:** a documented case in which the slow slot moves the fast one with no measurable change in the fast slot first, across a well-observed interval. That would falsify a cascade-only ordering and require an additional primitive relation.

### Q4 - What does a threshold compare, and is it reversible?

- Fig 12 :87: **"a threshold relation between two quantities"** (⋛). A tipping point is not a thing; it is a comparison of a control parameter against a critical value.
- Lenton et al. define it: *"The term 'tipping point' commonly refers to a critical threshold at which a tiny perturbation can qualitatively alter the state or development of a system."* They list critical values for Arctic sea ice, the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the Atlantic thermohaline circulation, monsoons, and Amazon and boreal forest, while noting that *"Many of the systems we consider do not yet have convincingly established tipping points."*
- The resilience literature adds a structural warning from a low-overlap field: MathisonSlee et al. 2022 report Proposition 12 as **"Social-ecological systems have multiple interacting thresholds, giving rise to multiple pairs of alternate regimes, only a few of which are feasible."** A single threshold per slot is an oversimplification; the union has interacting thresholds (Fig 9 :57).
- Hysteresis means the comparison includes history: the EGUsphere sea-ice summary states *"The same global temperature can therefore correspond to different sea-ice states, showing that climate history matters for recovery."* Under Fig 12 :87 that is still a ⋛ relation, but the second operand is a path-dependent quantity, not a scalar control alone.
- **Falsifier:** a threshold at which the control value gives the same state approached from both directions, with no hysteresis, for every candidate element in the Lenton list. That would remove path-dependence as an operand and simplify the comparison.

## CONCLUSION

The four named systems are members of one **functional group** as the question names them, and **isomers** over the shared atom set when written as first-order cross-kind molecules. They are realized as **cycles**, not chains, so the sign of re-entry is the reportable quantity. Dependency order is by measurement availability and by how far the contact cascade reaches, not by importance or by a fixed timetable. Thresholds are ⋛ comparisons whose second operand may include history. Each of the four claims carries a falsifier above.

This framing rescues what is defensible in the question without inventing a seventh primitive or a single future ECS number. The specific critical value for any slot remains a `gap` relative to the figures: it is measured by instruments, with provenance, and is not compositionally derivable from the six-term set.
