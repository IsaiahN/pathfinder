## 0. The residual, stated in effect terms

The question asks for a **rate**: how much of each of three outputs (crop productivity, water retention, ecosystem resilience) is lost per unit time under a degradation stress (erosion, salinization, compaction, organic-matter loss, nutrient depletion, contamination). In effect terms:

> Given a soil system under one or more degradation processes, what is the time-to-detectable-effect and the slope of decline for each of three distinct outputs, each measured by its own instrument?

This is not one residual. It is a bundle of at least three slots, plus a degradation-driver dimension (several processes acting at once). "How quickly" is a rate in units of effect per unit time, not a yes/no and not a quantity one instrument reads.

**What an answer would look like, bound before the run:** three separately anchored rate statements, each with actors, currency, anchor, mode and refuter. Binding this shape now makes it refusable later (formula step "Direction": a frame cannot check the direction of its own question unless the sought-for shape is made an ordinary term).

---

## 1. R by arity, symmetry and scale

- **Arity:** three output slots (productivity, water retention, resilience) plus a multi-process driver. Not a scalar.
- **Symmetry:** asymmetric. Decline and recovery are not mirror processes. Fig 13 :22 calls a gradient "a difference that can be spent"; spending is directional. Fig 11 :40 says an improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, so adding an amendment is not the reverse of removing structure.
- **Scale:** plot (field trials) through landscape to globe (gridded models); annual yield response through decennial and centennial soil-stock change. Different slots live at different scales.

## 4. Findings

Every substantive claim below carries a mode. `general` = structural, from the figure set. `specified` = a binding of soil terms onto figure terms, falsifiable. `ground` = settled by a measurement, which the figures do not provide.

### F1 — The three outputs are three slots, and averaging them destroys the signal (decisive)

Fig 1 :19: **"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."**
Fig 1 :22: **"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."**
Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**
Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."**

Mode: `general` (structural), triangulated across Fig 1 and Fig 9, two figures with different subject matter and low overlap (an agent's bookkeeping versus the leave/arrive procedure). The *Library of Babel* essay (opened 2026-11-22) corroborates: "Perhaps your problem was a bundle or conflation of several problems masquerading as one? You will need to search all of those problems individually." That is one author and therefore a shared evidence pool, so it is recorded as corroboration, not as a second independent witness.

Finding: a single "speed of soil degradation" is not a well-formed readout. Productivity can hold while water retention falls (nutrient compensation via fertilizer), and resilience can fall while yield holds for a season (loss of soil biota with a standing fertilizer subsidy). Any answer that reports one number for the three outputs has committed the Fig 1 :22 error.

**Refuter:** show that one scalar (for example soil organic carbon debt) predicts all three outputs with equal fidelity, such that slot-specific measurement adds no residual anywhere. If that holds, the three-slot claim fails and a single number is legitimate.

### F2 — The rate is a ground question; the figures return a shape, and the number is owed from outside

Fig 2 :29: **"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks. A measurement, an experiment, a counter, a market, a patient outcome."**
Fig 1 :23: **"The ground enters as the only metric."**
Fig 9 :32: **"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"**
Fig 13 :134: **"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence"**

Mode: `general` for the claim that the rate is not figure-derivable; `specified` for the claim that soil-science measurement is the appropriate frame to import. Triangulated across Fig 2, Fig 1 and Fig 9 (three figures, low overlap: anchoring, agent bookkeeping, leave/arrive).

Finding: the figures decide the **form** of the answer (three slots, ground-anchored, unaveraged) but contain no rate constant. Closure is idempotent, so searching the figures longer cannot add a number (Fig 8: *"no search, however long, adds an atom"*). The number must be imported from soil science (D5) with a source gate and a shadow test. Underivable from the figure frame is not unknowable: *"underivable is not unknowable"* (Fig 8 :54). Verdict on the numeric rate from the figures: **gap**. This is a verdict, not a silence.

**Refuter:** produce a rate for any one slot derived from the figure composition alone, with no empirical input, that matches a measured rate within its uncertainty band. If that succeeds, the gap verdict fails.

### F3 — Underivable and unreachable-at-budget are different facts here

Fig 8 :40: *"Underivable-in-principle and unreachable-at-this-budget are different facts, and they issue different instructions."*
Fig 8 :39: **"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."**
Fig 6 :30: *"outward: does another frame already hold it? The description travels. Fails by being sharpened into terms only we can read. Cast it in effect terms."*

Mode: `general`, single-source for the distinction (Fig 8), corroborated by Fig 6 (same author, shared evidence pool).

Finding: this question is **underivable in principle from the figures** (they hold no soil physics), not merely hard to find in them. The instruction is therefore OUTWARD import (soil-science measurement), not INWARD search of the figure set. Importing a rate "against a budget limit" would add an atom that was not needed; here there is no such limit, the atom is genuinely owed (Fig 8 :42). Describing R in effect terms first matters: if we name "soil degradation" as a single thing before unbundling, any field we pick will seem to fit (Fig 9 :27-28).

**Refuter:** show that a rate for all three slots is already in the figure closure and was merely unfound (a reachability/budget fact). Then the remedy would be more search, not import, and this finding fails.

### F4 — Degradation effects cascade beyond first contact; resilience is the three-steps-out slot

Fig 11 :43: **"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."**
Fig 11 :44: **"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."**
Fig 11 :52: **"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."**
Fig 11 :54: **"Carson, Silent Spring, 1962. The mechanism, biomagnification through the food chain to eggshell thinning, was established over the following decade."**

Mode: `general` (structural), **single-source** for the figure verdict (Fig 11 only). The Carson citation is the figure's own provenance, so it is not an independent world corroboration of the figure line. A genuine low-overlap world corroboration is Rattan Lal (2023, *Welthungerhilfe Global Food Journal*, opened 2026-11-22): "Excessive soil compaction restricts root penetration, water infiltration and retention, and gaseous exchange." That is a first-contact physical effect; the figure's claim is that the resilience effect sits further out, which the FAO newsroom (2025) supports with "land degradation leads to a loss of habitat and a decline in species diversity, negatively impacting ecosystems and their ability to provide essential services and making them more vulnerable to drought."

Finding: ecosystem resilience is not the same slot as crop productivity. It is reached through the food web and vegetation cover, which are second- and third-order contacts from the soil physical change. Scoping only to the first contact (yield) is how the resilience effect is missed, exactly the DDT/eagles error the figure names.

**Refuter:** demonstrate that degradation impacts on resilience are strictly first-contact, with no measurable second- or third-order pathway. Then the cascade claim fails and resilience can be folded into the yield slot.

### F5 — Management is permanent work; introduction is available, exclusion is not

Fig 11 :61: **"The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not. You can add an actor and you cannot subtract the habitat, because contact is a fact about the world and does not consult you."**
Fig 11 :63: **"Which is why a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state."**

Mode: `general`, **single-source** (Fig 11). World corroboration (low overlap, different evidence pool): the FAO *Status of the World's Soil Resources* technical summary (2015, opened via search extract 2026-11-22): "Even if the inputs are available, the restoration of productivity on degraded soils can be difficult because soils may have been degraded to the point where they cannot readily respond to fertility-improving management techniques." And Rattan Lal (2023): "reaching a new equilibrium of SOC stock may take up to 80 years after land-use change."

Finding: recovery is not the reverse of decline. You can add organic matter or cover (introduction) but you cannot subtract the altered habitat or simply rewind the clock. This is why reported recovery times (decades) are not the mirror image of decline times, and why a rate quoted in one direction cannot be inverted to get the other.

**Refuter:** show a managed soil system that returns to its exact prior state by an exclusion operation (removing an actor or restoring the habitat without continued introduction), with the same anchor reading before and after. Then the asymmetry claim fails.

### F6 — The decline is directional, and the figure's "gradient" is a spendable difference, not a rate

Fig 13 :22: **"a difference that can be spent"**
Fig 11 :40: **"A system cannot be made more capable by improving the agent in isolation. An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."**

Mode: `specified` for mapping soil fertility/structure onto Fig 13's gradient. **Strain test:** the gradient line governs the *directionality* of degradation (a stored difference being drawn down), NOT the numerical slope. An earlier reading tried to derive the rate from "gradient" and was retracted (see §8). Reading it as a rate would be a strain, so it governs another quantity and the mapping is re-censused here to structure only.

Finding: degradation spends a difference (structure, organic matter, nutrient capital). "How quickly" is the rate at which that difference is spent, which is a property of the specific soil and its contact with drivers, measured by an instrument. The figure licenses the direction, not the slope.

**Refuter:** show a degradation process in which no spendable difference is drawn down (a pure state relabelling with no loss of capacity). Then the gradient mapping fails.

### F7 — Divergent reported rates are a metamerism problem: several sources, one reading

Fig 10 :24: **"the ground does not decay, the channel does"**
Fig 2 :46: *"Each frame is corrected by it separately, and what makes the correction sound is that neither frame can move it."*

Mode: `specified`. Single-source for the channel/ground distinction (Fig 10), corroborated by *The Hard Problem is About Source* (essay, opened 2026-11-22): "If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information," and "An instrument that can't distinguish these cases demonstrates its own inadequacy." Same author, shared evidence pool, so corroboration only.

Finding: world sources report very different numbers (see `07` and `09`): roughly 0.4 percent per year global yield loss attributed to erosion; a 1.75 percent increase in yield gap per 10 percent increase in degradation; median 3 percent annual yield reduction in affected grid cells; up to 80 years to a new soil-carbon equilibrium. These are not contradictory answers to one question. They are readings of different slots at different scales through different instruments, and the reading does not determine the source. What is owed is an instrument that discriminates among slots, not an average of the numbers.

**Refuter:** show that all reported numbers are estimates of the same quantity on the same scale, so their disagreement is measurement error around one true rate rather than metamerism. Then the "different slots" reading fails.

### F8 — Use world numbers as filters for the budget and witnesses per slot, never as a single verdict

Fig 9 :60: **"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."**
Fig 2 :42: **"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."**

Mode: `general`, triangulated across Fig 9 and Fig 2 (low overlap).

Finding: a literature number decides which slot gets measured first (a budget filter), not whether the shape claim is true. Because the cost of being wrong about resilience is high (it is the three-steps-out slot, easy to miss), independence should be bought there first: a soil ecologist reading the residual without the figure interior, not a second model trained on the same dataset.

**Refuter:** show a procedure in which a literature rate is itself the verdict for all three slots. If that is legitimate, the filter/witness split fails.

## 5. Strain test and re-census

1. **Fig 13 gradient read as a rate ( strained ).** Initially mapped "a difference that can be spent" directly onto "how quickly." That strained the line: a gradient is directional structure, not a slope in time. Re-census: it governs the *asymmetry* (decline is directional, F6), while the rate goes to D5 as an import (F2). After re-census, no strain.
2. **Fig 11 habitat read as D2's yield slot ( strained ).** Initially the "habitat" seemed to be just the crop field. That strained the cascade lines, which require contact chains reaching biodiversity three steps out. Re-census: habitat governs D3 (resilience) and the management asymmetry, not yield. After re-census, no strain.
3. **Fig 1 slot language read as measurement slots ( checked, not strained ).** Fig 1's "object slot" is an agent's tracked object, not a soil sample. The mapping is a structural analogy (`specified`), not a claim that the figure was written about soil. Kept with mode attached.
4. **Fig 9 union vs partition ( checked, not strained ).** The three outputs overlap (shared drivers), so union is the correct reading, not partition. No strain.

After re-census: no hidden strains. The one mapping that strained (gradient as rate) was re-censused and is recorded above.

## 6. Pre-registered refuter (written before the conclusion)

Per the seat map's rule that a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, the shape claim is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. **Slot test.** A single scalar is shown to predict all three outputs with equal fidelity, so that slot-specific measurement adds no residual anywhere (breaks Fig 1 :19, :22).
2. **Ground test.** A rate for any slot is derived from the figure composition alone, with no empirical input, matching a measured rate within uncertainty (breaks Fig 2 :29, Fig 13 :134).
3. **Cascade test.** Degradation impacts on resilience are shown to be strictly first-contact, with no second- or third-order pathway (breaks Fig 11 :52).
4. **Asymmetry test.** A managed soil returns exactly to its prior state by exclusion, with the same anchor reading before and after (breaks Fig 11 :61, :63).

If any fires, the shape claim is withdrawn or narrowed. Until one is run, no part of this may be labelled `grounded` by the figures.

## 7. Recommendation

1. Report three rates, one per slot, each with its own anchor and mode. Never publish a single "speed of soil degradation" (F1).
2. State plainly that the figures give the shape and the ground gives the number; import the number with a source and a shadow test (F2, F3).
3. Buy the first independent check in the resilience slot, where the cost of being wrong is highest (F4, F8).
4. Keep decline and recovery rates separate; do not invert one to get the other (F5).
5. Present divergent literature numbers as readings of different slots, and ask for a discriminating instrument rather than an average (F7).
6. Lead outreach with the residual and the refuter, never with the framework (Fig 10 :52: *"Bring a characterised residual, never the source."*).
