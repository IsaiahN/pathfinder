## Four things in the wider literature that bear directly on this question

1. **The numbers measure different slots.** Carr et al. (2021) report a median 3 percent annual yield reduction in *affected* grid cells; Hadi et al. (2026) report a 1.75 percent yield-gap increase per 10 percent degradation across all croplands; the FAO soil-erosion assessment reports roughly 0.4 percent per year global yield loss from erosion; Lal (2023) reports up to 80 years to a new soil-carbon equilibrium. These are not rival estimates of one speed. They are readings of different slots, instruments and scales. Per *The Hard Problem is About Source*, an instrument that "can't distinguish these cases demonstrates its own inadequacy." Ask which slot a number reads, not which number wins.
2. **Water retention is its own slot.** Lal (2023) notes that compaction "restricts root penetration, water infiltration and retention"; the SOIL journal listing reports water-retention gains building over two decades of conservation agriculture. Water retention changes on its own timescale and must not be inferred from yield.
3. **Resilience is the cascade slot.** The FAO newsroom reports degradation "leads to a loss of habitat and a decline in species diversity... making them more vulnerable to drought." That is the second- and third-order contact Fig 11 :52 names, not a yield effect.
4. **Recovery is not the reverse of decline.** FAO's soil-resources summary says restoration "can be difficult because soils may have been degraded to the point where they cannot readily respond to fertility-improving management techniques," and Lal gives an 80-year equilibrium time. This is the ground counterpart of Fig 11 :61, *"Introduction is available; exclusion is not."*
