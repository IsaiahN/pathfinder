# Are the two learning regimes isomers? Structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The main audit shows that both regimes import vocabulary rather than a capacity, and that their failures are symmetric. Two structural questions are owed:

1. Are formal schooling and informal learning **isomers** over one atom set, or only members of the same **functional group**?
2. What **order** does each impose on the atoms, and does that order explain the different failure modes in F4 and F5?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms available for a learning episode: a learner, a vocabulary of evidentiary terms, a bond to the ground, a habitat, a correction loop, and a carry-forward. Bonds available: the seven (three symmetric, four ordered), plus negation. No new primitive is admitted; the figure set is closed and the arrangements are not.

One domain only for this derivation: the structure of a learning episode. Currency: what composes and in which order. Anchor: the verbatim bond definitions in Fig 12 and the Operators table. Verdict at the end is per claim, never averaged.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Same atoms, different bond?

Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*

Described at the same level, both regimes contain the same six atoms. The differences are in the bonds:

| atom | formal schooling | informal learning |
|---|---|---|
| vocabulary | explicit, stated before the referent, examinable | tacit, demonstrated, largely unstated |
| bond to ground | criterion and certification | practical consequence in the habitat |
| habitat | extracted from daily life, bounded by a curriculum | the daily setting itself, unenumerated |
| correction | delayed, by an answer key | immediate, by the material and the practitioner |
| carry-forward | credential and rule | rebuilt skill, "you had to be there" |

So the stronger claim is available: over the shared atom set they are **isomers** in the figure's sense. The formula (a list of ingredients) cannot tell them apart; the bond does, and the behaviour differs decisively. The "same atoms" is not a rhetorical concession: Scribner and Cole found the same basic capacities in all groups, with differences in the *functional learning systems* built from them.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either regime requires an atom the other does not (a capacity, a primitive, a kind of ground), the isomer claim fails and the two are different kinds of thing, not rearrangements.

### Q2 — The weaker claim: one functional group

Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

Both regimes share the substructure: **import terms → test against something → carry what worked**. That substructure fixes the kind (a learning system). The qualifier fixes the member (criterion-bonded or consequence-bonded). As normally described, the shared-substructure claim is the safer one, because it does not require the two regimes to be enumerated at identical granularity.

So there are two defensible claims, and they should not be collapsed:

- **Weak:** same functional group, two members. Single-source for the figure definition, but robust.
- **Strong:** isomers over a shared atom set. Requires the enumeration in Q1 to be faithful; `specified`, not grounded.

### Q3 — Order: sequence versus production

The Operators table defines → (sequence) as *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* and ⇒ (production) as *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*. Both are **ordered** bonds, so under Fig 12 :92 the order is part of the term: *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."*

Scribner and Cole report the two orders directly. Informal learning *"proceeds in the main by demonstration without accompanying verbal statements of 'rules' or 'principles'"*: the learner sees instances, and *"from these accumulated instances he acquires some generalized ways of performing the activity"*. That is ⇒, production: the generalised way did not exist before the instances fired. Formal teaching *"frequently begins with a verbal formulation of a general rule or a generalized verbal description"*: that is →, sequence. The rule comes first and the referent is supposed to arrive later.

This predicts the two failure modes without adding a new atom:

- If the sequence is `rule → referent` and the referent never arrives, you get *"empty verbal constructs"* (F4).
- If the production is `instances ⇒ skill` and the skill is never articulated, you get *"inarticulated practical constructs"* (F5).

Same atoms, different ordered bond, different substance. Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* A list of the two regimes' ingredients cannot predict their failure modes; the bond can.

### Q4 — Cycle versus chain, and the catalyst

Fig 12 :121: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."*

Informal learning has the shape of a cycle: attempt, correction, attempt, with the output re-entering as input. *Decoding Tacit Knowledge* describes it as a two-way loop in which the learner must act so their understanding is visible enough to be corrected. The master is Fig 12 :119's catalyst: *"A part that survives its own use."*

Formal schooling, as idealised for assessment, has the shape of a chain: instruction, test, credential. The chain terminates at the credential, so it cannot close by construction. This is why a convention can decide the outcome: a terminal link has no return path from the world.

**Census correction, recorded rather than smoothed:** Scribner and Cole explicitly say they were *"positing an idealized version of school learning, not describing learning as it actually occurs in a New York City school or in a Mexican village school."* Real classrooms contain cycles (feedback, revision). So Q4 is a claim about the *assessment chain*, not about every classroom. The earlier phrasing "formal schooling is a chain" is retracted in favour of "the credentialing chain that sits on top of schooling is a chain." A classroom that returns work for revision is a cycle and Q4 does not govern it.

### Q5 — Recursion: each settled regime becomes an atom at the next level

Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."*

Once a person's expectation is settled by a ground reading, it can be composed over: it becomes a term in the next learning episode. This is why E is learnable (F1) and why the two regimes can be compared at all. It also means that neither regime is irreducible in itself; "irreducible" is a fact about the level at which the comparison is made.

## CONCLUSION

Over the shared atom set the two regimes are a real isomer claim with a stated falsifier; at minimum they are two members of one functional group. Their difference is an **ordered-bond** difference (production → skill for informal learning, sequence → referent for formal instruction), and that difference predicts the symmetric failure modes in F4 and F5. The correction loop makes informal learning a cycle; the credentialing layer on top of schooling is a chain. The cycle reading is general; the chain reading is restricted to the assessment layer after a re-census. Neither claim requires a seventh term or an import that moves the wall.
