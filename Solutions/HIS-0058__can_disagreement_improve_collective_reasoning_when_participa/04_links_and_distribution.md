## 3. Wider literature that bears on the question

Seven substantive world sources were opened. Each is secondary corroboration; none overrules a figure. The field labels show the intended low overlap.

1. **Mathematics / economics, agreement theorem.** Wikipedia, "Aumann's agreement theorem": "two Bayesian agents with the same prior beliefs cannot 'agree to disagree' about the probability of an event if their individual beliefs are common knowledge." It also records that "Halpern and Kets argued that players can agree to disagree in the presence of ambiguity, even if there is a common prior." This is the mathematical version of F2: shared priors plus common knowledge force convergence, so a persistent disagreement points to differing partitions, ambiguity, or private information. It corroborates the figure distinction between a shared pool and genuinely different frames. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aumann%27s_agreement_theorem
2. **Cognitive science / social epistemology, crowd aggregation.** Wikipedia, "Wisdom of the crowd": "The squared error of the collective prediction equals the average squared error minus the predictive diversity" (Scott E. Page's diversity prediction theorem). The same page says "the crowd needs to be independent but also diversified, in order to allow a variety of answers" and "Averaging can eliminate random errors that affect each person's answer in a different way, but not systematic errors that affect the opinions of the entire crowd in the same way." It also records that groups attempting consensus can become less accurate and that organizations leveraging crowds may marginalize contrarian opinions. Direct corroboration of F4 and F1. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_of_the_crowd
3. **Social psychology, polarization.** Wikipedia, "Group polarization": "a disagreement becomes more extreme as the different parties consider evidence on the issue. It is one of the effects of confirmation bias." It records Moscovici et al. 1972: "interaction within a group and differences of opinion are necessary for group polarization to take place," and Lord, Ross and Lepper 1979 on biased assimilation. Corroborates case 4 (mirror chain), not the yes case. Re-census in `01` section 8 dissolved the apparent conflict. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_polarization
4. **Social psychology / organizational studies, groupthink.** Wikipedia, "Groupthink": Janis defined it as concurrence-seeking that "tends to override realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action," and prescribed "devil's advocate", outside experts, independent groups and withheld leader opinion. It also reports Sunstein's observation that an insincere devil's advocate is much less effective than genuine dissent, and that a Red Team pursuing an alternative "for real" works better. Corroborates F2, F8 and F9, and adds a live design constraint: a role played without stake may not carry the correction. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupthink
5. **Animal behaviour, collective decision.** Wikipedia, "Collective animal behavior": small fish groups used a quorum rule and "generally resulted in the 'correct' decision but occasionally cascaded into the 'incorrect' decision"; "as the group size increased, the fish made more accurate decisions in following the more attractive fish model"; homing pigeons in groups showed "increased efficiency and decision making". Genuinely low overlap with the human-group literature. Corroborates the mechanism shape (quorum and cascade), not the human verdict. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_animal_behavior
6. **Philosophy of science, incommensurability.** Wikipedia, "Commensurability (philosophy of science)": theories are incommensurable when "embedded in starkly contrasting conceptual frameworks whose languages do not overlap sufficiently to permit scientists to directly compare the theories or to cite empirical evidence favoring one theory over the other." It records Kuhn's three reasons, including that proponents of competing paradigms "have different ideas about the importance of solving various scientific problems, and about the standards that a solution should satisfy," and that Kuhn "did not deny that two incommensurable theories may have a common reference environment". This is the historical name for the question's condition. It corroborates F2 and F3 without deciding either. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commensurability_(philosophy_of_science)
7. **Philosophy of science / history of science, consensus.** Wikipedia, "Scientific consensus": consensus is reached through "scholarly communication at conferences, the publication process, replication of reproducible results by others, debate, and peer review"; "Scientific consensus is related to (and sometimes used to mean) convergent evidence, meaning independent sources of evidence converge on a conclusion." It also records that "each of the various branches of science functions in somewhat different ways with different forms of evidence and experimental approaches". Corroborates the distinction between a convention and an anchor, and the need to keep verdicts per domain. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus

**Overlap declaration.** Sources 2, 3, 4 and 7 are all broad human-group social-science pages on Wikipedia and share a general evidence pool; they are one witness for the human-group claim, not four. Source 1 is genuinely low overlap (formal decision theory). Source 5 is genuinely low overlap (animal behaviour). Source 6 is a different field again (philosophy of science) but overlaps source 7 conceptually. The framework essays all share the author of the figures, so their agreement with the figures is expected and carries no independent weight.

## 6. Two-stage offer and the constraint on simulated work

Stage 1 is the residual, the separator and the refuter. Stage 2, opt-in only, is the theory and the formula. Stage 3 is reusing the formula on a group the reader can measure, with a self-written refuter and a stated mode. The order follows Fig 10 :52 and Fig 2 :45; stage 2 is opt-in because a reader who has absorbed the interiors stops being an outside frame (Fig 10 :42); stage 3 sends a method, which Fig 4 :22 permits.

**Constraint.** Some journals in the human-group fields restrict or require disclosure of AI-assisted or entirely simulated work. PSA's author guidelines require anonymous review and a word limit; CogSci requires double-blind review and a preprint policy; SSRN requires provenance of versions. A simulated derivation is not a measurement, so submit it as a method or a hypothesis with the mode stated, never as an empirical result about real groups. Check the current policy of the chosen venue before submitting. Nothing is sent by this agent; hosting and sending are the user's actions.
