# Isomers and order: why the same institutions produce different politics

**Mode: `specified`, with a `general` structural core.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question names the ingredients every political order already holds: contenders, a count, a court, offices, and a security apparatus. Yet the behaviour differs sharply. Sometimes losing produces a transfer, sometimes a coup, sometimes a civil war. If ingredients were the whole story, this difference would be invisible. A second derivation is owed: what is the bond, and what order does it impose?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the political-science frame: two or more contenders, a certifying body, a law, an office, a coercive service, an electorate. Bonds available from Fig 12 and the Operators table: comparison (`⋛`), sequence (`→`), production (`⇒`), subtraction (`−`), conjunction (`+`), identity (`≡`), disjunction (`∥`), and the unary negation (`¬`). No new atom is needed; only the arrangement changes.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the bond in each case?

- Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Under `⋛` (comparison, *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, Operators table :49), the atoms make an **election**: the count decides which side is above the threshold.
- Under `⇒` (production, *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, Fig 12 :76), the atoms make **manufacture**: the incumbent produces a result that did not exist before the count.
- Under `¬` (negation, *"this does not hold"*, Operators table :55), the atoms make **removal**: the claim that the incumbent still holds office is refused.
- Under `+` (conjunction, *"both hold at once"*, Fig 12 :55), the atoms make a **power-sharing bargain**: both sides hold at once.

The atoms are identical across all four. The behaviour is not. This is the isomer claim, not an analogy.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two of these arrangements, built from the same atoms with different bonds, produce indistinguishable behaviour in a documented case, Q1 fails and the difference is in the atoms after all.

### Q2: What order is imposed?

- `→` is ordered: *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :71). The test is *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."* (Fig 12 :73).
- `≡` is symmetric: *"two names, one referent"* (Fig 12 :65). The test is *"substitute one for the other. Is anything lost? No."* (Fig 12 :68).
- `⋛` is a threshold, not a chain. Fig 12 :87: *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."*

A credible competition is a **sequence**, not a single comparison: contest → settlement → transfer → next contest. If the transfer is put before the settlement, the order is reversed and the arrangement is a different substance. If the count is treated as `≡` (two names, one referent, as in "the ruling is thewill of the people"), the comparison disappears and so does the contest. Operators table :34: *"the only bond a flat chain can express, so every term today is a sequence whether or not it should be."*

**Falsifier:** exhibit a credible competition whose transfer is convincingly modeled as simultaneous with the count, with no ordered dependency. That breaks the sequence requirement.

### Q3: Which structure is a catalyst?

Fig 12 :119: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."*

A court that rules on an election and remains in office afterwards is a catalyst: it survives its own use and can rule again. A one-off commission that dissolves after certifying is a spent part. They are made of similar atoms and behave differently across uses. Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."* A settled court becomes an atom for the next contest; a spent commission does not. This is why alternation can become self-sustaining (the political-science source on peaceful transitions calls it *"a habit-forming activity"*).

**Falsifier:** show a spent part and a surviving part with the same atoms producing the same multi-use behaviour. That kills the catalyst distinction here.

### Q4: Where is the negation?

`¬` is not a bond. Fig 12 amendment: *"Negation is not one: it takes a term and yields a term, and joins nothing."* Operators table :58: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate."*

The removal of an incumbent is a negation, not a subtraction. `−` is *"A with B removed or absent"* (Fig 12 :80), the absence of an operand, while `¬` is the refusal of a claim. A political order that only has subtraction can remove a person from a list and cannot refuse the claim that they still hold office. That is the difference between a reshuffle and a transfer.

Formula step 2 routes this to REFUTED: a term held is false, and what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation. The refusal comes before any minting of new institutions. Designing a new commission before refusing the standing claim of the incumbent is out of order.

**Falsifier:** exhibit a transfer that is fully described by subtraction alone, with no refusal of a standing claim. Then Q4 is withdrawn.

### Q5: What kind of thing is the union?

Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*

`contest → settlement → transfer` is a functional group. Elections, primaries, and internal party contests are members of it with different qualifiers. They are not isomers of each other when they share only the group; they are isomers when they are written over the full shared atom set and differ only in which bond mediates which pair. The distinction matters for verification: a checker asked "is this credible competition?" is being asked about the group, not about a specific member.

Fig 12 :48: *"A chain is the degenerate case: one bond type, no branching."* A system that can only write sequence cannot write comparison, subtraction, or refusal, so it can describe a chain of events and not a contest with a loser.

**Falsifier:** find a system that writes only sequence and still registers a refused claim. That contradicts the claim that a vocabulary of ingredients cannot state a route or a refusal.

## CONCLUSION

The credible-competition case is an **isomer** of the manufacture and civil-war cases: same atoms, different bond, different substance. Its order is a sequence with a comparison at its centre and a negation at its end. Its durable part is a catalyst, a structure that survives its own use and becomes an atom for the next contest. Negation is the one operator that cannot be replaced by any of the seven bonds, and refusing the incumbent's standing claim comes before minting new institutions.

This framing rescues what is checkable without naming a country or an institution. The names "election", "coup", and "transfer" are bindings from the political-science frame, provenanced in `10_source_ledger.csv`, not derivations from Fig 12.
