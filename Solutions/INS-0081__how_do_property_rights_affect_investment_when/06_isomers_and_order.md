# Are a secure group and a precarious group isomers? Bonds, order and competing explanations

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The record's plain contrast, "secure ownership raises investment, precarious ownership lowers it", treats security as one more ingredient in a list. Two questions are owed: (1) can the two groups be written over the **same** atom set, differing only in the bond, so that they are isomers under Fig 12's definition; and (2) what is the dependency order that decides which link fails first?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held for this derivation: land, labour, capital, technology, a claim of right, an enforcing hierarchy, a market (Fig 11 :45-48 enumerates actors, conditions and relations from the world; nothing here is composed from them). Bonds held: the seven, including ⋛ comparison (*"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, Fig 12 :87) and → sequence (*"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, Fig 12 :72). No new primitive is available without import, and a title adds no atom (Fig 6 :25).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Same atoms, different bond: are they isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Write secure group S as: `{land, labour, capital, technology, claim, hierarchy, market}` bonded by an **enforced, transferable appropriation** relation.
- Write precarious group P as: the **same seven atoms**, bonded by a **contested, revocable appropriation** relation.
- The atom sets are identical. The bond differs. The behaviour differs: S plants trees, P plants annuals (Ali, Dercon and Gautam 2011 report long-term investment in trees and shrubs responding to perceived transfer rights and expropriation threat). By Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**. Mode `specified` for the behavioural consequence; `general` for the structural claim.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either group can be written only by adding an atom the other does not hold (for example, P genuinely has no claim of right at all, or S alone has market access), the isomer claim fails and the weaker functional-group reading below stands instead.

### Q2 - If the atom sets differ, what are they?

- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- In most field records P is not written over the same set: no collateral, no registered claim, no court. The substructure `state → structure → state` (a present investment crossing to a future return through an enabling structure) is shared, but the atoms in the slots differ. By Fig 12 :123 the two are members of the same **functional group**, not isomers.
- This is the honest default for the empirical literature, because studies rarely measure the full atom set on both sides. The isomer claim is the stronger, checkable version; the functional-group claim is what the current record supports. Both are recorded, not blended (Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*).

### Q3 - Which bond does the work?

- The appropriation relation is not one of the seven named bonds; it is a **binding** from the legal/habitat frame onto held atoms. Fig 6 :25: **"An import adds no atom to the world."** It must be provenanced, not treated as a new primitive.
- What the bond changes is the **fidelity of the → handoff** from present action to future return (Fig 7 :38: *"the bracket’s fidelity across the handoff"*). Under an enforced claim the bracket crosses; under a contested one it does not. Fig 7 :39 adds the second factor, *"the ground’s pressure on the room"*: if the plot stops paying everyone, no bond restores investment.
- The comparison ⋛ between S and P is a **threshold relation between two quantities** (Fig 12 :87), not a substance that either group owns. Operators table :52: *"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"* - the comparison is what lets the derivation state a goal (a target level of investment) at all.

### Q4 - What is the catalyst and what is the spent part?

- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**
- A title is a catalyst for S: it survives use in credit and sale and is regenerated by each successful transaction. The same title is a spent part for P if a single dispute consumes it, or if the hierarchy that granted it is replaced. Fig 11 :49-51 gives the reason: valence is relative to the residual and no standing list of good actors survives a change of residual.
- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."** Investment that raises the chance of retaining the tenancy closes a positive loop, which is how Banerjee and Ghatak 2004 obtain a positive effect of eviction threats. Investment whose return is appropriated by someone else closes a negative loop. The sign is decided by the bond, not by the atom.

### Q5 - What order do the links run in?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."**
- Order: 1 perception of last season's return → 2 vocabulary for what tenure affords → 3 objective (a stateable seven-year return) → 4 planning → 5 learn and carry.
- The middle link breaks first (Fig 3 :24-27): it has no instrument, while perception and planning do. A group with a contested claim has terms for the plot and for the harvest, and no term for the harvest **that will still be its own**. This is the precise location of the failure, and it is where an instrument is owed.
- Most policy effort lands above the break, where measurements already exist: more credit, more training, more planning (Fig 3 :29-30). Optimising those cannot rescue a missing objective link (Fig 3 :19).

## CONCLUSION

The two cores are **isomers if written over the full shared atom set**, with the enforced versus contested appropriation bond as the difference; as the empirical literature usually writes them they are members of the same **functional group** with a shared `state → structure → state` substructure. Their comparison is ⋛ at a threshold, not a merged substance. The title is a catalyst for one group and a spent part for the other, and the sign of the investment loop is decided by that bond rather than by the atoms. The order of failure runs through the vocabulary/objective link, which is the one with no instrument. This framing rescues what is defensible in the "property rights affect investment" intuition without naming an arrangement the framework cannot name: `metric of success` remains an instrument question, `enforced versus contested claim` is a bond provenanced from the habitat frame, and the isomorphism is a checkable structural claim with a stated falsifier.
