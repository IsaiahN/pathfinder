# Union, order and analogues: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6), not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The question asks when influence promotes consensus and when it produces polarization or fragmentation. Before choosing a figure line, three sub-questions are owed: (1) is the outcome pair a partition or a union, (2) what is the dependency order of the conditions, and (3) are the two outcomes isomers under Fig 12's definition, or something else?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in this derivation: frames, opinions, an anchor, an evidence pool, a residual, a room. Operators held: influence (directed, weighted), comparison (⋛), sequence (→), negation (¬, for the refusal). No new primitive is introduced. The world sources are imports with provenance, not atoms.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 : Partition or union?

Fig 9 :4-5 "First: is there a fact here? Disagreements that do not shrink with effort, where each rule keeps working well on a different subset, are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."

Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."

Consensus and fragmentation are **not two ends of one scale**. They are overlapping conditions of the same contact process:

- A group can agree and be wrong (drift or one pool).
- A group can disagree and be right (a union of conditions, or a stalemate filtering a bad correlated cue).
- A group can agree and be right (triangulation).

So the mapping is many-to-one from conditions to observed agreement. Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again." The practical instruction: do not try to maximize an agreement score; identify which condition is present.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if observed agreement maps one-to-one onto ground accuracy across a declared sample, the union reading fails and a single scale would have been adequate.

### Q2 : What order do the conditions sit in? (Fig 3 dependency chain)

Fig 3 :20-21 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

The order is:

1. **Is there a non-updating ground for this residual?** (Fig 2 :29-35). This is the anchor test. Without it, the "grounded" panel is unavailable and only the two collapses remain.
2. **Are the frames independent?** (Fig 2 :31-32; Fig 13 :38-39). This is the evidence-pool test. Without it, agreement carries no verdict weight even if an anchor exists.
3. **What does the update produce?** (Fig 2 :13-15). Given 1 and 2, drift toward influence weight is diagnosed; given their absence, triangulation is diagnosed.
4. **Is the residual one question or several?** (Fig 9 :4-5). This decides whether to close the disagreement at all.
5. **Only then, is composition across rooms required?** (Fig 7 :36). Adding a room from an identical closure cannot rescue a broken link at step 1 or 2.

**Falsifier:** if a manipulation at step 4 or 5 rescues a failure at step 1 or 2 (for example, adding more homogeneous rooms fixes a captured anchor), the dependency order is wrong.

### Q3 : Are consensus and polarization isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Under the figure definition, the answer is **no as usually described, yes under a stricter reading**:

- As normally reported, a consensus group {frames, agreement} and a polarized group {frames, disagreement} do not share an atom set: one has agreement, the other does not. They differ in an atom, not only in a bond.
- Under the stricter reading, both are molecules over the full atom set {frames, opinions, anchor, evidence pool, residual}. They differ in which **bond** connects frames to anchor and frames to one another: frames ranged separately against the anchor versus frames updating on each other or on one shared pool. Same atoms, different bonds, different substance. That is a real isomer claim.

Fig 12 :94 "The arrangement is the substance." And :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

So the two outcomes are **isomers over the shared atom set** when the arrangement is specified at the level of bonds. A measurement that records only "agreement level" has flattened the bond and cannot distinguish them, which is exactly the flat-formula collapse (Fig 12 :14-16: "the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not").

**Falsifier:** if the two outcomes can be written over different atom sets with the same bond arrangement, the isomer claim fails and they are simply different molecules.

### Q4 : The analogue that governs why more rooms do not help

Fig 7 :14 "blending: novelty swamped to the mean." This is the figure's version of a well-known result in collective decision making: when all members draw on the same signal, aggregation reduces variance without adding information. The world analogue is effective sample size under clustering (Killip et al. 2004: *"In accounting for the similarities among clustered subjects, there is a net loss of independent data."*) and homophily (McPherson et al. 2001). These are analogues, not derivations: the figure governs, the world sources corroborate.

A second analogue is the **stalemate** (Winklmayr et al. 2020: *"stalemates improve collective accuracy by selectively filtering out incorrect decisions"*). This maps onto Fig 9 :4-5 rather than onto Fig 7: a group that does not converge may be correctly refusing a premise. The analogy must not be stretched into "stalemates are always better"; Fig 11 :49-51 makes valence residual-relative.

### Q5 : Does the framework name the mechanism?

No. Fig 9 :32 "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself" and the canonical README's "shape, not a name" govern. Naming "confirmation bias" or "echo chamber" as *the* cause is a binding from the psychology or sociology frame onto the figure's collapse panels, provenanced as such. *They're Scanning Books* warns that *"Declaring the primitives does not make them neutral. It makes the choice visible."* The three mechanisms are named in the figures (anchor-less update, common pool, union of conditions); their empirical identity in a given case is owed to a ground reading.

## CONCLUSION

Consensus and fragmentation are a **union**, not a scale. Their conditions sit in a dependency order with the anchor test and independence test upstream (Fig 3). Over the shared atom set they are **isomers** in Fig 12's sense: same atoms, different bond arrangement, different substance, indistinguishable in a record that stores only agreement level. More rooms from the same closure do not rescue an upstream break. The framework names the conditions, not the empirical mechanism in any specific case.
