## What in the wider literature bears on this question

1. **The ranking is conditional, and the field says so.** Ranius et al. 2022 report that the 74 reviews they synthesised *"are not always consistent with each other"* and that *"there are inherent trade-offs to be addressed."* Their Figure 3 caption states that which strategy is best *"depends on the extent and characteristics of the disturbances, and the need for and capacity of the landscape to allow for species persistence and dispersal through the landscape, which in turn is affected by the biology (e.g., dispersal ability) and habitat requirements of species."* This corroborates Fig 11 :51 and Fig 9 :57. It is one witness, not 74.

2. **The vocabulary is uninstrumented at the middle link.** Tonetti et al. 2023 found *"Definitions, terminologies and measurements of LH were diverse and conflicting. Most articles (534 out of 661) did not provide any definition for LH"*, and only 10 studies measured functional heterogeneity. This corroborates Fig 3 :27, *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works."* It shares no evidence pool with the figures.

3. **A widely recommended pattern fails at low dispersal.** Macdonald et al. 2025 found *"Corridors showed minimal impact, becoming effective only at the highest dispersal thresholds"* and that *"the apparent benefits of corridors are primarily attributable to the increased habitat area they contribute, rather than to connectivity per se."* This is not a conflict with Fig 11. It is an instance of valence relative to a residual: the residual is a dispersal ability. Returned to the census, it corroborates F1 and F5.

4. **Even the methods are objective-dependent.** Keeley et al. 2018 identify 13 design approaches and state that *"Guidance is provided on selecting the best methods for connectivity design depending on the objectives, available data, and landscape context."* Their Table 1 attaches a disadvantage to every advantage. This corroborates the union reading (F7) and refuses a single lever.

5. **Valence can reverse under rapid change.** Hale and Swearer 2016 define ecological traps as cases where animals *"mistakenly prefer habitats where their fitness is lower than in other available habitats following rapid environmental change."* This is the world-facing twin of Fig 11 :49–51 and a concrete warning against ranking patterns by the cues species select.

6. **A fixed frame fails when the question moves.** Trinh 2019 reports that *"experts suffer from the curse of knowledge and cognitive entrenchment, which prevents them from being able to adapt to changing situational demands."* At the individual stratum this corroborates Fig 10 :72 and Fig 7 :36. It is a cognition source, so low overlap with ecology.

7. **No universal best, from mathematics.** Wolpert and Macready 1997 prove that for any algorithm, *"any elevated performance over one class of problems is offset by performance over another class."* A landscape pattern is a strategy over a class of residuals; the theorem is a low-overlap corroboration of F1. It is an analogue, not a derivation, and is weighted accordingly.

No source conflicts with the figure verdict after re-census. The one apparent conflict (Macdonald et al. vs the connectivity consensus) was resolved by re-census rather than by changing the verdict.

This is Fig 10 :52 (*"Bring a characterised residual, never the source"*) and Fig 2 :45 (*"Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."*).
2. **Stage 2, opt-in only.** If they are curious after recording a verdict, offer the three local framework links. Their stage-1 verdict is never pooled as later corroboration (Fig 2 :40). Their later agreement is expected, because by then they hold the interior (Fig 10 :42).
3. **Stage 3, method only.** Offer the formula as a generator, with the instruction that each user writes a refuter and states a mode before running it. A method may cross up; an answer may not (Fig 4 :22).
