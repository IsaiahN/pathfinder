# Supporting derivation: structure, order, and analogues

**Mode: `specified` for the ecological application, `general` for the structural rules.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §6), not a ground reading.

## RESIDUAL

The primary audit leaves three supporting questions open, each answerable from a figure line without naming a winning pattern:

1. If "biodiversity under changing climate" is a union, what is the dependency order of its conditions, and which link has no instrument?
2. Are competing landscape patterns best read as **isomers** (same atoms, different arrangement) or as members of one **functional group** (same kind, different members), and what does that change about the comparison?
3. Does a pattern validated at one scale carry to another, and what is the honest measure of what is lost?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the ecology frame: core habitat, corridor or linkage, stepping stone, matrix, refugium, and the conditions (temperature, water, season, terrain) and relations (pollination, predation, competition, dependency, dispersal) enumerated for a given residual. Bonds held: the seven from Fig 12, including ⋛ comparison and → sequence. No new primitive is available without import. Anchors: measured persistence for D1/D4/D5; climate is a condition that updates, not an anchor (Fig 11 :73).

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Dependency order of the union

- Fig 3 :20–21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."*
- Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*
- Ordered chain for this question: perception (measure which species persist where) → vocabulary (terms for what each landscape element affords that species under that shift) → objective (what counts as persistence, per slot) → planning (a pattern to implement) → learn and carry (monitor, re-rank, select the next room).
- The break is at the vocabulary/objective link. Tonetti et al. 2023 found 534 of 661 papers gave no definition of the central term and only 10 measured functional effects. Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* More pattern-comparison studies land above the break.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** produce an instrument that returns a per-species, per-shift affordance reading and show its output is used to set the objective. If that exists and the link still fails, the diagnosis is wrong.

### Q2 - Isomers or a functional group?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- As the literature usually writes them, "large cores" and "connected small patches" share only habitat area. Under Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**; they are two members of a functional group, `state → structure → state` with different atoms in the slots. Macdonald et al. 2025 found area dominated and corridors contributed little, which is a result about a member of that group, not about the group.
- The stronger form: write both designs over the full shared atom set `{core, corridor, stepping stone, matrix, refugium}` plus the same conditions and relations, and vary only which structure mediates which contact. Then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different persistence outcome. That is the claim worth testing, and it is testable because the atom set is stated before the comparison.
- Keeley et al. 2018 corroborate from inside the field: *"Because different species have different ecological requirements with respect to landscape configuration, landscapes containing large protected areas connected through linkages, or stepping stones embedded in a permeable matrix will promote population persistence and facilitate range expansion at the leading edge for the greatest number of species."* That is a functional-group statement (kind plus qualifier), not a ranking.
- **Falsifier:** if either design requires an atom the other does not (for example a design that needs a microclimatic refugium the other does not contain), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.
- Triangulated for the figure definition (Fig 12) and the field statement (Keeley et al., low overlap with the figures). Single-source for the specific atom set, which is a binding from the ecology frame.

### Q3 - Cross-scale order and the round-trip gap

- Fig 4 :20: **"it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."**
- Fig 4 :27–30: *"The transform between scales loses information, and that is the point... So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."*
- `THE_FORMULA.md` step 6: `R_T = gap( x , (T_E ∘ T_A)(x) )`.
- A pattern validated at patch scale, abstracted to a landscape rule, and concretised back at region scale will not match what was sent up. The gap is not an error to eliminate; it is the measurement of what the coarser description cannot hold (Fig 4 :31–32).
- Concretely, Ranius et al. 2022 distinguish "patch connectivity" from "landscape connectivity" and note that an intervention good for one can be poor for the other. That is a round-trip gap in the field's own terms. Fig 10 :72: *"When the question changes, the room should change."*
- **Falsifier:** show that a patch-scale result carried to region scale and back reproduces the original reading with zero residual on the measured persistence variable, across a climate shift. That would set `R_T` to zero and dissolve the scale warning.
- Single-source for the transform rule (Fig 4), triangulated with Ranius et al. for the field instance.

### Q4 - Analogues, explicitly labelled as analogues

- **Mathematics, low overlap.** Wolpert and Macready 1997: *"any elevated performance over one class of problems is offset by performance over another class."* No universal best pattern over all residual classes. This is a theorem about optimisation, not a theorem about landscapes. It corroborates F1 at the level of structure only.
- **Animal behaviour, low overlap.** Hale and Swearer 2016: ecological traps occur when *"animals mistakenly prefer habitats where their fitness is lower than in other available habitats following rapid environmental change."* This corroborates the valence-reversal rule (Fig 11 :49–51) in a different stratum. The mechanism is cue decoupled from outcome, not the figure's mechanism, so it is a corroboration of the shape, not an instance of it.
- **Human cognition, low overlap.** Trinh 2019: expertise brings *"cognitive entrenchment, which prevents them from being able to adapt to changing situational demands."* This corroborates Fig 10 :72 at the individual stratum.
- **Falsifier for the analogue block:** a source with genuinely different evidence showing a pattern that is best across all residual classes, or a field measurement in which preferred habitat remains high-fitness through a rapid change. Either would break the analogue support, though not the figure verdict by itself.

## CONCLUSION

The union is ordered by dependency, and it breaks at the vocabulary link, which has no instrument. Competing patterns are a functional group as usually written and a real isomer claim only over an explicitly shared atom set. The cross-scale transform is lossy, so any upscaled rule carries a measured `R_T`, not an assumed zero. The low-overlap analogues corroborate the shape but do not derive it. None of this names a winning pattern, which is the correct result for a question whose premise assumes a standing ranking.
