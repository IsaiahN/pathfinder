**Subject:** One output to check about tariffs, before any theory

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know about trade policy. I am not asking you to read a framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with a position on tariffs. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`).** The question "do tariffs protect jobs and supply chains, or raise costs and invite retaliation?" bundles at least five different slots into two rhetorical packages. A border price change is one action. Its effects register as: output and employment in the import-competing sector; consumer and importing-firm prices; input costs and employment downstream; export volumes and foreign welfare when partners respond; and the continuity of supply networks. Those are different currencies measured against different anchors. The results are therefore per slot and must be kept separate:
>
> 1. Import protection can raise output in the protected sector. In the 2018 US tariffs, the estimated employment gain from protection was small and was more than offset by two other channels.
> 2. Input tariffs and retaliatory tariffs raised costs and reduced employment and job postings in downstream and export-exposed activities.
> 3. Retaliation is a response from a frame with a different closure, not a side effect on a passive variable. The Smoot-Hawley episode shows the same loop with a longer fuse.
> 4. A tariff changes the price of one bond at the border. It does not, by itself, rebuild a supply network. Reshoring is a separate move: it substitutes one habitat for another and carries two silent failures, what you failed to reproduce and what you unintentionally introduced.
>
> The shape this yields is a per-slot separator: for each outcome, name the currency, name a measurement that does not move when a frame thinks about it, and record the residual. It does **not** yield a single national verdict that tariffs are good or bad. That aggregate is a declared gap here, not a silence and not a hidden answer.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** not that tariffs never help any worker, not that retaliation is automatic in every episode, not that a welfare total is the truth about jobs, and not that a framework settles an empirical trade question. The figure-based part of this work only fixes the shape any answer must have. It cannot measure an employment effect. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim** (written down before this was sent):
> 1. a single non-updating measurement that returns one signed effect for jobs, prices and resilience at once, with a conversion each instrument accepts;
> 2. a tariff shown to raise supply continuity with no change in the supplier or route set;
> 3. a large tariff episode with no response from any trading partner and unchanged exports against a fixed anchor;
> 4. a constructed cross-currency ground that closes the aggregate gap.
>
> If any one holds, the separator is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures, not my summary of them, are the authority.
> - Figure text extract with line numbers (local framework file): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - The three-channel employment decomposition: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2019086pap.pdf
> - Price pass-through and supply-chain reconfiguration: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.33.4.187
> - The Smoot-Hawley retaliation record: https://academic.oup.com/ej/article/132/647/2500/6519264
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the framework figures. Run it on a trade question you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
