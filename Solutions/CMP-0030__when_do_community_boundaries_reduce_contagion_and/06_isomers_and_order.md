# Isomers and order in the boundary question

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §5).

## RESIDUAL

Two networks can have the same nodes, the same number of edges and the same degree sequence, yet one slows an outbreak and the other sustains it. Why does the same ingredient set give opposite behaviour, and what is the order of the composition at which the boundary acts?

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting derivation)

Atoms held in D1: nodes, edges, membership, bridge, group (a settled composition of nodes and internal edges). Operators held: `+` conjunction (both conditions hold), `⋛` comparison (a threshold relation, Operators table :48-51), `→` sequence, `−` subtraction, `¬` negation. No new primitive is needed. Fig 13 :115: "The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are two community partitions isomers?

Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Fig 12 :94: **"The arrangement is the substance."**

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**

Take two graphs with identical degree sequences. Graph A puts the edges inside two dense blocks with a single bridge; graph B scatters the same edges at random. Their ingredient lists are identical. Under Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**: the difference is invisible in a degree-sequence formula and decisive in the outbreak trajectory. An epidemic model that takes only the degree distribution as input has literally recorded half of the system. Fig 12 :95-96: "Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing. They are different things whose notation cannot tell them apart, and the difference is carried entirely by the bond."

**Strain check:** the chemical isomer example (silver fulminate and silver cyanate, Fig 12 :97-98) concerns molecular structure, not graphs. Re-census: the figure's claim is about notation and arrangement, which is domain-agnostic. The bond here is the within/between allocation of edges, a typed relation not recoverable from the operand list. Low strain after re-census.

### Q2 — What is the order of the boundary's action?

Fig 12 :104: **"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world."**

- **First order:** node to node, inside one group. A meeting, a contact, an exposure. The group is not yet an operand.
- **Second order:** group to group. Each group's local state has settled into something that can serve as an atom. A single cross-boundary case between two settled groups is a second-order composition. Fig 4 :21 calls the crossing a membrane between sizes, which is the same distinction in D3 vocabulary.
- **Why this matters:** a bridge count is a first-order reading of a second-order quantity. It tells you how many edges exist, not whether the groups on either end have settled into a state that can be spent. Conversely, a group-level reproduction number is a second-order reading that cannot tell you which node is still seeding (Fig 1 :19).

Fig 12 :111-113: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one. A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction." A modularity value names a state. It cannot name the route by which a case crossed and returned.

### Q3 — Catalyst, cycle, and the sign of the loop

Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."**

An overlapping member is a catalyst. If it were spent by the first crossing, repeated reseeding would be impossible. If it survives and is regenerated by belonging to two groups, the same single node can carry many crossings. A recording of "number of bridges" does not say which bridges are catalysts.

Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**

Within-group reinfection is a cycle. Negative sign, the outbreak dies; positive sign, it closes as an endemic reservoir. The figure makes the key structural point: a chain model (one pass through SIR, say) cannot close by construction. So the reservoir half of CMP-0030 is not a parameter setting of a chain model; it requires a different operator, the cycle.

**Operators table :48-51** defines `⋛` comparison as "a threshold relation between two quantities" with the test "is it about which is larger, rather than both being present?" Complex contagion is a `⋛` comparison (is the number of exposures above the threshold?), not a `+` conjunction of exposures. Confusing these is why a model can count exposures and still miss the threshold effect.

### Q4 — Functional group versus isomer

Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Simple contagion and complex contagion share a functional group: a state, a structure, a thresholded state. They differ in the qualifier (one exposure versus several) and in the bond (a crossing that suffices versus a crossing that does not). Under Fig 12 :124 the group says what kind of contagion; the qualifier says which regime the boundary selects. So the four-cell table in F6 is not four unrelated questions; it is one functional group with two qualifiers crossed with two orders.

### Q5 — The two currencies

Fig 12 :126: **"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them"** and will not notice the shortfall.

- **Bits:** the modularity value, the partition description, the bridge count. Cheap to compute from an adjacency matrix.
- **Actions:** a contact-tracing study, a cross-boundary transmission experiment, a sequenced outbreak. Expensive.

Fig 12 :128-129: "A long chain of cheap parts is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute. A short arrangement over a costly operand is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle." A four-cell regime table is cheap to state and expensive to settle. That is the honest reason the number in F8 is a gap rather than an omission.

## CONCLUSION

Two partitions with the same ingredients are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different epidemic behaviour. The boundary acts at two orders, first as an edge between nodes and second as a membrane between settled groups. Overlapping members are catalysts, within-group reinfection is a cycle whose sign decides persistence, and complex contagion is a threshold comparison rather than a conjunction of exposures. The simple and complex cases form a functional group crossed with the two orders, which is why a single scalar cannot decide the sign.

## FALSIFIER, WRITTEN BEFORE THE RUN

If two graphs with identical degree sequences but different internal/between edge allocation produce statistically indistinguishable outbreak trajectories under the same one-exposure process, the isomer claim fails. If a one-pass chain model reproduces a persistent reservoir without a cycle operator, the catalyst/cycle claim fails. If exposures combine additively rather than through a threshold in a pre-registered behavioural-adoption test, the comparison-bond claim fails.
