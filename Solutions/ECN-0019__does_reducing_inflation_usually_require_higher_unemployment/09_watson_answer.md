## QUESTION

Decide, in effect terms, what the Tether says about the claim that disinflation usually requires higher unemployment and that better policy can avoid much of the cost. Option A: accept the question as a request for one average coefficient. Option B: refuse the average as an aggregation error, split the compound, and state the shape a measured answer must have. What each leads to: A discards the slot signal and invites a policy that moves the target to buy employment, which the figures predict produces coherent drift; B yields an indexed shape, three pre-registered refuters, and an open magnitude that the ground must fill.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain with actors / currency / anchor, partition or union, and order by dependency.

- **R (the residual).** The fixed-coefficient prediction for d(u)/d(pi) versus the episode record showing very different costs for similar inflation reductions. Arity: two surface quantities, four underlying conditions. Symmetry: ordered comparison, not symmetric conjunction; the target is a goal and a goal is a comparison (Fig 12 :113). Scale: first order within an episode, second order at the regime level.
- **D1, the policy loop.** Actors: central bank, policy rate, price level, unemployment. Currency: a loss over inflation versus target and unemployment versus natural rate. Anchor: the nominal target, which must not move when private expectations move. Partition boundary: what the bank can and cannot set.
- **D2, the expectations frame.** Actors: firms, workers, wage setters, indexation rules. Currency: expected inflation in nominal contracts. Anchor: a credible commitment, a convention that is not allowed to decide the outcome. Separate frame, low overlap with D1.
- **D3, the ground.** Actors: price indices, unemployment surveys, episode record. Currency: measured d(pi) and d(u). Anchor: the data, which does not change because a model thinks otherwise.
- **D4, the cost compound.** Actors: expectations, nominal rigidity, supply shocks, labour-market structure. Currency: which condition is binding. Anchor: the binding condition, identified by an instrument. **Union**, not a list of separate conditions.
- **Order by dependency (Fig 3).** Anchor first, then expectations, then the slope of the tradeoff, then the cost at the ground. The middle link has instruments now but they can be pooled or saturated.

## WORLD (secondary sources read)

Every source below was opened on 2026-11-23. Each gives field, author / venue / year, quote, prediction here, and status. World sources corroborate; they never overrule a figure line.

- **Field: economic methodology.** Lucas, "The Lucas critique" (Wikipedia article, 2026). Quote: "any change in policy will systematically alter the structure of econometric models." Predicts that a historical sacrifice coefficient will not survive a regime change. **corroborates** F1.
- **Field: monetary economics.** Sargent and Wallace as summarized in "Rational expectations" (Wikipedia, 2026). Quote: "the government can only increase the inflation rate but not employment." Predicts that exploiting the tradeoff yields inflation without employment gain. **corroborates** F2, but it is a model-frame result and is not adopted as a figure verdict.
- **Field: monetary policy.** "Monetary policy" (Wikipedia, 2026). Quote: "in the absence of credibility in the eyes of the public inflationary expectations will not drop, and the short-run effect of the announcement and a subsequent sustained anti-inflation policy is likely to be a combination of somewhat lower inflation and higher unemployment ... But if the policy announcement is deemed credible, inflation is likely to come down more quickly and without so much of a cost in terms of unemployment." Predicts exactly the anchor split. **corroborates** F2.
- **Field: monetary economics.** "Inflation" (Wikipedia, 2026). Quote: "if the central bank has a reputation of being 'tough' on inflation ... inflationary expectations will come down rapidly, thus allowing inflation itself to come down rapidly with minimal economic disruption." Predicts lower cost under a pinned reputation. **corroborates** F2.
- **Field: game theory and behavioural economics.** "Dynamic inconsistency" (Wikipedia, 2026). Quote: "Monetary policy makers suffer from dynamic inconsistency with inflation expectations, as politicians are best off promising lower inflation in the future. But once tomorrow comes lowering inflation may have negative effects, such as increasing unemployment, so they do not make much effort to lower it." Predicts the need for a commitment device. **corroborates** F2 and F4; the behavioural-material strand is lower overlap with the macro sources.
- **Field: macroeconomics.** "Phillips curve" (Wikipedia, 2026). Quote: the Akerlof-Dickens-Perry result that moving inflation from two percent to zero "will be permanently increased by 1.5 percent" unemployment. Predicts a real but conditional cost. **corroborates** F1 and supplies a magnitude the figures refuse.
- **Field: economic history.** "Paul Volcker" (Wikipedia, 2026). Quote: inflation "peaked at 14.8 percent in March 1980, fell below 3 percent by 1983" while "the national unemployment rate rose to over 10%". Predicts a costly episode when the anchor was rebuilt. **corroborates** F1 and F2 at the ground.
- **Field: monetary economics, named author.** Kevin D. Hoover, "Phillips Curve" (Econlib, Library of Economics and Liberty). Quote: "contractionary monetary and fiscal policies that drove the average rate of unemployment up to about 7 percent (i.e., one point above NAIRU) would be associated with a reduction in inflation of about one percentage point per year." Predicts a conditional, estimable cost and a hysteresis complication. **corroborates** F1, F3.
- **Field: international policy.** Kenneth Rogoff, "Globalization and Global Disinflation" (IMF speech, 2003; page is an IMF-hosted structured bundle). Quote: global inflation fell "from 30% to 4%" with institutional change and "Makes prices more flexible, reducing the real output gains from unanticipated inflation." Predicts disinflation with falling cost through institutional and competition changes rather than one lever. **corroborates** F3 and F4.

**Overlap declaration.** Seven of the nine sources are Wikipedia pages and one is an encyclopedia entry; they draw on the same Friedman/Phelps/Lucas/Sargent canon. Their agreement is one evidence pool talking, not independent witnesses (Fig 2 :40). The behavioural-economics strand and the Rogoff institutional data are the two lowest-overlap lines. No source conflicts with the figure verdict; the conflict rule was not triggered. All are secondary corroboration only.

## VERDICT (from the figures)

The word "usually" is refused as a request for one coefficient: that is the aggregation error Fig 1 names. The shape that survives is fourfold. Index the cost per regime slot. Pin the nominal anchor, because a target that moves with expectations or employment has stopped being an anchor and produces coherent drift. Unbundle the union before choosing a lever, because higher unemployment clears only the demand-only region. Treat regime change as a provenanced import from a frame whose closure differs, and remember that capability is agent-and-habitat, not a better controller alone. The magnitude, including any sacrifice ratio or NAIRU value, is a `gap` at the ground and remains `candidate`. Verdict mode: `specified`, ungrounded.

- **cost if wrong:** bounded and locatable. The packet states mode, three refuters and provenance before the conclusion, so an error is findable. The real cost is spending an outside reader's independence by sending a conclusion instead of a generator, which Fig 2 :42 prices: "Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong." That cost is avoidable by leading with the shape and its refuter.
- **undoable:** yes. The numeric gap is fillable by measurement without touching the shape. A refuted finding can be withdrawn and the others kept, because the four domain verdicts are separate and never averaged. No primitive has to be given up.
- **reversed by:** a ground reading, not agreement. A per-slot dataset with one stable coefficient and no regime interaction would kill F1. A pinned credible target with no faster decline in expected inflation and no smaller output cost would kill F2. A partition result with no interactions would kill F3. A provenanced regime change with no cost reduction, or an agent-only improvement that moves the ground, would kill F4 and F5. Until one fires, the verdict stands as `candidate`.
