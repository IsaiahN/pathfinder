# Union, isomer and order: the supporting derivation

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

Two things are owed beyond the main audit: (1) is the cost compound a partition or a union, and what follows structurally from each; (2) are the "exploit the tradeoff" policy and the "pin the anchor" policy made of the same atoms under a different bond, that is, are they isomers? An order claim and a falsifier are required for both.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held in the policy frame: the target, the policy rate, private expectations, the shock, the labour-market structure. Bonds held: the seven, in particular comparison (⋛), sequence (→) and negation (¬). No new primitive is available without an import. The two domains already identified are the policy loop (D1) and the expectations frame (D2); the compound (D4) is a union inside them.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Partition or union?

- Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."
- Fig 9 :55 "A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."
- Fig 9 :57 "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"

If the cost were a partition, "disinflation with unanchored expectations" and "disinflation with a supply shock" would be separate cases, and one coefficient per case would do. The world record does not behave that way: the cases overlap, and a recession clears the demand component while leaving the expectations and supply components standing. By Fig 9 :57 the union is categorically different, not just harder. **Result: union.** Each overlap gets its own loop and its own lever. This is also the essay's bundle warning (`05` section 3).

### Q2 - Are the two policies isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."

Policy A (exploit the tradeoff): target → expectations move together; each updates on the other. Policy B (pin the anchor): expectations → target, with the target fixed. Both use the same four atoms: target, rate, expectations, shock. They differ only in the bond direction. Under Fig 12 they are **isomers**: same ingredients, different substance, and the ingredient list alone cannot tell them apart. The behavioural difference is decisive, which is exactly the figure's test: A produces coherent drift (Fig 2 :13, :34), B produces falling expectations against a fixed reference (Fig 2 :33).

This is a structural claim, not a policy recommendation. It says why two proposals that look like the same list of tools can have opposite outcomes. It does not say which one a country has.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if the target's response to expectations and expectations' response to the target are observationally the same object (one is just a re-parameterisation of the other), the isomer claim fails and the two policies are one substance under two names. That would be Fig 8's warning about frames that overlap almost completely.

### Q3 - Order by dependency

- Fig 3 :20 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
- Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

Order: **anchor → expectations → slope of the tradeoff → cost estimate at the ground.** Effort spent estimating a single global cost coefficient (the last link) cannot rescue an unset anchor (the first link). The middle link, expectations formation, was for a long time the one with the weakest instrument; survey and market measures have since made it readable, but readings can still be pooled and saturated (Fig 10 :25-27). The dependency order, not the importance ranking, is why the anchor finding is reported before the magnitude finding in `01`.

### Q4 - Analogues from lower-overlap fields

Analogues corroborate; they do not decide.

- **Control engineering.** A controller whose setpoint is repeatedly re-set to chase the measured output does not regulate; it drifts. This is the same structure as Fig 2 :13 and :34, and it is why the analogue is useful. It predicts that a moving target will show up as a stable-looking but wandering series, exactly the "coherent drift, which is why nobody notices" case.
- **Behavioural economics.** The Odysseus-and-the-mast example in the *Dynamic inconsistency* source (opened 2026-11-23) is a commitment device: the agent binds its future self so that a promise becomes credible. That is the structural twin of Fig 10 :47 "a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one", read in reverse: a convention that cannot be changed by the outcome is what makes the promise credible.
- **Survey statistics.** The effective-sample-size correction for correlated observations (noted in Fig 2 :44) is the arithmetic behind Fig 2 :40. Ten economists reading the same report are one witness, not ten. This governs how the world sources in `09` are weighted.

None of these analogues supplies a figure verdict. They are cross-field corroboration with declared overlap.

## FALSIFIER (pre-registered, covering both claims)

1. If the cost compound behaves as a partition (one coefficient per condition, no interactions), Q1 fails.
2. If A and B are observationally the same object under a re-parameterisation, Q2 fails.
3. If a change in the slope estimate can be produced without changing the anchor or the expectations regime and survives at the ground, Q3's dependency order fails.
4. If any analogue predicts the opposite of the figure claim in a checkable case, the analogue is withdrawn and the figure claim is re-censused, not the other way round.

## CONCLUSION

The compound is a union, so it must be unbundled before a lever is chosen. The two candidate policies are isomers over the same four atoms, with the bond direction carrying the entire behavioural difference. The dependency order runs anchor → expectations → slope → cost, so the magnitude is the last thing to be estimated, not the first. All three claims are `specified` and carry the falsifiers above.
