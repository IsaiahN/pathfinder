## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Arity and symmetry of the cooperative act

A cooperative act is dyadic or multi-party. It cannot be indexed by a single slot in the way a physical state can. Fig 1 :19 "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated" applies here with the slot being the relation, not the actor. Symmetry: the payoff structure is symmetric between roles, but the valence is directed and flips with the residual (Fig 11 :50 "The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds").

**Consequence:** any answer that names a single actor property (generosity, altruism as a trait) has already made an arity error, reducing a relation to an operand.

### Q2 — Order by dependency (Fig 3)

Fig 3 :20-21 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces". Applied here:

1. **Enumerate the habitat** (Fig 11 :43-44 "The habitat is enumerated, never composed"). List actors, conditions and relations in contact with the residual before judging any of them.
2. **Assign valence relative to the residual** (Fig 11 :49). Only after enumeration can a relation be called beneficial or harmful for this act.
3. **Close the bond** (Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input."). Repeated encounter makes the return available without immediacy.
4. **Select between rooms** (Fig 7 :35). Group-level differential can carry cooperation that within-group selection removes.
5. **Transmit the method** (Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."). Norms generalize only as methods, not recordings.

A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can (Fig 3 :22-23). So an argument that jumps to group selection before enumerating the habitat is a reading below the break, which is "a reading of nothing".

**Falsifier for the order:** show a case where selection between rooms operates without any enumerated relation to the residual, and still explains the cooperation. That would break the dependency order, not merely reorder it.

### Q3 — Are the mechanisms isomers?

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :124 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

The five named mechanisms do **not** share an atom set in the strict sense. Kin selection binds genetic relatedness; direct reciprocity binds repeated encounter; indirect reciprocity binds reputation; network reciprocity binds graph position; group selection binds group membership. Different bindings, different atoms from the social-science frame. Under Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**; they are members of a **functional group**: each is a way of making a cooperative act contact a return beyond the immediate payoff. The group says "return beyond immediate payoff"; the qualifier says which mechanism.

The stronger isomer claim would require writing all five over the full shared substrate set and varying only the arrangement. That is a real checkable claim, but it is not established here.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if any two mechanisms require atoms the other does not (which the current census says they do), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands. This is already observed, so the isomer claim is **not** made.

### Q4 — Catalyst, cycle, and what they buy

Fig 12 :119 "catalyst. A part that survives its own use." Reputation and trust are catalysts: spending them in one act does not consume their standing for the next.

Fig 12 :121 "cycle. The output re-enters as input." A repeated interaction is a cycle, and "The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." A one-shot direct-reward frame is a chain; it cannot close by construction. That is the structural reason it cannot see the no-incentive return.

Fig 12 :104 "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." A cooperative norm settled in a group becomes an atom for the next level, which is why group-level selection can compose over individual cooperation without a new primitive.

### Q5 — The union is not a partition

Fig 9 :54-58: a bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, and "a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound." A cooperative act that is repeated **and** governed by a norm is a specific compound, not a harder one-shot act. It needs its own loop. This is why F9 in `01` keeps five verdicts separate.

## CONCLUSION

The cooperative act is a relation, not an operand (Q1). The five domains are ordered by dependency, not importance (Q2). The named mechanisms are a **functional group**, not isomers, because they do not share an atom set (Q3). Catalyst, cycle and the settled-molecule rule explain how the return arrives without immediacy and without a new primitive (Q4). The compound is a union, so the verdicts must stay separate (Q5).

This rescues what is defensible without adding a primitive: the order, the functional-group reading and the union claim are structural. The mechanism names remain provenanced imports from the world literature.
