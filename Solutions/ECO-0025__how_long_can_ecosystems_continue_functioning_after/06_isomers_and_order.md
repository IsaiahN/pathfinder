# Isomers, redundancy and order in an extinction debt

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (`05_what_shape_means.md` §7).

## RESIDUAL

The audit left three structural questions open: (1) can two systems with the same species list have different lag times, and under what figure definition; (2) what is the bond structure of "redundancy" and of a "key species"; (3) does the order in which species are lost change which function crosses first? A fourth question follows from Fig 12 :125-128: description cost and action cost are different currencies, so a cheap species list can be expensive to settle and a long interaction chain cheap to refute.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are two ecosystems with the same species list isomers?

- Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**
- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Let two sites hold the same set of species A, B, C. At site 1, B pollinates A and C preys on B. At site 2, C pollinates A and B preys on C. Same atoms, different bonds, different functional substance. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, and the different bond arrangement is exactly what the lag attaches to.
- This is not a metaphor imported from chemistry into ecology. Fig 12 :97-98 records the original case: *"silver fulminate and silver cyanate have the same composition and different properties, which is what forced structure into the notation."* The figure is about notation generally, and an interaction list without typed bonds is the same flat-formula failure.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two systems with the same species set, the same typed interaction bonds and the same life-stage structure still have different crossing times under the same perturbation, then the bond is not carrying the lag and a third variable is owed. The isomer claim would be under-specified, not wrong in its figure definition.

### Q2 - What is the bond of redundancy, and what kind is a key species?

- Operators table :19, the test for ∥: **"either suffices; not both required"**. Fig 12 :64: **"remove one. Does it still work? Yes."**
- A pollinator guild with two or more members that can each pollinate the same plant is a ∥ structure under the relevant function. Losing one member leaves the disjunction intact, so the function reading stays on its side of ⋛. Losing the last member removes the operand entirely. That is *−* (subtraction), not a smaller ∥: *"A with B removed or absent"* (Fig 12 :80).
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** The guild is the functional group; "the only remaining pollinator at this site" is the qualifier. Fig 11 :51 refuses the standing list: *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*
- Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."** A long-lived adult tree, a seed bank, a dormant stage: these parts are still counted after their contribution has stopped being regenerated. Anderson et al. 2011 found similar adult densities but 55% fewer juveniles per adult in the functionally extirpated mainland sites. The adult cohort is catalyst-like: present, not spent in the accounting, and not regenerating.
- **Falsifier:** show a "redundant" guild where removing all but one member reduces the function at the same rate as removing the whole guild, with an assay that resolves the difference. Then ∥ is the wrong bond here and the relationship is + (both needed), which is a different term with a different lag.

### Q3 - Does order matter in the cascade?

- Fig 12 :72, the test for → sequence: **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"**. Fig 12 :78, the test for ⇒ production: **"is there a B before A fires? No."**
- Species loss is ordered relative to a residual. If the pollinator declines before the long-lived plant reaches its final adult cohort, the plant's reproduction fails but the standing adults hold the "vegetation present" reading. If the plant is lost first, the pollinator loses a resource and declines later. Same atoms, opposite order, opposite term.
- ⇒ is the stronger claim: the second operand did not exist before the first fired. Co-extinction of a specialized parasite after its host is gone is a production relation in this vocabulary; the parasite's local population *"did not exist before"* the host crossed its threshold. Fig 11 :52-53 supplies the scale rule: *"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."*
- Fig 12 :111: **"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."** A species list names the state after the loss. Only the ordered bonds name the route, which is what a lag is.
- **Falsifier:** show that two different orders of loss over the same atoms and bonds produce the same crossing time at the same function assay, with no cancellation in the arrangement. Then → is the wrong bond for that cascade and the route is commutative there.

### Q4 - Cycles, and why a seed bank is not a reset

- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."**
- A self-replacing population is a cycle: adults produce recruits which become adults. A positive loop closes and the population persists; a negative loop (deaths exceed recruitment) unwinds. A seed bank is a cycle with a long delay in the return path. It can keep the "vegetation present" reading on the old side even after the above-ground loop has turned negative, which is another channel-lag mechanism, consistent with F3 of the audit.
- A chain, by contrast, cannot close: if a mutualist is lost and no alternative closes the loop, the cascade runs outward and stops. Fig 12 :121 is the reason "recovery" is not the reverse of "decline" in this vocabulary: the reverse of an open chain is a different chain, not a cycle that was always there.
- **Falsifier:** exhibit a recovery that retraces the loss sequence exactly, with the same actors and the same bond signs, under an instrument that resolves both directions. That would show the loop closes symmetrically here and the asymmetry claim is narrowed.

### Q5 - Two currencies: describing the debt versus settling it

- Fig 12 :125-126: **"A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add."** *"Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds."*
- A long chain of cheap parts, many interacting species with weak links, is expensive to describe and may cost one action to refute (a single assay that shows the function already crossed). A short arrangement over a costly operand, one dominant pollinator with a long generation time, is cheap to state and may take a whole budget to settle (years of monitoring to see whether recruitment failed).
- Fig 12 :130: **"Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it."** This is why a published half-life (description cost) cannot substitute for a prospective crossing assay (action cost), and why a literature review (cheap bits) cannot settle a duration (expensive actions).
- **Falsifier:** show a single currency in which both the complexity of the interaction list and the cost of the confirming assay are measured and traded off, with published conversion rates. Then Fig 12's two-currency rule is wrong for this domain.

## CONCLUSION

Over a fixed atom set, two ecosystems with the same species list but different typed interactions are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117), so the lag is carried by the arrangement and not by the ingredient list. Redundancy is ∥ (disjunction), a key role is a functional group (Fig 12 :123) with residual-relative valence (Fig 11 :49-51), and a long-lived adult or seed bank is catalyst-like (Fig 12 :119) or a delayed cycle (Fig 12 :121). Loss order is a → or ⇒ relation, so the route, not just the final state, decides which function crosses first. Description cost and action cost are held apart (Fig 12 :125-130).

This **rescues** what is checkable in the question while refusing the universal number: there is a real structural claim about which arrangement produces a longer lag, with four stated falsifiers. It needs no new primitive and no duration constant.
