## 0. The residual, stated in effect terms

The question asks whether a change in yield (a per-place reading of a quantity) can change land cover (a physical contact), and whether that conversion moves two grounds, biodiversity and climate forcing, in a worsening direction.

- **Arity.** Four links in a dependency chain: yield reading, conversion decision, biodiversity outcome, climate outcome. The word *and* splits the outcome into two conditions with different anchors, so the tail is a union, not one effect.
- **Symmetry.** Yield is a spectrum (more or less, orderable). Conversion is an ordered, asymmetric action: production precedes a changed land state, and the reverse is not available on the same terms. The yield-to-profit comparison is a threshold, not a symmetric join.
- **Scale.** Per field or region slot at the trigger, landscape and biome at the cascade, planetary at the carbon flux. The question silently crosses scales, so a single-frame answer will be wrong about at least one link.

**Binding the sought-for shape as an ordinary term.** What an answer would look like: a four-link chain, with a union tail, per-slot indexing, an asymmetric conversion action, a cascade in the biodiversity limb, and two non-updating anchors. Written down here before the run so it can accrue a residual and be refused by the same test as everything else (Fig 5 amendment on making the sought-for shape an ordinary term; THE_FORMULA.md "Direction"). If the re-census below had returned a different shape, this binding would have been withdrawn rather than protected.

### 1.3 Partition or union

**Union.** The two outcomes have different anchors, and conversion is the overlapping condition. Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the"*. Yield intensification can clear a biodiversity-only region (less land under cultivation in one place) and leave the climate-only region and the intersection standing (displaced production elsewhere, Fig 9 :57). The question's *and* therefore requires two verdicts kept separate, never averaged.

### 1.4 Order by dependency (Fig 3)

Perception of a yield change → vocabulary for what the land situation affords → an objective (profit, food security, policy target) → a plan to convert → the conversion act → the biodiversity cascade and the carbon flux → the union verdict. Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step"*. The break, if there is one, is expected between vocabulary and objective (Fig 3 :24-27), not at the yield measurement and not at the carbon accounting, both of which have instruments.

## 3. Findings F1 to F9

### F1  -  The trigger is per slot; a global yield average hides the live signal

Fig 1 :19, *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :22, *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*

A global statement such as "crop yields are rising and land is being spared" (opened in Our World in Data, quoting Ausubel, Wernick and Waggoner 2013) can coexist with a live per-region residual in which a specific crop fails and a specific frontier expands. The chain starts at the slot, not at the world total. A question about "changes in crop yields" asked at global scale is already reading the wrong object.

**Mode:** `specified`, triangulated across Fig 1 and the per-region structure of the IPCC land assessment (opened 2026-11-22), which explicitly reports large regional variability.

**Refuter:** show that conversion responds only to a global aggregate yield with no informative per-slot variation, or that per-slot yield residuals do not precede conversion in the relevant regions.

### F2  -  The yield-to-conversion link is the middle link, and it is where chains break

Fig 3 :20, *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* Fig 3 :27, *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

Yield change does not convert land by itself. It changes a reading; the reading enters a vocabulary of affordances (what the land can be used for) and an objective (profit, food security, a policy target), and only then a plan. Fig 3 :24-26 says the link between having terms for what a situation affords and saying what counts as progress has neither obvious failures nor obvious tests, so it attracts the least attention. That is exactly the trigger link in this question. The IPCC land report (opened) states that competition for land *"could increase food prices and lead to further intensification"* with *"the further loss of biodiversity"*, a mediated chain, not a direct law.

**Mode:** `specified`, single-source on the figure side for the break location (Fig 3), with world corroboration from IPCC. The link itself is a union of conditions, not a constant.

**Refuter:** demonstrate a direct yield-to-conversion law in which the decision frame, price, tenure and policy play no mediating role.

### F3  -  Conversion is an introduction and a substitution, not a reversible subtraction

Fig 11 :40, *"An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."* Fig 11 :61, *"Introduction is available; exclusion is not. You can add an actor and you cannot subtract the habitat, because contact is a fact about"*. Fig 11 :66, *"Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another."* Fig 11 :68, *"what you failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails"*.

Land conversion adds agriculture to a habitat. It cannot subtract the habitat it replaces. What looks like reversal is a further substitution, with two silent failures: what was not reproduced and what was unintentionally introduced. IPBES (opened) ranks *"changes in land and sea use"* first among direct drivers and reports that *"More than a third of the world's land surface"* is devoted to crop or livestock production. This is why the cost of being wrong about the trigger is high (Fig 2 :42, *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."*).

**Mode:** `specified`, triangulated across Fig 11 asymmetry and the IPBES driver ranking (low overlap: a figure law and an intergovernmental assessment).

**Refuter:** show that conversion is a reversible subtraction, that exclusion is available, or that restoration returns the original habitat rather than substituting another one.

### F4  -  The biodiversity limb is a cascade, so first-order scope systematically undercounts it

Fig 11 :43, *"The habitat is enumerated, never composed."* Fig 11 :44, *"List everything in contact with the residual, then what is in contact with those, and outward until the cascade stops mattering."* Fig 11 :52, *"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three"*.

The material biodiversity loss from a conversion is typically not at the point of conversion. It is three steps out. The IPCC land report (opened) states that reduced resources for herbivores *"can have knock-on consequences for predators, which can potentially lead to disruptive ecological cascades (limited evidence, low agreement)"*. The ecological cascade reference (opened) defines a cascade as *"a series of secondary extinctions that are triggered by the primary extinction of a key species in an ecosystem"* and gives the sea otter and kelp case.

**Mode:** `specified`, triangulated across Fig 11, the IPCC text and the ecology reference (low overlap between a figure law and an ecology source).

**Refuter:** show that the biodiversity effect is strictly first order, with no second-order or third-order contact effect that matters for the residual.

### F5  -  Valence flips between the food residual and the biodiversity residual

Fig 11 :49, *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."* Fig 11 :51, *"No standing list of good actors survives a change of residual."*

A high-yield cultivar, a fertiliser, an irrigation scheme or a new road is a benefactor to the yield residual and a malfactor to the biodiversity residual in the same place. There is no standing list of good intensification, because valence attaches to the residual, not to the actor. IPCC (opened): *"Land use caused global biodiversity to decrease by around 11–14%"*. The same lever that the food frame scores as progress is what the biodiversity frame scores as loss.

**Mode:** `specified`, triangulated across Fig 11 and IPCC.

**Refuter:** exhibit one lever that improves yield, biodiversity and climate at the same place and time with no shifted burden. A gain in one place produced by displacement elsewhere does not count.

### F6  -  The *and* is a union of two conditions with different anchors

Fig 9 :4, *"are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search."* Fig 9 :54, *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 9 :55, *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

Biodiversity loss is settled by species and habitat measurements; climate change is settled by atmospheric concentration and forcing. They are not two readings of one quantity, and a single verdict cannot average them. Land conversion is the overlapping condition where the two bind together. A lever that clears the biodiversity-only region (spare land here) can leave the intersection standing (production displaced to a frontier, carrying both losses).

**Mode:** `general` on the union structure, `specified` on the two limbs, triangulated across Fig 9 and the separate anchor definitions in Fig 2.

**Refuter:** show that biodiversity loss and climate forcing share one anchor and that one lever clears both without displacement.

### F7  -  The climate limb: a finite stock becomes a spendable gradient, and its anchor does not update

Fig 13 :14, *"conserved, finite, directionless alone"*, on energy. Fig 13 :22, *"a difference that can be spent"*, on a gradient. Fig 10 :24, *"the ground does not decay, the channel does"*.

A standing forest or soil carbon stock is finite and has no direction until a gradient is applied; conversion spends the difference and removes the sink. The atmospheric anchor does not change because a model reports a different land-use scenario, but the reporting channel decays (saturated metrics, pooled readings). IPCC (opened): *"Land use contributes about one-quarter of global greenhouse gas emissions"* and *"Food trade can also have negative environmental impacts by displacing the effects of overconsumption"*.

**Mode:** `specified` on the flux, `general` on the gradient reading, single-source on the figure side for the gradient mapping; world corroboration from IPCC.

**Refuter:** show a land conversion with no net carbon flux, or a climate anchor that moved in response to a frame's report rather than to emissions.

### F8  -  The trigger is conditional, not a universal law (a world disagreement returned to census)

Two opened world sources pull in opposite directions. Our World in Data (Ritchie and Roser, quoting Ausubel, Wernick and Waggoner 2013): *"A combination of agricultural technologies raised yields, keeping downward pressure on the extent of cropland and sparing land for nature."* The Jevons paradox reference, Agriculture section (opened): *"increasing efficiency may make it more profitable to grow wheat and lead farmers to convert land to the production of wheat, thereby increasing land use instead."*

Per the conflict rule, this is not resolved by choosing a side. It is returned to the census. The disagreement sits entirely in D2, the decision link: demand elasticity, tenure, trade and policy select the regime. Both statements can be true in different slots at different times. The figure verdict about the *shape* of the chain is untouched; what changes is that the yield-to-conversion link must be stated as conditional on the decision frame's terms. The question asks *could*, and the honest answer is that it could under a specifiable set of conditions, not that it always does.

**Mode:** `specified`, `general` on the conditionality. Two opened sources in different fields (economic history and land-use data), but they are a genuine disagreement, not two agreeing witnesses.

**Refuter:** show that a single regime holds everywhere, with the decision variable that selects it named and measured in the same dataset.

### F9  -  No figure returns a magnitude; magnitude is a declared gap

Fig 9 :32, *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*. Fig 13 :134, *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*. Fig 8 :39, *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."*

No figure line returns hectares converted, species lost, or parts per million. The figures return the shape: per-slot indexing, a conditional mediation link, an asymmetric substitution, a cascade, a union tail and two non-updating anchors. The numerical answer is owed to instruments: land-cover time series, species surveys, flux towers and atmospheric measurements. Until those are read for a particular place and period, the magnitude is `gap`, reported as unreached rather than as silence.

**Mode:** `general` on the shape, `gap` on the number.

**Refuter:** a figure line that returns a hectare, species or ppm value (there is none), or an instrument reading for the specific place and period, which would settle magnitude without changing the shape.

### F10  -  Filters are not witnesses

Fig 9 :60, *"Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict."*

Global yield indices, production elasticities and model scenario ranges are filters that decide where to look and how much budget to spend. They are not witnesses for whether a specific conversion happened for a specific reason. This matters because the easy evidence in this field is global and modeled, while the decisive evidence is local and measured.

**Mode:** `general`, single-source on the figure side.

**Refuter:** show a global model output that by itself settles a local land-cover verdict without a ground reading.

## 4. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per the design-instrument rule: a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument. The shape claim (four-link chain, union tail, per-slot trigger, asymmetric substitution, cascade, two non-updating anchors) is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. Land conversion is a reversible subtraction from a fixed habitat, with exclusion available, breaking Fig 11 :61 and :66.
2. The biodiversity effect is strictly first order, with no second-order or third-order contact effect that matters for the residual, breaking Fig 11 :52.
3. Yield change and conversion are one frame reading one evidence pool, with no independent decision mediation, so the "trigger" is only a correlation (Fig 2 :40).
4. A per-region, per-slot measurement shows that yield change did not change land-cover contact in the relevant slots, breaking F1 and F2 at the ground.
5. Biodiversity loss and climate forcing share one anchor, so the outcomes form a partition rather than a union, breaking F6.
6. One lever improves yield, biodiversity and climate at the same place and time with no displaced burden, breaking F5.

If any fires, the shape is withdrawn or rewritten. Until one is run, no part of this folder may be labelled `grounded`.

## 5. Recommendation

1. Answer *could* with a conditional yes, not a universal law and not a number. The structure makes the path possible and explains why it is undercounted (F1, F4, F6).
2. State the mode on every substantive claim (F9).
3. Keep the biodiversity and climate verdicts separate and never average them (F6).
4. Scope the habitat to at least second order before claiming a small biodiversity effect (F4).
5. Treat the yield-to-conversion link as a union of conditions, and name the decision variables that select the regime (F2, F8).
6. Lead outreach with the shape and the pre-registered refuter, then ask what would count as a failure. Offer links only after the verdict is recorded (Fig 10 :42, Fig 10 :52).

## 8. Updated verdict

The shape survives and is useful. Yield change can trigger land conversion, but only through a per-slot decision link whose regime is selected by price, tenure, policy and demand; conversion is an introduction and a habitat substitution with asymmetric power; the biodiversity loss that matters is a second- or third-order cascade that first-order assessment misses; the two outcomes form a union with different anchors and cannot be averaged. What does not survive is the universal form of the claim, the idea that conversion is reversible subtraction, and any numerical magnitude asserted without a ground reading. Mode `specified` on the per-domain claims, `general` on the structural shape, `gap` on magnitude, ungrounded, candidate.
