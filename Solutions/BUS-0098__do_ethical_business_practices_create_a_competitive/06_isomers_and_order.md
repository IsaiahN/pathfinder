## Q1 - Are two firms with the same ethics code isomers?

> **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."** (Fig 12 :5)
> **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."** (Fig 12 :117)
> **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** (Fig 12 :124)
> **"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."** (Fig 12 :92)

Two firms with an identical published code have the same atoms. If one composes those atoms as a repeated sequence applied to every decision and the other as a reconfigurable network that changes with the stakeholder, they are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117: same atoms, different bond, different behaviour, with the difference invisible in the published code. This is the figure-grounded version of "it is not what you say, it is what you do with it", but it is stronger, because the arrangement is itself a substance and carries information the ingredient list does not.

If the firms share only a pattern (both use `principle → decision → market`) while differing in which principles occupy the slots, the weaker claim holds: they are members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124), the qualifier saying which member. The presence of the pattern fixes the kind, not the outcome.

The distinction is testable. A code comparison that finds no difference between two firms cannot distinguish isomer from functional-group; the bond has to be recorded. The opened Cooperation (evolution) page gives an independent, low-overlap version: in by-product mutualism both parties benefit, but *"neither participant's behavior yields a benefit from the other, and thus cooperation is not taking place."* Same observable benefit, different relation.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if two firms with identical atoms and identical bonds nevertheless diverge in persistence with no difference in ground contact or imported atoms, the isomer framing is insufficient and the difference must be habitat (D2), which would move the explanation out of D3.

**Status:** `specified`, triangulated for the legality of the reading (Fig 12 and Fig 11 :50, different figures), single-source for the business application.

## Q2 - What order do the conditions bind in?

> **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."** (Fig 3 :20-21)
> **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** (Fig 3 :19)
> **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."** (Fig 3 :25-26)

Order for a firm facing an ethics question in a changed market:

1. **Perception.** Can it read both the market's return and the harm its practice causes? Failure here is visible and has obvious tests (accounting, audits, complaints).
2. **Vocabulary.** Does its Γ have terms for what the changed situation affords, and can it say what would count as progress on the harm axis? This is the middle link. It has almost no instrument.
3. **Objective.** Can it state a comparison that would count as getting somewhere? Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."* A vocabulary with only ingredients cannot state a goal on either axis.
4. **Planning.** A route to that objective under incomplete information.
5. **Learn and carry.** Name what worked, verify it against the ground, carry it to the next problem.

The two conditions map onto this chain asymmetrically. C1 (the pay condition) is mostly a failure of links 1 and 2: the firm's reading of what pays goes stale, saturated or pooled. C2 (the obligation condition) is mostly a failure of link 3: the firm can name duties but cannot state what would count as keeping them, so the commitment stays a convention (Fig 10 :47). That asymmetry is why a single lever cannot clear both (Fig 9 :55).

The order also decides which instrument to buy first. Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."* The market link (1) has accounting and share data; the obligation link (3) has almost none. Budget piles up where the measurements already exist.

**Falsifier:** a firm whose chain demonstrably works at 1, 2 and 3 and fails only at 4. That would falsify the "usually middle" claim of F8 in the primary audit.

## Q3 - Where does refusal enter?

> **"refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so"** (Fig 5 :53)
> **"A term that refuses another pays the same bargain. Stating the refusal, plus what remains unexplained after it, costs less than what was unexplained before."** (Fig 5 :54-55)
> **"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"** (Operators table :58)
> **"¬ is the refusal of a claim."** (Operators table :73)

"Should be met regardless of financial return" is a refusal of a claim, not a claim about the magnitude of return. The claim it refuses is "the market return settles whether this practice should be kept". Fig 5 :50-52 says that when nothing is scoring, the frame can aim a refusal at the outcome it did not want rather than at the belief it holds; the remedy is to make the sought-for shape an ordinary term and let it accrue a residual.

Here the refusal is aimed at the belief that the market is the legitimate anchor for a question about persons. That is the belief Fig 2 :36-37 refuses, so the negation is well aimed. The firm's ethics code is the refused-and-replaced term: it pays the same MDL bargain, because stating the code plus what remains unexplained costs less than the confusion it removes. But Fig 5 :55 also says *"What it removes is not a term but a term’s standing to be composed over."* A code that refuses the market's standing to decide must itself be settleable; otherwise Fig 10 :49 applies, *"Declaring a meaning does not ground it."*

The order matters: refuse before mint (Formula step 2). A firm that first builds a reputation on the code and only later asks whether the market may decide has already spent the independence of its checkers (Fig 2 :40).

**Falsifier:** a case in which the negation operates on the wrong term, that is, the firm refuses the market's standing but the actual wrong belief was about the practice's effect. In that case the refusal is an echo, not a correction, and this Q3 collapses into a naming error in D3.

## Q4 - Competing explanations, and what each can and cannot reach

| candidate explanation | what it names | figure-line bearing | what it cannot reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Instrumental stakeholder theory** (Donaldson and Preston 1995, via the opened stakeholder page: *"connections... between the management of stakeholder groups and the achievement of corporate goals"*) | C1, the pay condition | close to Fig 11 :39-40 and Fig 6 :23 | It has no anchor rule for harm questions, so it cannot reach C2. It is one aspect of a three-part theory, not the whole answer |
| **Normative stakeholder theory** (same page: *"moral or philosophical guidelines for the operation and management of the corporation"*) | C2, the obligation condition | close to Fig 2 :36-37 and Fig 10 :19 | It does not say what would refute a particular code, and it does not separate C1 from C2. Goodpaster's *"ethics without business"* is the failure mode |
| **Shareholder primacy** (Friedman doctrine via Wikipedia: *"one and only one social responsibility of business... within the rules of the game... without deception or fraud"*) | the market anchor and the rules of the game | part of D1; note that Friedman himself includes ethical custom and no fraud, so it is not pure profit | It cannot settle a question constitutively about persons (Fig 2 :36-37), and Zingales's "theorem, not a doctrine" reading shows its conclusion depends on assumptions |
| **CSR / shared value** (opened CSR page: *"strategic CSR... based on brand alignment, stakeholder integration, and ethical behaviour"*) | an import that tries to move the wall | close to Fig 6 :23 when it really brings a new atom | The page's own specification dispute shows a reading cannot name its source (F7). Reinhardt's imitability condition bites: a copyable programme adds no atom |
| **Reputation and signalling** (opened CSR page: firms adopting responsible behaviour *"are primarily investing in their reputations"*; Cooperation page: a reliable signal must be costly) | D4, the channel | Fig 1 :26-27; Fig 10 :24 | Frame-internal. It cannot settle the action (essay: prestige is not action) |
| **Reciprocal altruism** (Trivers 1971 via Wikipedia) | the repeated-interaction and cheater-detection condition on C1 | low-overlap corroboration of F5 | It is an animal-behaviour analogue; Fig 4 :20 requires the anchor to be renamed at the firm scale |
| **Strong reciprocity** (behavioural economics and evolutionary anthropology via Wikipedia) | cooperation without apparent benefit; valence flips with the group | low-overlap corroboration of Fig 11 :49-51 | The page records the criticism that lab effects may not generalise to the field; the construct does not identify a particular practice |
| **Luck / information asymmetry** | an unearned early reading that persists while unfuted | Fig 8 :40: *"Underivable-in-principle and unreachable-at-this-budget are different facts"* | Cannot be excluded from inside a frame; Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* Honest `gap`, not a rival verdict |

No candidate is discarded by prestige or familiarity. Each is ranged against the same residual, in the order Fig 8 :53 requires. The point is not to pick one; it is to notice that the first five are all in one evidence pool (Fig 2 :31) and that the two low-overlap fields corroborate mechanism, not the firm-level fact.

## Q5 - Does the shape generate the literature's answers?

Partly. The shape can generate:

- instrumental stakeholder theory's emphasis on stakeholder management and performance, as C1 under a closure that has not met a differing closure;
- normative stakeholder theory's emphasis on guidelines, as C2 with an anchor rule and a refusal that can be violated;
- shareholder primacy's "rules of the game" clause, as the convention layer that becomes a grounded obligation only when breach has a cost (Fig 10 :19, :47);
- the CSR and shared-value emphasis on strategic integration, as C2 and Fig 6 :23, with the imitability caveat from Reinhardt;
- the reputation critique, as D4 and Fig 1 :27.

It cannot generate the *name* of the specific practice that will work next. That is the `gap` recorded in F10 of `01`. It is a gap at the instrument boundary, not a silence: Fig 13 :134 (*"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence,"*) and Fig 8 :39 apply. The source essay states why: an instrument that reads a result *"cannot comment on the source"*, and *"The answer is unreached until a better instrument exists."*

## CONCLUSION

Two firms with the same code but different bonds are isomers under Fig 12 :117; two firms sharing only a `principle → decision → market` pattern are members of one functional group under Fig 12 :124. The conditions bind in dependency order, with the vocabulary and objective link the usual break, which is why C1 and C2 need different instruments. Obligation enters as a negation aimed at the market's standing to decide a question about persons, and it must itself be settleable or it remains a convention. The standard business explanations are mostly bindings of one evidence pool and are generated by the shape only up to the point where a name would be required. The isomer claim has a written falsifier; the order claim has one; the refusal has one; the naming gap is declared rather than filled.
