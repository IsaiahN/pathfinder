**Subject:** Check one claim about official discretion before you decide whether the framework is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`general` on the shape, `specified` on the institutional application, not `grounded`):** an official's decision is not one question with two answers. It is a union of two conditions, each with its own loop and its own ground. Before acting, the official compares two predictions: the cost of deviating from the codified rule, and the cost of defying the unwritten network expectation. The comparison is a threshold, not a merger of the two into a third thing, and the weighting between private history and inherited prior is learnable from past outcomes. The residual is measured per official and per case, never averaged across officials.
>
> An unwritten expectation does useful work: it coordinates shared meaning where a written rule cannot. It becomes a failure at the moment it decides an appointment, a protection or an exoneration. At that point a convention has taken the verdict seat, and a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one. A network whose answer changes when its members push on it is not an anchor.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that informal norms are always corrupt; that a code of ethics alone changes behaviour; that a corruption index settles a particular case; that the framework can name which official will follow which action. It returns a shape, not a name. The shape is: which closure must be reachable, which anchor must not update, which weighting is learnable. Which person acts in which case needs a ground reading, an enforcement record that discriminates the two closures.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim.** I wrote these down before sending this:
>
> 1. One decision variable predicts per-case choices across both conditions with the per-case residual trending down and no third term bound.
> 2. An official follows the higher-predicted-cost action with no new term (duty, identity, oath or threat) bound and predicting.
> 3. Two officials with identical histories and identical weighting diverge with no new term bound.
> 4. A network expectation is shown to be a genuine anchor, that is, one that does not change its answer when members push on it.
> 5. An oversight room with an unauthored, differing pool and different information still fails to range against the enforcement record while a mirrored room succeeds.
> 6. An actor's valence toward the residual is invariant across a documented change of residual.
>
> If any one holds, I will narrow or withdraw the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
