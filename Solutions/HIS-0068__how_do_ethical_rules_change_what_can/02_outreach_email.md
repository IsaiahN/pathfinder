**Subject:** Check one claim about research ethics before you decide whether the derivation is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one structural claim about how ethical rules change research, and I would like you to test it against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the claim does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The claim (`specified`, not `grounded`):** an ethical rule is a refusal, not a subtraction. It leaves the topic in the record and removes that topic's standing to be composed over, so the set of studyable slots falls by addition rather than deletion. The effect is per slot, never global: a consent rule closes one kind of trial and leaves observational work open. Two different things then need separate verdicts, what can be studied (a reachability question) and what counts as an acceptable method (an admissibility question). Averaging them loses the signal.
>
> The legitimacy condition is the part I most want checked. A rule is a proper anchor only when the question is constitutively about the people the rule touches, for example "may this be done to these people". Applied to a world-question such as "does this treatment work", the same rule is an instrument, and an instrument can be wrong on axes it does not measure. When a review convention is used to decide the world-question, it has stopped being a convention. I also claim that moving a study to another habitat (human to animal to organoid to simulation) is a substitution, not a removal, with two silent failure modes: what you failed to reproduce is invisible until the goal fails, and what you unintentionally introduced is invisible until it acts.
>
> **What would refute it, written before this message:**
> 1. a rule that erases a topic from the record rather than refusing its standing;
> 2. a rule whose effect is uniform across every studyable slot and scale;
> 3. an ethics convention that settles a world-question without ground contact;
> 4. a review body that authors the protocol it approves and still gets a sound verdict;
> 5. a standard no real lab can meet that nevertheless protects subjects;
> 6. a habitat substitution that reproduces everything the goal required, introduces nothing, and leaves the readings unchanged.
>
> If any one holds, I withdraw or narrow the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract in my working package): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`, especially Fig 1 :19-22, Fig 2 :29-37, Fig 5 :55, Fig 6 :42, Fig 9 :51 and :54, Fig 10 :42-54, Fig 11 :40-69.

> - Declaration of Helsinki, World Medical Association (public policy page, adopted 1964, current revision 2024): https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki-ethical-principles-for-medical-research-involving-human-subjects/
> - The 3Rs, NC3Rs (public page): https://www.nc3rs.org.uk/3rs/
>

Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it.
