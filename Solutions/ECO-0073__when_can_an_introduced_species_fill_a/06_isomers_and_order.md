Two supporting questions are owed: (1) what is the bond structure of the compound, and (2) what order does the decision chain run in, and where does it break? ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What bond joins the two conditions?

- Operators table: **∥ disjunction** - *"either suffices; not both required"*, test *"remove one. Does it still work? Yes."*
- Operators table: **+ conjunction** - *"both hold at once"*, test *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."*
- The two outcomes are not + in the manager's aim (the aim is to fill F, not to also disrupt), but they can be + in the world. Fig 11 :50: **"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another."** One actor, two residuals, both relations holding at once.
- So the world state is a **+** over residuals (role restored on slot s1 AND resident declined on slot s2), while the manager's selection rule wrongly treats it as **∥** (either outcome suffices). The error is a bond error, not a missing atom. Fig 12 :5: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*
- **Falsifier:** show a case where the role-restored relation and the disruption relation never hold at the same time for the same actor under any residual split. Then the bond is ∥ and the union reading is wrong.

### Q2 - Is the act of introduction a production bond?

- Fig 12 :76-77 (production, ⇒): **"A produces B; B did not exist before"**, test *"is there a B before A fires? No."*
- The lost function F is absent before the introduction and present after, in the successful case. So `A ⇒ F` is the correct bond for "fill a role", not `A + F` (both hold at once) and not `A → F` (sequence, where F would already be waiting).
- But the same introduction can also fire `A ⇒ D` where D is a new disruption. One actor, two production bonds, different operands. Fig 12 :8: *"Melt / Freeze / Boil ... three different changes, one written formula"* is the warning: if you record only the ingredients (A, F, D), nothing downstream can tell the bonds apart.
- Fig 12 :112: **"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."** A management plan written as a sequence of introductions can state what happens and cannot state what would count as getting somewhere unless the objective (a comparison, ⋛) is bound. This is the middle link of Fig 3.

### Q3 - Are the two outcomes isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123-124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- "Fills a role" and "intensifies disruption" with the **same** actor and the **same** habitat but different relations (different bonds to different residents) are isomers under Fig 12's definition: same atoms, different bond, different substance. The behaviour differs (function returns versus resident declines) while the ingredient list is identical.
- However, if the two outcomes involve different habitats or different residents, they are not isomers; they are two members of a functional group (an actor introduced to perform a function) with different atoms. The isomer claim is therefore conditional on holding the atom set fixed. State the condition; do not assert the stronger form by default.
- **Falsifier:** if one outcome requires an atom the other does not (a resident absent from the first case, a condition absent from the second), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 - Analogs the figure names

- **Catalyst** (Fig 12 :119): *"A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* An ecosystem engineer (beaver, coral) is this: the actor persists and keeps modulating resources. Jones et al. 1994, *"Ecosystem engineers are organisms that directly or indirectly modulate the availability of resources to other species, by causing physical state changes in biotic or abiotic materials. In so doing they modify, maintain and create habitats."* A one-off introduction cannot be scored as if the actor were spent on first use.
- **Cycle** (Fig 12 :121): *"The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Restoring a function can change the habitat, which changes what the actor affords, which changes the function. A monitoring chain that records only one pass cannot see the loop. This is why Fig 11 :63 says a managed ecosystem is permanent work.
- **Negation** (Operators table :53-57): *"this does not hold"*, test *"is the point that it is false, rather than absent?"* A refused introduction is a negation, not a subtraction. Removing an actor is not the same as refusing a claim that it fills the role. The distinction matters for correction: an introduction that fails cannot simply be deleted from the record (Fig 6 :42-45, the reach falls because something was added).

### Q5 - What order does the chain run in, and where does it break?

- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- Fig 3 :24-26: *"Why the middle link is where chains usually break. Perception has obvious failures and obvious tests. Planning has obvious failures and obvious tests. The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither."*
- For this question the chain is: perceive the lost function (s1) -> enumerate what the habitat and actor afford (s2, Fig 11 :43-48) -> state what counts as filling the role and what would count as disruption (s3, a comparison) -> plan and introduce (s4) -> monitor and carry (s5).
- The documented failure is at s2-s3: *"few conservation translocation programs explicitly state or monitor function as an objective"* (Andrews et al. 2022) and Defra 2025 asks *"What does success look like?"* before the plan. Effort spent at s4 (better introduction technique) cannot rescue a break at s3.
- Fig 3 :19: **"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."** This is the figure-grounded reason the answer is a set of conditions on enumeration and objectives, not a better stock of candidate species.
- **Falsifier:** a documented program that skipped the objective and enumeration steps and still detected third-order disruption at introduction time, with a measurement. That would move the break.

## CONCLUSION

The two conditions join by **+** in the world and are wrongly treated as **∥** in selection. The successful act is a **production** bond (A ⇒ F), and the failed or disruptive act is a second production bond (A ⇒ D) over the same actor. Under a fixed atom set the two outcomes are **isomers** (Fig 12 :117); without a fixed atom set they are a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123). The engine actor is a **catalyst** in Fig 12's sense and the feedback is a **cycle**, so the management chain has no single settled state. The decision chain breaks most often at the objective link (Fig 3 :24-26), which is exactly where the world literature reports the gap. All of this is structural and `specified`; none of it names a species or settles a case.
