# Structure, order and analogues: supply chains as bonded arrangements

**Mode: `specified`.** A simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks what "domestic capacity" is, structurally, and in what order the two policies must be considered. Two sub-questions are owed: (1) is the object of policy an inventory of industries or an arrangement of them, and (2) what is the dependency order that makes a verdict at one step valid rather than a reading of nothing.

## DOMAIN CENSUS (supporting)

Atoms held in the policy frame: productive capabilities (fabs, chemical plants, assembly, logistics, skills, research). Bonds held: the seven from Fig 12, chiefly sequence (→), production (⇒), subtraction (−) and comparison (⋛), plus the eighth operator negation (¬) for refusal. No new atom is available without import (Fig 6). The currency is continuity under a named cut. The anchor is a supplier graph plus an actual cut with a measured cost.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Is the object a list or an arrangement?

Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."

Fig 12 :117: "Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

A national industry list records atoms and not bonds. Two countries with the same list can have different continuity because the bonds differ. Under Fig 12's definition they are **isomers**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. That is the structural claim worth testing. A policy that counts domestic plants is using the flat formula of Fig 12's first collapse; the difference it needs to measure is the one the formula cannot write.

Fig 12 :99: "A bond costs what it could have been, and nothing more." A redundant supplier link is priced by the number of alternative links that could have occupied that slot and by what they cost to maintain. This is why "more domestic" is not a monotone improvement: a bond that costs nothing carries nothing, and a link that duplicates a common upstream source carries no independence.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit two national capability sets with identical atoms and identical bond topology whose continuity under the same named cut is decisively different for reasons no further bond captures. Then the bond does not carry the information Fig 12 assigns to it, and the isomer claim fails.

### Q2: What is the order of the two policies?

Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

Fig 3 :20-21: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

The dependency order is not "first decide free trade or protection". It is:

1. **Can "strategic" be anchored?** Name a cut scenario and its cost. Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."
2. **Describe the residual before choosing a frame** (Fig 9 :27-28). Otherwise whichever industrial policy is fashionable will seem to fit.
3. **Refuse the blanket term before minting a new one** (Formula step 2). The claim "comparative advantage always wins" is a held term with a persistently positive residual; it owes a refusal first.
4. **Settle the allocation link, which already works, and leave it working** (Fig 3 :19). Use market prices as the budget filter.
5. **Only then price independence** (Fig 2 :42) against the cost of being wrong, on the bond map from Q1.

A verdict delivered at step 5 without steps 1 to 3 is a reading below the break. It is not a weak verdict; by Fig 3 :22-23 it is "a reading of nothing."

### Q4: Where are the strains?

- **Fig 13 applied to industries.** Considered and dropped. Fig 13's six terms are a physical substrate (space, time, energy; spectrum, gradient, network). Reading industries as "states" and supply relations as "structures" is a strained analogy, so Fig 13 does not govern this question. Recorded as a strain, not smoothed over.
- **Fig 12's isomer definition at the national scale.** Fig 12 is written about molecules. Applying it to supplier graphs is a cross-level import with a source gate, not a literal use. It is marked `specified` for that reason.
- **"Independent supplier" is not yet an instrument.** Fig 2 says buy independence in proportion to cost of being wrong, but the exact test for two suppliers sharing an upstream source needs a supplier graph and a measurement. This is the live type question carried into `11_audit.md`.

## CONCLUSION

"Domestic capacity" is not an inventory; it is a bonded arrangement. Two countries with the same atoms and different bonds are isomers under Fig 12, and their behaviour under the same cut is decisive in a way their ingredient lists cannot show. The order of deliberation is fixed by dependency: anchor the word "strategic", describe the residual, refuse the blanket term, keep the working market link, then price independence on the bond map. The analogues corroborate the shape from ecology, finance, engineering and trade policy, with one conflict that was resolved by re-census rather than by flipping a verdict. The isomer claim and the order claim both have written falsifiers; neither has been measured.
