# Isomers, bonds and order: the supporting derivation for ECN-0060

Two structural questions remain: (1) are the two policy types **isomers** under Fig 12, so that a single formula cannot tell them apart; and (2) what **order** and **bond** the comparison itself has, and where the dependency chain actually breaks.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are the growth policy and the redistribution policy isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :5 (as recorded in the extract): a set of ingredients is not a molecule; the bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording.

Write the two policies over the shared atom set `{households, income, instruments, time}`:

- **Growth policy:** `effort ⇒ income` (production: *"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, Operators table :37), composed with a tax rule that leaves the split approximately where it was. The binding relation is production.
- **Redistribution policy:** `income(A) − transfer → income(B)` (subtraction: *"A with B removed or absent"*, Operators table :43), where the absence of the transferred amount from A is the point of the operation.

Same atoms, different bond, different substance. They are **isomers** in Fig 12's exact sense. This is why a formula that records only the ingredient list (total revenue, total transfers, net fiscal balance) cannot tell a growth policy from a redistribution policy: the difference lives in the bond, which the flat formula does not record.

If the two policies are written narrowly, growth over `{income, production}` and redistribution over `{income, transfer}`, they share only `income`. Then they are not isomers but members of the same **functional group** (Fig 12 :124): **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."** Both are "policy that changes income"; the qualifier distinguishes the bond.

- **Falsifier, written before this conclusion:** if both policies can be written with the **same bond** over the same atoms, differing only in a continuous parameter of magnitude, the isomer claim fails and they are one policy family on a single axis. That would also revive the possibility of a single comparative score. It is a real empirical and structural question, not a rhetorical one.

### Q2 - What bond is the comparison itself?

- Operators table :49: ⋛ is **"a threshold relation between two quantities"**, identified by the test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present? Yes."*
- Operators table :52: **"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"**
- Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."*

So the comparison of A and B is ⋛, **not** a sequence (→), **not** a conjunction (+) and **not** an addition of the two effects. Conflating it with a sequence treats "compare" as "do A then B"; conflating it with conjunction treats "both matter" as "both hold at once". Neither identifies which is larger, which is the whole point of ⋛.

The comparison carries two explicit parameters: the two quantities compared, and the threshold that decides which is larger. The question as posed supplies neither, which is `01` F6. Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*: the framework returns the kind of comparison owed, not the winner.

### Q3 - Where does the dependency chain break?

- Fig 3 :20-21: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
- Fig 3 :25-26: **"The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."**

Perception (national accounts, surveys) and vocabulary (tax, transfer, incidence) work. Link 3, the objective, has no instrument. Planning and learning sit below it. The correct image is not "economists lack data". It is "economists lack a ground-returned objective, and better data arrives at a step that cannot use it". Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

### Q4 - Is the union a partition?

- Fig 9 :54: **"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."**

The three unbundled cases are:

| case | condition A (total changes) | condition B (split changes) | lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| pure efficiency | yes | no | productivity or production policy |
| pure distribution | no | yes | a redistributive tax with offsetting behaviour that holds total fixed |
| compound | yes | yes | most real tax and transfer packages |

Fig 9 :57-58: **"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either. It stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound."** The compound is not a point between the other two; it has its own loop and must be evaluated on its own terms. A single lever that resolves the pure-efficiency region leaves the intersection standing.

### Q5 - Round-trip on the comparison shape

Fig 4 :30: *"So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked."*

1. **Sent up (x):** the verbatim figure rules, chiefly the no-add rule (Fig 12 :125-130), per-slot indexing (Fig 1 :19, :22), union unbundling (Fig 9 :54-58) and the anchor definition (Fig 2 :29).
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** the shorthand used in outreach: "hold two currencies apart, report per group, unbundle, name the threshold, record provenance".
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** a concrete ranking procedure that a policy evaluator would actually run on data.

The gap `R_T` is **nonzero** and is recorded qualitatively in `11_audit.md`. What drops out in the round trip is the anchor condition (nothing in the shorthand forces the threshold to be ground-settled), the union intersection (a flat checklist tends to treat the compound as two simple cases), and the per-slot requirement (an implementation can revert to a national average). The residue that survives cleanly is: the two currencies are not additive, and the objective has no anchor. That is the part worth sending.

## CONCLUSION

The two policy types are **isomers over the shared atom set** (or a functional group under the narrower writing), differing in bond rather than ingredients, which is why a single flat summary cannot distinguish them. The comparison is a ⋛ goal with a threshold, not a sequence or a conjunction. The dependency chain breaks at the objective link, not at measurement. The union must be unbundled before any comparison is run. The isomer claim has a stated falsifier (same bond, same atoms, continuous parameter); the ranking claim remains a `gap` (no anchor).
