# Outreach draft: check the shape of the comparison before you decide about the answer

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through a public venue in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** nothing in this email asks the recipient to read the framework first, to trust it, or to agree with it. If the stage-1 output fails, there is nothing else to look at.

---

**Subject:** one checkable claim about comparing growth policies and redistribution policies

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the theory first and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified` on the shape, `gap` on the ranking):** a policy that raises total income and a policy that spreads income more evenly are recorded in two different currencies. The first is money income, anchored in markets and national accounts. The second is the arrangement of income across persons, anchored in records and surveys. To rank the two, a third quantity is needed: a social objective saying how much of one may be traded for the other. The standard candidates (utilitarian sum, maximin, Bergson-Samuelson welfare function, Kaldor-Hicks compensation test) all change their answer when their weights or comparison assumptions are changed. A thing that moves when you push on it is not an anchor. So I do not report a winner. I report the shape a comparison procedure would have to have.
>
> **The shape:** report the two currencies separately rather than adding them into one score; report them per group or percentile rather than as one national average; separate the pure-efficiency case, the pure-distribution case and the compound case where one lever moves both; state the two quantities and the threshold the comparison applies; and record where the objective came from, because importing a welfare function gives access to a convention, not a new ground fact.
>
> **What I am explicitly not claiming:** that one policy is better, that a particular welfare function is correct, that a single summary statistic can rank them, or that this derivation is a measurement. It is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against a figure set, not a ground reading. It cannot settle the question it reports on.
>
> **What would refute it, written before this email:**
> 1. an objective whose ranking of the two policies does not change under any admissible reweighting and is not just a measured quantity;
> 2. a unit in which total income and the full distribution are commensurable without a lossy, convention-dependent conversion;
> 3. an instrument that settles what counts as progress when the two conflict and returns different readings for the two policies;
> 4. a single summary that preserves every decision-relevant group effect for every policy considered.
>
> If any one holds, the shape claim is wrong and I will retract it. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Canonical usage rule (local): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`
> - Expert packet with the three checkable sub-claims: `08_expert_verification_packet.md` in this folder
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it: a loop cannot check the direction of its own question, so an unwritten refuter lets any result be fitted. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
