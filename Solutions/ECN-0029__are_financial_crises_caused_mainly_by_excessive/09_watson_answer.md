## QUESTION

Decide, in effect terms, what the Tether says about the claim that financial crises are caused mainly by excessive risk-taking, unstable credit growth, bad regulation, or structural features of finance. Option A: treat the four as rival answers and select a winner. Option B: test the framing, unbundle the compound, and keep per-domain verdicts with separate anchors. What each leads to: A produces a single-cause verdict that aggregates away per-episode residuals and selects a school before the residual is described, and it spends the independence of the people asked to check it. B produces a checkable union shape, refuters written in advance, and an open slot for the dominant factor until an anchor settles it.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain with actors, currency and anchor; partition or union; order by dependency.

- **R (the residual).** The gap between a crisis prediction built from a causal description and the crises that actually occur. Arity: four candidate conditions plus their intersections, so a compound. Symmetry: low, because the currency differs across domains (behaviour, flows, rules, architecture). Scale: individual decisions through balance sheets to market architecture, connected by a lossy transform. Fig 9 :4-5 names this a non-shrinking disagreement to be split, not searched.
- **Domain A, excessive risk-taking.** Actors: investors, borrowers, lenders, fund managers. Currency: incentives, expectations, herd behaviour, default. Anchor: observed decisions and default data. Nameable, so this is a domain. First-order and directly visible in the habitat.
- **Domain B, unstable credit growth.** Actors: banks, lenders, borrowers, credit creation. Currency: growth rates of credit and asset prices. Anchor: credit aggregates and leading indicators. Nameable. Second-order, operating on the same actors as A but over an aggregation and a time derivative.
- **Domain C, bad regulation.** Actors: regulators, legislators, agencies. Currency: rules, enforcement, capture. Anchor: rule changes and post-reform outcomes, but partially updating because rules respond to crises. This is where the anchor test is weakest. A frame acting on the habitat from inside.
- **Domain D, structural features.** Actors: balance sheets, leverage, interconnectedness, shadow banking, market structure. Currency: fragility, contagion, systemic risk. Anchor: exposure and balance-sheet data. Nameable. Highest-order in the contact cascade.
- **Partition or union.** Union of overlapping conditions, not a partition. Fig 9 :55-56: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones. A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*
- **Order by dependency.** Perceive the crisis, have terms for what the situation affords, state what would count as "mainly", plan a lever, carry the result. The third link is uninstrumented. Contact order is separate: A first, B and D further out, C acting on the habitat.
- **Strain test.** An initial temptation to apply Figure 13's physics six-term set to finance was re-censused as strained. Figure 4 :19-20 governs the transfer instead: *"The kernel is the part that does not change with size: the same loop and the same rule for naming what worked, whether one agent is running it or a thousand. Only the actors, the currency and the anchor change."* Figure 12's isomer and cycle structures are used as analogues only, with the bond mechanics not transferred.

## VERDICT (from the figures)

The union shape survives; the question's own framing does not. The four candidates are overlapping conditions, not rival answers. "Mainly" aggregates away the per-episode signal, so verdicts are kept per domain with separate anchors and never averaged. Contact order runs from the visible risk-taker outward through credit and balance-sheet structure, with regulation acting on the habitat from inside; this is an ordering warning, not a claim that structure dominates. Regulation is a convention that can become an outcome variable, and its sign and stake must be declared. No figure line governs causal share, so no factor is named as dominant. That is a `gap` reached after a genuine census, and the result is `specified`, ungrounded, candidate. The strongest single figure-grounded point is the split: a non-shrinking four-way disagreement is several well-formed questions, not one hard one.

- **cost if wrong:** bounded and locatable. The packet states its mode, three refuters and its provenance before any conclusion, so an error is findable rather than invisible. The real cost is spending the independence of outside readers by sending a single-cause narrative before a residual is described, which Fig 2 :42 prices: "Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong." That cost is avoidable by leading with the union claim and its refuters.
- **undoable:** yes. The dominant-factor claim was never asserted, so no primitive or verdict has to be withdrawn to revise the answer. A checker who fires one of the refuters in `08_expert_verification_packet.md` replaces the union claim with a narrower single-cause claim without disturbing per-domain anchors. The shape claim can be superseded by a corrected packet, and no term in the figure record was deleted to get there.
- **reversed by:** a ground reading, not agreement. A partition test showing that each crisis falls under exactly one condition and that removing the dominant one drives the residual to near zero on held-out episodes would fire the Fig 9 verdict. An aggregate statistic retaining held-out predictive power with no live slot left would fire Fig 1 :22. A model truncated at first-order contact losing no predictive power would fire Fig 11 :52. Two genuinely low-overlap anchors converging on one factor, with routes recorded, would begin to name a share. Until one of these fires, the verdict stands as `candidate`.
