## Two findings from the wider literature that bear directly on this question

1. **The distinction already has a name and an apparatus.** Celen and Kariv (2004) show that cascades *defined in terms of unobservable beliefs* are harder to identify than herds *defined in terms of observable actions*, and that belief elicitation plus controlled private signals in the laboratory makes the distinction possible. This is exactly the figure verdict: the separator is the source record, not the act. It also fixes the novelty claim. Any outreach must cite this paper before claiming to distinguish the two.
2. **The distinction may not be clean even with the apparatus.** The social-psychology source records the caveat that informational and normative conformity may not be fully differentiable, and that public compliance can become private acceptance through dissonance. This is a real conflict for any sharp two-type ontology. Per the derive-first rule, the conflict sends us back to the census, not to a softer reading: the two hypotheses are kept as distinct slots even where a person can move between them, because the source at the moment of the act is still what the instrument must read. The animal-behaviour review adds a second caveat: inconsistent terminology across fields, which is a habitat problem, not a figure conflict.
