## 1. The residual, in effect terms

The question asks why one and the same network behaves two different ways under two operations. State the residual as an effect, not a cause:

- **What failed to resolve:** a single number called "robustness" does not predict both behaviours. Remove nodes uniformly at random and the network keeps carrying traffic; remove a small fraction chosen by degree and it fragments. A measure that averages over nodes or over removal modes loses the signal (Fig 1 :22).
- **What an answer would look like:** not a scalar but a shape plus two regime-specific verdicts. The shape is the arrangement of heterogeneity across object slots; the verdicts are separate because the operations are not the same operation (Fig 9 :57).
- **Binding the sought-for shape as an ordinary term** (Formula "Direction"): *answer = (per-slot degree arrangement) x (presence or absence of a ranking seat)*. This is written down here so it can accrue a residual and be refused, rather than remaining an invisible preference.

## 2. Domain census, before any figure line is chosen

### 2a. R by arity, symmetry, scale (Fig 9 :22)

- **Arity:** two conditions bind, random deletion and targeted deletion. Not one field with two readers (that would be a seam), but two operations on one object. So a **union compound**, not a single hard question.
- **Symmetry:** the two operations are not interchangeable. Uniform sampling is exchangeable across nodes; degree-ranked selection is ordered by a comparison. Swapping them changes the outcome, so they are not the same operand under a symmetric bond.
- **Scale:** one step of removal, then a cascade of disconnection (Fig 11 :52), then a regime shift at a threshold (`f_c`). The first-order effect is node loss; the residual that matters is second or third order, the loss of the largest component.

### 2b. One line per domain, with actors / currency / anchor

- **Domain 1, the graph itself.** Actors: nodes, edges, degree per slot, hubs. Currency: connectivity, largest component, path length. Anchor: the adjacency data and the measured degree distribution. This is a domain because the anchor is nameable.
- **Domain 2, the removal regimes.** Actors: a uniform random sampler; an informed agent that ranks by degree. Currency: fraction of nodes removed, `f`; critical fraction `f_c`. Anchor: the percolation experiment or simulation. Unmaintained versus maintained habitat (Fig 11 :56-58).
- **Domain 3, the measurement frame.** Actors: the analyst, the metric (largest component, diameter, mean isolated-cluster size). Currency: residual R between predicted and observed connectivity. Anchor: the ground, never agreement (Fig 2 :29).
- **No fourth domain yet.** An "attack strategy optimisation" domain would need its own anchor (a defender's loss function). None is named in the question, so it is not admitted. This is a boundary, not a gap.

### 2c. Partition or union

Union. Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."* Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."* Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*. The overlap is the network being removed; the compound is (arrangement, removal regime). It gets its own loop.

### 2d. Order by dependency (Fig 3)

1. Enumerate the habitat before ranking actors (Fig 11 :43).
2. Measure the per-slot degree arrangement before choosing a robustness metric (Fig 1 :19).
3. Separate the two removal regimes before comparing them (Fig 9 :54-58).

## 4. Findings

### F1 - The question is a union compound, not one property (decisive for the shape)

Fig 9 :54: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*
Fig 9 :55: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*
Fig 9 :57: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*

The two removal regimes are not two values of one variable. They are two different operations, and a single robustness score averages the asymmetry away (Fig 1 :22). The compound is (degree arrangement) x (removal operation). It has its own loop. Verdict: answer with two regime-specific verdicts, never an average.

**Refuter:** exhibit one metric, measured at the ground, whose value under random removal and under degree-ranked removal is the same number for every network in a test set, while both behaviours the question names are still present. Then the union reading is wrong.

**Status: triangulated.** Fig 9 (union/partition) and Fig 11 (two habitat modes, one with a seat) have low overlap: one is about search, the other about agency in a habitat.

### F2 - Robustness under random failure requires per-slot heterogeneity, not an aggregate

Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."*
Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*
Fig 1 :23: *"The ground enters as the only metric."*

A network whose slots are all alike has no live slot. Removing any one node then removes the same amount of path structure, so the diameter grows monotonically and the component eventually fragments at a well-defined threshold. A heavy-tailed degree distribution puts most connectivity in a few slots. Random sampling almost never selects those slots, so the network deflates one leaf at a time and the giant component persists (Albert, Jeong and Barabási 2000, read 2026-11-22: *"nodes with small connectivity will be selected with much higher probability, and the removal of these 'small' nodes does not alter the path structure of the remaining nodes"*). The mean degree is the aggregate that hides this (Fig 1 :22).

**Refuter:** a homogeneous (narrow or Poisson) degree network that shows the same late fragmentation threshold as a heavy-tailed network under uniform removal, at matched N and edge count, with the same metric. That would collapse the per-slot claim.

**Status: triangulated.** Fig 1 (per-slot residual in perception), Fig 12 :5 (*"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*) and Fig 13 :125 (*"Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member."*) agree on different quantities and share little subject matter.

### F3 - The attack has a seat; the random failure does not

Fig 11 :14-15: *"nothing selects; what survives is what the ground kept"*
Fig 11 :37: *"Where a seat exists, something is doing work, and it is exactly what the seat does: enumerate the habitat, rank by cascade, introduce actors, build instruments, read the ground."*
Fig 11 :58: *"Maintained. A seat exists and can act: choose the instruments, distil the impactful actors, introduce what was not there."*
Fig 11 :61: *"The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not."*

Random failure is the unmaintained mode: no selector, pure contact, what the ground kept. Targeted disruption is the maintained mode: an agent enumerates the habitat and ranks by degree, then removes in that order. This is not a difference of degree but a difference of kind (Fig 11 :55). Fig 12 :92: *"Symmetry is what decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."* Uniform sampling and degree-ranked selection are not related by a symmetric bond, so they are different terms, not two spellings of one operation. The Operators table defines comparison as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, which is what degree-ranked selection performs.

**Refuter:** show the same early collapse under a sampler that is exchangeable across nodes, at the same fraction removed and with the same network. Then the presence of a ranking seat does not explain the asymmetry.

**Status: triangulated.** Fig 11 (habitat modes) and Fig 12 (symmetry of bonds) are low-overlap figures. World corroboration is strong: Albert et al. write that *"An informed agent that attempts to deliberately damage a network... will not eliminate the nodes randomly, but will rather target the most connected nodes"* (read 2026-11-22).

### F4 - Robustness is a derived composition property, not a new primitive

Fig 13 :24: *"that entities relate, and influence travels"*
Fig 13 :26: *"it decides what the measurement would mean"*
Fig 13 :74: *"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."*
Fig 13 :125: *"Smoothness, symmetry, chaos and discreteness are each what a particular composition leaves behind, and none is a seventh member."*

Network is one of the six structures. Robustness and fragility are not members of the set; they are what a particular arrangement leaves behind when a removal operation acts on it. Asking "what makes a network robust" is therefore asking about an arrangement, not about an ingredient. Fig 13 :115: *"The set is closed. The arrangements are not. That gap is why import exists."*

**Refuter:** derive a seventh primitive called robustness from the other five, or show that the six-term set cannot compose a network at all. Either breaks this finding.

**Status: single-source.** Fig 13 and the `THE_FORMULA.md` substrate section share an author and an evidence pool, so this is one figure read with a companion document, not two independent witnesses.

### F5 - Hubs are neither good nor bad; valence is relative to the residual

Fig 11 :49: *"An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries."*
Fig 11 :50: *"The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."*

A hub is the node that preserves paths under random failure and the node that is removed first under attack. There is no standing list of "good nodes" that survives the change of regime (Fig 11 :51). So a recipe that says "protect the hubs" or "eliminate the hubs" is answering a different question in each case. Fig 11 :39: *"And capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."* Fig 11 :40: *"An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves."* Uniformly adding links does not change the contact with a degree-ranked attacker.

**Refuter:** exhibit a node property whose valence for connectivity is the same under both removal regimes, measured at the ground. Then Fig 11 :49 was aimed at another quantity here.

**Status: single-source for the figure line; corroborated in world sources.** Nuwagaba et al. 2017 report that removing generalists causes the most secondary extinctions, while specialists are more tolerable. That is a different habitat with the same valence flip.

### F6 - Same atoms, different bond: the isomer reading, after re-census

Fig 12 :117: *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
Fig 12 :103: *"Composition is recursive, and the level decides what is irreducible."*
Fig 12 :104: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it. So irreducible is a fact about a level and never about the world"*

A potential strain was checked: Fig 13 names network as a structure (a primitive), yet a network plainly has internal nodes and edges. Re-census resolves it with Fig 12 :103-104. Irreducible is a fact about a level. At the network level, nodes and edges are atoms; at the substrate level, the network is an atom. There is no contradiction, so no strain is recorded.

With that settled, two networks with the same N and the same link count (the same ingredient list) but different degree arrangement are isomers in the figure's sense. Albert et al. did exactly this comparison: they *"generated networks that have the same number of nodes and links such that P(k) follows a Poisson distribution for the exponential, and a power-law for the scale-free network"* and found opposite responses. The difference is invisible in `(N, L)` and decisive in behaviour.

**Refuter:** show that degree sequence alone, with arrangement varied freely, produces identical removal curves at the ground. Then the bond information adds nothing and Fig 12 :117 does not govern this domain.

**Status: triangulated.** Fig 12 (bond and level relativity) plus the measured comparison in Albert et al. 2000. Low overlap between a framework figure and a physics experiment.

### F7 - What the figures do not give: the number and the name

Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"*
Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."*
Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."*

The figures return a shape: per-slot heterogeneity, a few high-degree live slots, and two removal operations distinguished by the presence of a ranking seat. They do not give `f_c`, the exponent `gamma`, the inequality `gamma < 3`, or the word "scale-free". Those are imports from the world literature with a source gate and a shadow test (Fig 6 :23). The figures also cannot distinguish scale-free from other heavy-tailed arrangements on shape alone. That needs an instrument. This is a **gap on the numbers**, not on the shape, and it is recorded as such.

**Refuter of the gap:** produce a figure line that yields a numerical percolation threshold for degree-ranked removal. Until then, the numbers stay in the world frame and are cited with provenance, not presented as derived.

**Status: single-source for the limit; corroborated by the figures README** (*"a missing thing would have to look like (a shape, not a name)"*).

### F8 - The ground that would settle it

Fig 1 :23: *"The ground enters as the only metric."*
Fig 2 :42: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."*

The ground here is a percolation measurement: remove a fraction `f` of nodes uniformly and then in descending degree order on the same graph, and record the largest component and mean isolated-cluster size. Albert et al. report `f_c` near 0.18 for attack and an extremely delayed transition under random failure in their scale-free model; Callaway et al. give exact generating-function solutions for arbitrary degree distributions; Cohen et al. show that networks with `alpha <= 3`, resilient to random removal, are still sensitive to intentional attack. These are ground readings from the world frame, not derivations from the figures. Their agreement with one another is partially a shared evidence pool (each cites the 2000 papers), so it is one witness chain, not three independent witnesses.

## 5. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per Seat Map :287: *"a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."*

The shape claim is refuted if any of the following holds at the ground:

1. A homogeneous degree network shows the same late fragmentation threshold as a heavy-tailed one under uniform removal, at matched N and edge count (breaks F2).
2. A heavy-tailed network does **not** collapse early when nodes are removed in descending degree order, while a homogeneous one does (breaks F3).
3. The asymmetry persists under a sampler that is exchangeable across nodes with a different random seed (breaks the seat distinction in F3).
4. Degree sequence alone, with wiring varied, fully determines the removal curves (breaks the bond claim in F6).
5. A seventh primitive "robustness" is derivable from the other five, or a direct state-state or structure-structure composition is admitted in this domain (breaks F4).

If any fires, the shape is withdrawn or re-censused. Until one is run, no part of this is `grounded`.

## 8. Round-trip gap `R_T`

The Symbols table defines `R_T` as the gap between what was sent up and what came back down, `gap(x, (T_E ∘ T_A)(x))`. Fig 4 :30 requires it to be measured rather than assumed.

1. **Sent up (x):** the verbatim figure set, chiefly Fig 1 :19 and :22 (per-slot residual), Fig 9 :54-58 (union compound), Fig 11 :37 and :58 (seat), Fig 12 :5 and :117 (arrangement), Fig 13 :74 (cross-kind composition).
2. **Abstraction (T_A):** the shorthand used in `07_the_tether_answer.md`, namely "heterogeneity plus a ranking seat, two separate verdicts, numbers owed to the ground".
3. **Concretisation (T_E):** the concrete world claim, namely power-law degree distributions with `gamma < 3`, `f_c` near 0.18 under attack and an extremely delayed transition under random failure.

**`R_T` is nonzero.** What the shorthand holds but the concrete claim does not carry cleanly:

- the specific exponent inequality `gamma < 3` is not in the figure set;
- the numerical `f_c` values are measurements, not compositions;
- the word "scale-free" imports a physics frame that the figures do not name;
- the possibility of other heavy-tailed arrangements with the same shape is dropped by the concrete claim.

What survives the trip cleanly: heterogeneity across slots, a ranking seat, and the requirement of two separate verdicts. `R_T` is recorded qualitatively as this enumerated set, not numerically, because no measurement was taken in this run. Treating the formula as identical to the figures would set `R_T` to zero and would be the dead-reckoning error Fig 4 :30 names.
