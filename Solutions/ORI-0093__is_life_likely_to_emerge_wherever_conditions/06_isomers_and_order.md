## DERIVATION

### Q1: Are the two options isomers?

- Fig 12 :117 *"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
- Option A uses the atoms of `C` only. Option B uses the atoms of `C` plus an imported atom `X`. Their atom sets differ, so under Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**. The error of calling them isomers would erase the very difference the question asks about.
- The weaker, checkable isomer claim is internal to each option. Two proposed prebiotic pathways that use the same molecular set but different bond orders, for example lipids before polymers versus polymers before lipids, are isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. Fig 12 :96 *"Two entries can share every ingredient and be inverses of one another."*
- A third case is the functional group. Fig 12 :123 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Two pathways that both require a gradient but differ in the molecular qualifier are members of one functional group, not isomers of each other.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if a proposed rare chain can be rewritten using only the atoms of the closure with no remainder, the import claim fails and the two options collapse into the isomer case. If two pathways said to be isomers are found to require different atoms, the isomer claim fails and they are different functional groups.

### Q2: What order is the chain?

- Operators table :30 defines sequence as *"A then B; B needs A to have happened"* (Fig 12 :71). Operators table :36 defines production as *"A produces B; B did not exist before"* (Fig 12 :77).
- An origin chain is not a sequence of pre-existing states. The living state is absent until the transition fires. That is production, so the chain is written with `⇒`, not `→`.
- Fig 12 :48 *"A chain is the degenerate case: one bond type, no branching."* A list of conditions written with `→` is the degenerate case and throws away the bond information the question needs.
- Fig 12 :111 *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."* This is the precise defect of a condition checklist: it names a state.
- **Falsifier:** a demonstration that the transition is a reordering of already present states, with the living state already latent and merely revealed, which would make `→` correct and `⇒` wrong.

### Q3: Convergence is not identity

- Fig 13 :121 *"Different routes can arrive at the same substance."* Fig 8 :50 *"Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence."*
- Convergent evolution (Wikipedia, read this session) documents independent arrival at analogous structures: *"Convergent evolution is the independent evolution of similar features in species of different lineages."* This makes convergent arrival a real mechanism at D2.
- But Fig 12 :117 says the same atoms under a different bond are a different substance, and :96 says entries sharing every ingredient can be inverses. So two routes reaching something we call life may have reached different substances that our instrument cannot tell apart. *The Hard Problem is About Source* gives the reason: a reading that cannot discriminate sources cannot name the source. Its words: *"If several paths create the same behavior, then behavior is a projection with lossy information."*
- **Conclusion:** convergence at D2 is genuine, but it does not license the inference *convergent evolution exists, therefore abiogenesis is likely*. That inference jumps the D1/D2 boundary that Fig 11 :36 establishes.
- **Falsifier:** an instrument that discriminates the products of two independent routes and shows them to be the same substance under the same bond. That would upgrade convergence from a shape to a measurement.

### Q4: The structures a flat list cannot write

- Fig 12 :119 *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."* Mineral surfaces and autocatalytic sets are catalysts in this sense.
- Fig 12 :121 *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* Self-replication is a cycle; a checklist is a chain and cannot close.
- Fig 12 :123 *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome."* A proton gradient across a membrane is a functional group whose presence fixes what the system can do.
- Fig 12 :5 *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."* So a claim that conditions are suitable records ingredients and not the arrangement.
- **Falsifier:** a flat ingredient list that predicts the origin rate as well as a bonded arrangement, which would mean the bond carries no information here.

## CONCLUSION

The two headline options are not isomers because their atom sets differ by the imported atom. The isomer claim survives only inside one option, over a shared molecular set with different bond orders. The origin chain is production (`⇒`), not sequence (`→`), because the product does not exist before it fires. Convergence is real at D2 and is not evidence about D1. Catalysts, cycles and functional groups are the structures a condition checklist cannot write, and they are where the bond information lives.
