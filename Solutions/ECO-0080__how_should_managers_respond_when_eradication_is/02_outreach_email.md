**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover note attached to a submission through a public venue in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until the shape claim and the refuters in `01_figure_grounded_audit.md` survive a specialist's first read. The output must survive validation or the two-stage offer collapses on contact.

**Subject:** One checkable claim about suppression side effects, before any theory

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output about invasive-species management I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The residual (`specified`, not `grounded`).** A manager has an established non-native actor that cannot be excluded. Repeated suppression is reducing the target, but it is also changing non-target species and relations. The figure this comes from treats a managed ecosystem as a maintained habitat. Its rule for the maintained mode is that the seat's power is asymmetric: introduction is available, exclusion is not, and a managed ecosystem is "permanent work rather than a solved state." The side effects are explained in the same figure by a first-order habitat scope: "Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out." So the residual I am testing is not "how do we kill the invader better" but "which slots are degraded, and how far from the target do we have to enumerate before the cascade stops mattering?"
>
> **The shape claim.** Four things follow, and none of them names a species or a place:
> 1. The response is not a solved state. It is a maintained contact pattern with monitoring, because subtraction of the habitat is unavailable.
> 2. The manager must enumerate the habitat outward through actors, conditions and relations, per slot, before choosing a lever. One averaged impact score hides the live slot.
> 3. The goal has the form of a threshold comparison, not a maximum-effort rule. A plan is a sequence, a goal is a comparison, and progress is a subtraction. The statement "eradication is the goal" is refused, not subtracted.
> 4. Containment is substitution of one habitat for another. It inherits actors you did not choose and has two failure modes that stay invisible until the goal fails or the introduced actor acts.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** The framework does not name which control agent to use, what density counts as unacceptable, or which substitute habitat to choose. It returns a shape, not a name. Those specifics would have to come from your data, with provenance and a check that the imported actor explains this gap. I am not claiming that biological control is automatically safe: an actor's valence is relative to the residual, and cane toads and kudzu are the same move with the cascade unenumerated.
>
> **What would refute it.** I wrote these down before sending this:
> 1. Show a manager in the maintained mode subtracting the habitat or excluding the established actor with the same authority the figure grants to introduction, or a solved state after which the work stops.
> 2. Show the side effects are strictly first-order and fully captured by one global impact score with no live slot residual.
> 3. Show a single lever that clears both "cannot eradicate" and "suppression has side effects" with one instrument and no intersection residue.
> 4. Show a containment action that substitutes no habitat, inherits no unchosen actors and has no failure mode invisible until the goal fails.
> 5. Name an actor whose valence is fixed across a change of residual, or import one with no provenance and no shadow test and show it still closes the residual.
> 6. State a management goal with no threshold comparison that still gives the plan a success criterion.
>
> If any one holds, I retract the corresponding claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed. I have a stake in the outcome, so I am declaring it: I want the shape to survive. I have no interest in which reading you return.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** the sources are below. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figures with line numbers (local framework extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`, read as `Fig 11 :52` and `Fig 11 :61-63`.
> - Functional eradication as a framework for invasive species control, Green and Grosholz 2021: https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/fee.2277
> - Evaluating unintended consequences of intentional species introductions and eradications, Pearson, Clark and Hahn 2022: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9291768/
> - Organisms as ecosystem engineers, Jones, Lawton and Shachak 1994 (author interview, 2018): https://reflectionsonpaperspast.wordpress.com/2018/05/04/revisiting-jones-et-al-1994
>
> **If you want to use the machinery:** the formula is in the figures link. Run it on a habitat you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it. The framework's own rule is that a loop cannot check the direction of its own question, and the author's essay warns that outputs can be simulated rather than derived. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
