# Isomers, thresholds and order: supporting derivation for Earth-likeness

**Mode: `specified` on the structural claims, `general` on the union split.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 9).

## RESIDUAL

The question bundles a count and a feature discrimination under one "or". Three questions are owed: (1) what is the structure of the predicate "Earth-like"; (2) under what relation do Earth and its rocky peers stand to one another; (3) what is the order of the comparison, given that the count and the feature ranking have different instruments.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the structure of the predicate "Earth-like"?

Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."

"Earth-like" is written in the literature as a set of thresholds: radius between 1 and 1.75 or 2 Earth radii, insolation between 0.25 and 4 Earth flux, sometimes a mass range, sometimes a feature list. The ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not. Fig 12 :94: "The arrangement is the substance."

The empirical consequence is recorded in Petigura et al. 2013 Table 1: the same objects give 5.8, 8.6, 22, 26 or about 50 percent depending only on which habitable-zone boundary is adopted. That is a flat-formula failure: the predicate is a bundle of thresholds, and the count is a function of where the thresholds are drawn (Fig 12 :6, the flattening collapse).

**Result:** the predicate must be bound with its thresholds declared before any count. Mode `general`. Triangulated (Fig 12 :5 plus the measured spread of the astronomy source).

**Falsifier:** a single predicate that yields the same count across all published habitable-zone definitions and instrument revisions. If it appears, the bundle collapses into a partition and this finding is withdrawn.

### Q2: In what relation do Earth and its rocky peers stand?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Earth, Venus and Mars share the atom set above. Their differences are in the arrangement: distance from the star, volatile inventory, presence of a large Moon, presence of plate tectonics. Over that shared set they are figure-isomers. The honest structural form of "Earth is unusual" is a different arrangement, not a different substance.

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

The weaker form is that Earth belongs to a functional group (rocky, temperate, volatile-bearing). The group fixes the kind; the qualifier (Moon, tectonics, oxygen) picks the member. This is checkable without claiming uniqueness.

Two further structures from Fig 12 are relevant:

- Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use." Plate tectonics and the carbon-silicate thermostat are persistent parts that survive their own operation.
- Fig 12 :121: "cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." The thermostat is a negative-feedback cycle; a chain of causes cannot close by construction.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if a feature invoked to make Earth unusual introduces an atom absent from the comparison class, it is a different composition rather than a different bond. The isomer claim is withdrawn, not stretched. A large Moon may be exactly such a case; `01` F6 records it as a live strain.

### Q3: What is the order of the comparison?

Fig 12 :86: "a threshold relation between two quantities." The Operators table :51, in agreement: "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one."

"Common or unusual" is a ⋛ comparison against a reference distribution. Two conditions must be met before it can fire:

1. a reference class must be declared (otherwise the comparison is frame-internal, Fig 1 :27);
2. the operands must be quantities at the level on which the comparison is made (the Operators table restricts ⋛ to two quantities).

Dependency order (Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."): declare the predicate first, then count, then rank features, then compare. A count made before the predicate is declared is a reading of nothing, because the input never arrived (Fig 3 :22: "A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can.").

### Q4: Why the two halves cannot be averaged

Fig 1 :19: "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated." Fig 1 :22: "Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."

The occurrence rate is an aggregate over planets; the feature ranking is a per-object discrimination. Averaging them would discard the slot index that says which planets and which features carry the signal. They are separate slots with separate anchors (a sample counter for the count, a feature-effect measurement for the ranking). No combined "Earth-likeness score" is licensed by the figures.

### Q5: Where the count enters the loop

Fig 13 :134: "Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence." The count is a ground quantity and a `gap` in the figure library. It enters as an import with provenance: the radius and insolation box, the stellar sample, the completeness correction, the confidence interval, and the date. Fig 8 :50: "Two frames can hold the same atom by convergence, reaching it separately, which is corroboration at maximum independence." Convergence across instruments would be real corroboration; agreement among reviews of the same Kepler pool is not.

## CONCLUSION

The predicate "Earth-like" is a flat formula whose thresholds decide the count, so it must be bound with those thresholds declared. Earth and its rocky peers are isomers over a shared atom set, or members of a functional group when only some atoms are shared. "Common or unusual" is a ⋛ comparison that requires a declared reference class. The comparison is ordered after predicate declaration and counting, and the two halves cannot be averaged. What survives is a checkable structure with a stated falsifier; what does not survive is any single combined answer to the original "or" question.
