## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What is the structure of a lever?

- Fig 13 :22: gradient is **"a difference that can be spent"**. Fig 13 :23-24: network is the structure **"that entities relate, and influence travels"**. Fig 13 :74: **"Everything else is a composition across the two kinds, never within one."**
- A lever is therefore a structure bonded to states. In control-language: pinning a node, changing an edge weight, or rewiring a link is a structural intervention; its effect is a directed difference (gradient) that propagates through relations (network). This is why Fig 6 :14 calls import **"the only operator that moves the wall"** when the lever is not held internally.
- Fig 13 :75: **"Two states never bond directly. It is not space-time but space, a structure, time; and two structures join only through a state."** A control action that is described purely as "state A becomes state B" is not yet a figure-licensed lever. It must state the structure that carries the influence.
- **Falsifier, written before this run:** a ground-settled direct state-state control action that cannot be re-described as a cross-kind composition breaks this reading. The network-science analogue is an intervention whose entire effect is a target state change with no detectable structural mediator.

### Q2 - Are two intervention strategies isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :123: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**
- Case 1, weak form. Strategy X pins node set {A, B}; strategy Y pins {B, C}. They share only B. By Fig 12's definition they are **not isomers**; they are two members of a functional group ("intervene on a small node set") with different atoms in the slots. This is the relationship between most published driver-node sets.
- Case 2, strong form. If both strategies are written over the full shared set of acted-on nodes and edges, differing only in which structure mediates which state pair (node control vs edge control vs structural control), then they **are** isomers: same atoms, different arrangement, different outcome. The world literature names exactly this distinction: node control, edge control and structural control are three ways of arranging the same network (Coraggio, Salzano and di Bernardo 2025, opened this session).
- The difference is invisible in a flattened list of "which nodes were controlled" and decisive in behaviour, which is Fig 12 :117 verbatim. That is the checkable content of the claim.
- **Falsifier, written before this run:** if two intervention sets with identical acted-on atoms and identical bond and order produce identical ground residual trajectories across all slots and times, the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands instead.

### Q3 - What order do the interventions have?

- Fig 12 :70: `→` is sequence, **"A then B; B needs A to have happened"**. Operators :34: **"the only bond a flat chain can express, so every term today is a sequence whether or not it should be"**. A plan is a sequence (Fig 12 :112).
- Fig 12 :76: `⇒` is production, **"A produces B; B did not exist before"**. Pinning a node that creates a new propagating mode is production, not mere sequence.
- Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."** Closed-loop control is a cycle. A one-shot open-loop plan cannot close by construction, which is why control under disturbance needs feedback.
- Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."** The control chain is measure, objective, lever, carry, not importance-ranked.
- Fig 11 :61: **"The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not."** The order of a lever is an introduction into a habitat; it cannot be a subtraction of the habitat. A "remove the confounding edge" strategy is only expressible as the introduction of a different arrangement, which changes what the controller is answerable for.

### Q4 - Four structures recur in the control setting

- **Isomer** (Fig 12 :117): same acted-on atoms, different bond, different behaviour. The strong form of Q2.
- **Catalyst** (Fig 12 :119): **"A part that survives its own use."** An instrument or a maintained channel survives the interventions it enables. A controller that consumes its own measurement capability is a spent part, and Fig 12 warns that nothing records which a term is unless the lineage does.
- **Cycle** (Fig 12 :121): the output re-enters as input. Feedback, not a plan.
- **Functional group** (Fig 12 :123): "intervene on a small node set," whose qualifier picks which set. This is the weak form of Q2 and the honest default when the full shared atom set is not established.

### Q5 - What is the order of the union C1 to C4?

- C1 (anchor) is upstream of C2 (lever): a lever is only a lever relative to a target that the ground fixes. Fig 3 :9-10 requires the objective to be stateable in the system's own terms before planning.
- C2 and C3 (channel) are mutually dependent: an unobserved lever is not a lever, and an unanchored channel is drift (Fig 10 :24, Fig 2 :35).
- C4 (import) is not a fifth condition but the route to C2 when the lever is absent from `Γ` (Fig 6 :23).
- Fig 9 :55: **"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."** The order is a dependency order, not an importance order, and the union must be unbundled before searching again (Fig 9 :54).

## CONCLUSION

A control lever is a cross-kind composition: a spendable difference carried by a network, not a bare state-to-state wish. Two intervention strategies over the same acted-on atoms but with different bonds or order are **isomers** in the strong form, and the world literature's node, edge and structural control is the natural instance; as usually reported with different node sets they are only members of a **functional group**, and the honest default is the weaker claim. Interventions are ordered by dependency, and closed-loop control is a **cycle**, which a chain cannot express. The union C1 to C4 is ordered by dependency, not averaged. This rescues the checkable structural content of the question without naming a specific algorithm (Fig 9 :32).
