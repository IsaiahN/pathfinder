## Wider literature that bears on the question

1. **Physical ceilings are real but loose.** Markov's review is the most directly useful source for an outside checker: it separates manufacturing, energy, space, design effort, and algorithmic limits, and says that several are substantially loose. This matches the figure's separation of budget limits from barriers, but it is a corroboration from engineering, not the verdict.
2. **The energy price has a measured floor.** Landauer's bound, and its experimental confirmation by Bérut et al., give a real price for one logically irreversible bit operation. Wolpert's FQxI interview makes the crucial further point: real systems are many orders of magnitude above that floor, so the practical constraint is not the theoretical bound. This is the same shape as the figure's "a wrong atom costs more than starting from primitives" but in different units.
3. **Capability is not contained in the agent.** Clark and Chalmers argue for *"the active role of the environment in driving cognitive processes."* This is a low-overlap corroboration of Fig 11 :39-40 from human cognition, and it does not share the framework's author or evidence pool.
4. **More search is not always the remedy.** The Levinthal case (as reported by Clark 2008) shows that an unbiased random search over conformations would exceed the age of the universe, and that real proteins do not fold that way. No-free-lunch gives the mathematical version. Both corroborate Fig 8 :32 in fields far from the framework, but the figure is still primary.
5. **Limits policy is an active field.** The RAND working paper treats fundamental limits as things that can be mapped in advance. It is a useful venue signal that this shape is checkable by people who price limits for a living.

No world source conflicts with the figure verdict. If one did, the conflict would send us back to the domain census, never flip the verdict. The sources here are not pooled: a physical ceiling, a cognition result, and a search theorem are different witnesses, though all remain secondary to the figures.
