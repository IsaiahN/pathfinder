### Q3 - Symmetric correlation versus ordered comparison

A correlation between a candidate pattern and an outcome is symmetric: swapping operands leaves it unchanged. The Operators table :15 gives the test for `+` as *"swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No."* But "stable" is an ordered threshold claim: Operators :49 defines `⋛` as *"a threshold relation between two quantities"* with the test *"is it about which is larger, rather than both being present?"* `Fig 2 :39`: *"Correlation has valence."*

### Q4 - Are a stable pattern and a coincidence isomers?

`Fig 12 :117`: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."* `Fig 12 :94`: *"The arrangement is the substance."* `Fig 12 :5`: *"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."*

- As **ingredients**, a stable pattern and a one-occasion fit can share the same observed atoms: the same observations, the same candidate structure. The ingredient list does not decide which is which. That is exactly the flat-formula collapse `Fig 12 :14-16` names: *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not"*.
- As **terms**, they are not isomers in the strict figure sense. Isomers are two settled substances with the same atoms and different bonds (`Fig 12 :117`). A coincidence is typically not settled: `Fig 5 :44` says a term *"explains one occasion perfectly"* fails the bargain, and `Fig 5 :69-70` separates settlement from usability. An unsettled one-occasion fit has no standing as a substance to be an isomer of.
- What is left is the stronger, checkable form: over a fixed shared atom set, **two stable regularities** that differ only in which state their structure binds to are genuinely different terms. `Fig 13 :76`: *"A gradient over space and one over time are different terms, because which state it is bonded to is part of what it is."* That is a real isomer-style claim: same six atoms, different arrangement, different behaviour.
- A coincidence that is instead the refusal of a held claim uses ¬, which is not a bond: Operators :73-74: *"− is A with B removed or absent, which is the absence of an operand. Negation is the refusal of a claim."* `Fig 5 :56`: *"Same mint operator, same three guards, same bargain. Only the direction of the residual differs."*

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either a stable pattern can be written without an atom the fitted one requires, or a one-occasion fit can be settled and composed over exactly as a stable pattern can, the isomer-style claim fails and only the weaker functional-group reading stands (`Fig 12 :124`: *"A functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."*).

### Q5 - What order is the union?

The two conditions (settlement, contact-through-change) are a union, not a partition (`Fig 9 :54`: *"And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."*). A single lever clears a region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersection standing: `Fig 9 :55-56` says a single lever *"leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."*

`Fig 12 :104`: *"A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it."* Once a regularity is settled it can serve as an atom at the next level, so "keep tracking this regularity" is a second-order composition over a first-order settled term. It needs no new primitive and no import that moves the wall. What remains open is the operand type of the comparison at that level: the Operators table restricts `⋛` to two quantities, and whether settled molecules-now-atoms count as quantities needs an instrument, not an argument. This is the live strain S4 of `01`, named here rather than hidden.

## CONCLUSION

The dependency chain fixes the order: perception, then a vocabulary for regularity, then the objective that counts a shrinking residual as progress, then sampling, then settlement and carry. The stable-versus-coincidence decision is an ordered comparison, not a symmetric correlation. Over a fixed atom set, a stable pattern and a one-occasion fit are **not** strict isomers, because the latter is not settled; two stable regularities differing only in which state the structure binds to **are** different terms under `Fig 13 :76`. A coincidence that refuses a held claim is a negation, not a missing operand. The isomer-style claim has a written falsifier; the operand type of the second-order comparison is a live type question.

This framing keeps what is defensible without forcing a number: the order, the comparison type and the isomer test need no new primitive and no invented threshold. `Fig 9 :32`: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."*
