# The Tether's answer: what makes an institution legitimate despite disagreement?

**Mode: `specified` on the shape and per-domain verdicts, `general` on the structural claim, `gap` on the empirical mechanism. Not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures (`05_what_shape_means.md` §8), not a ground reading.

## 1. The residual it answers

A person opposes a particular decision yet continues to treat the institution as having standing to decide. The question is a union compound (Fig 9 :54-55): disagreement and standing overlap in the case of interest. Unbundled, the decision slot carries the person's residual; the standing slot carries legitimacy; provenance records how a ruling was reached; community agreement is a valence check, never a source.

## 2. The shape

1. **Standing is an anchor, and the anchor does not update on decision content.** Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* Fig 2 :33: *"The anchor must not update."* So legitimacy is not a running average of decision satisfaction. Disagreement registers locally; standing stays pinned.
2. **The ground for this question is human judgment.** Fig 2 :36: *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."* Fig 2 :37: *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people."* The question is about people, so neither the institution's own record nor a theorist's definition is the ground.
3. **Legitimacy is a convention, not a verdict.** Fig 2 :41: *"agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it"*; Fig 2 :43: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"*. Agreement about legitimacy is legitimate in its own lane. Using it to settle a contested outcome is the category error.
4. **Standing survives the outcome it was earned on.** Fig 10 :61: *"And standing is a stake that survives the outcome it was earned on."* This is the figure's exact effect-term for "even when they disagree with its decisions."
5. **Legitimacy is not correctness.** Fig 10 :65: *"Provenance shows a ruling was reached legitimately. It never shows it was right: the ground does that"*. A procedurally clean decision can be wrong. This is why disagreement and legitimacy are compatible.
6. **The institution cannot ground its own legitimacy, and agreement cannot manufacture it.** Fig 10 :93: *"A ground with a reputation to protect is a ground someone benefits from, and its keeper is thereby disqualified."* Fig 1 :27: *"A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."* Fig 7 :35: *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict."* The residual must be read per slot (Fig 1 :19), so an overall approval figure hides the live signal.
7. **The empirical mechanism is a `gap`.** No figure line after a genuine re-census selects among fair procedure, diffuse support, normative obligation and content-independent reasons. The figures give the shape these mechanisms must have; they do not name one.

## 3. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that any particular institution is legitimate. The framework returns a shape, not a name.
- Not that legitimacy equals public approval, satisfaction, or trust in a poll. Those are frame-internal or aggregate readings.
- Not that a legitimate decision is a correct decision. Provenance is not a verdict.
- Not that fair procedure is *the* mechanism. That is an import from a world frame with a source gate, and its studies share evidence pools.
- Not that the disagreement is unreal or merely rhetorical. It is a live residual at its own slot.
- Not `grounded`. No measurement in this folder settles the claim.

## 4. What would settle it - the ground

A source-discriminating instrument, not a further argument. *The Hard Problem is About Source*: an instrument that reads green *"cannot comment on the source"*; the spectrometer resolved that case. Here the corresponding tests are:

- **Anchor test:** hold procedure and provenance constant and vary push on a single decision. If standing rises when the institution becomes more responsive to that push (refuter 1), the anchor claim fails.
- **Survival test:** track regard for standing against decision outcomes over time. If it tracks each outcome with no lag and no procedure signal (refuter 2), "survives the outcome" fails.
- **Convention test:** present a contested outcome decided by an institution whose legitimacy is asserted. If the convention is still treated as a convention (refuter 3), F3 fails.
- **Self-scoring test:** compare an institution's self-accrued credibility against an independent, non-pooled ground (refuter 4). If they track, F5 fails.
- **Mechanism test:** find an instrument that discriminates among procedural, normative and content-independent accounts on one reading. That would close F9 and name the mechanism.

## 5. The honest stop

**Reached on shape, unreached on mechanism.** Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence."* The answer stops at the limit of what the figures can distinguish, and records the lineage so the stop is checkable. *"Indiscernibility is not identity."* The institution's habitat matters too: *How a Society of Agents Would Work* gives *"capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone."* Legitimacy is likewise a property of institution and habitat, not of the institution alone.

## 6. One-paragraph version for outreach

> An institution is regarded as legitimate not because people like each of its decisions, but because it occupies an anchor slot for the question of who has standing to decide. An anchor is whatever settles a question without changing its answer because of what anyone thinks, so a disliked decision registers as disagreement at the decision slot while standing stays pinned. Legitimacy is a shared convention, and agreement about a shared meaning is the product; the moment the convention is used to decide a contested outcome it stops being a convention and becomes an unchecked verdict. Standing is a stake that survives the outcome it was earned on, and provenance shows a ruling was reached legitimately, never that it was right. That is why a person can disagree with a decision and still regard the institution as legitimate. The institution cannot ground its own legitimacy, and a public that agrees from one evidence pool adds no anchor. What the figures do not supply is the empirical mechanism: that is left as an open gap with four written refuters and an instrument to be built, not a name to be guessed.
