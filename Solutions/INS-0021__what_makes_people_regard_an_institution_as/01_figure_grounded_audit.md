## 0. The claim under audit

The question as an effect, before any theory is chosen:

> A person receives a decision from an institution. The person predicts something else, so the decision registers as a positive residual for that person. Yet the person does not withdraw the institution's standing to decide. What makes the standing persist across a decision the person opposes?

Two options are conceivable, and they lead to different places:

- **Option A:** standing is recomputed from how much people like each decision. A disliked decision withdraws legitimacy. This makes legitimacy a running average of decision satisfaction.
- **Option B:** standing is anchored to something that does not change with a particular decision's content, so disagreement is registered at the decision slot while the standing slot stays pinned. This is the figure-derived shape.

What an answer would look like, bound as an ordinary term before the run: *an answer names the slot that holds the standing, states what that slot ranges against, and states what would move it. It does not name a particular institution, a particular procedure, or a particular empirical mechanism.* This is the "Direction" discipline of `THE_FORMULA.md`: write down what an answer would look like and let it accrue a residual like anything else.

## 1. Domain census (before choosing any figure line)

**R, by arity, symmetry and scale.** The residual is the gap between *"this decision is wrong for me"* and *"I still accept that this institution has standing to decide."* Arity: two conditions, not one. Symmetry: it is not symmetric, because the person's disagreement and the institution's standing live at different slots and one does not cancel the other. Scale: first order at the person's decision, second order at the institution's continuing standing. Fig 9 :54-55: *"a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either"*. This is a **union compound** with its own loop, not one hard question. `06_isomers_and_order.md` unbundles it.

| domain | actors | currency | anchor | question type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D1, the person's decision frame** | a person with belief `b`, weighting `w`, action `a`, observation `o′` | prediction residual `R = |Γ(b,a) − o′|` per slot | the person's own ground, their experience of outcomes | partition: which decision slot is live |
| **D2, the institution's standing** | the institution, its procedure, its record, its seat | standing and provenance | whatever settles "who has standing" and does not move when a frame pushes | anchor / convention question |
| **D3, the community agreement frame** | other people, observers, reviewers | agreement versus independence | the ground, never agreement | valence question: agreement about meaning versus agreement about the world |

**No nameable anchor means it is not a domain yet.** D1 and D2 have nameable anchors. D3's anchor is the same ground as D1 and D2, not the crowd, so D3 is a real domain only as a *valence* check on agreement, not as a source of standing. If a proposed fourth domain ("public opinion" as the source of legitimacy) is offered, it fails the anchor test: opinion moves when frames push on it, so it is a frame, not an anchor (Fig 2 :29-30).

**Partition or union.** The compound is a union: disagreement and standing overlap in one case. Unbundle before searching (Fig 9 :54). The standing mechanism governs; the person's disagreement is the residual that tests it; the community agreement is the valence check.

**Dependency order (Fig 3).** (1) A disagreement must register per slot before anything can be explained (D1). (2) The institution's standing must be anchored before agreement can be read (D2). (3) Community agreement is then read with its valence (D3). Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."* More agreement (D3) cannot rescue an unanchored institution (D2).

## 3. Findings

### F1 - Standing is an anchor, and the anchor does not update on a decision (decisive, triangulated)

Fig 2 :29: *"An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks."* Fig 2 :29-30: *"If it moves when you push on it, it is not an anchor."* Fig 2 :33: *"The anchor must not update."*

The question "does this institution have standing to decide" is settled by something that does not change because a person dislikes a decision. If the institution's standing is recomputed from each decision's popularity, it becomes a frame negotiating outcomes, not an anchor. Fig 2 :45: *"Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it."* Fig 2 :46: *"The anchor is not a third party to the disagreement and it is not the tie-breaker in a vote."*

**Refuter:** a controlled case in which an institution that changes its decision in response to push on that decision *gains* regarded standing, with procedure and provenance held constant. If that holds, the anchor reading fails.

**Mode:** `specified`. No measurement of this effect was taken here.

### F2 - The anchor is legitimate only when the question is constitutively about it (specified, single-source in the figures, triangulated with SEP)

Fig 2 :36: *"An anchor is legitimate when the question is constitutively about it."* Fig 2 :37: *"Human judgment is the right anchor for is this a good answer for a person, because the question is about people."* Fig 2 :37-38: *"instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure."*

This question is constitutively about people, so the ground for "do people regard it as legitimate" is human judgment, not an institution's own record and not a theorist's definition. An institution is only an *instrument* when the question is about something else, and instruments can be wrong on axes they do not measure. This is the figure line that decides who may check the claim.

**Refuter:** a demonstration that the question is constitutively about something other than people, for example a purely procedural question about internal consistency, which would move the ground off human judgment.

### F3 - Legitimacy is a convention, and agreement about it has valence (decisive, triangulated)

Fig 2 :40: *"agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool"*. Fig 2 :41: *"agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption of it"*. Fig 2 :43: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"*. Fig 10 :47: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one"*. Fig 10 :48: *"a convention nothing can check is a constant the seat authored, carrying the seat's authority"*.

Legitimacy is shared meaning, so agreement about it is productive, not corrupting. But the moment the convention is used to settle a contested outcome, *"it is not a convention but a verdict"* (Seat Map :105), and it carries an authored authority that no one can check. The inference *"it is legitimate, therefore this decision is correct"* is the named failure, not an implication.

**Refuter:** a figure re-census that admits a convention deciding an outcome while remaining a convention. Until that, the valence rule stands.

### F4 - Legitimacy is standing that survives the outcome it was earned on (decisive, triangulated)

Fig 10 :61: *"And standing is a stake that survives the outcome it was earned on."* Fig 10 :60: *"Where a stake cannot be removed, declare it. An undeclared interest is modelled anyway and an unnamed model runs unchallenged."* Fig 10 :59: *"an interest in the outcome is unavoidable, an interest in the reading is disqualifying"*.

This is the effect term for "even when they disagree with its decisions": the standing was earned on some earlier ground and persists across a particular outcome. It also gives the honesty rule. An institution has an unavoidable interest in outcomes; what is disqualifying is an interest in the *reading* of its own legitimacy, and where that cannot be removed it must be declared.

**Refuter:** a case where regard for standing tracks each decision's outcome with no lag and no procedure signal at all, which would break "survives the outcome."

### F5 - The institution cannot be the ground for its own legitimacy (specified, triangulated)

Fig 10 :93: *"A ground with a reputation to protect is a ground someone benefits from, and its keeper is thereby disqualified."* Fig 1 :27: *"A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."* Fig 1 :26: *"predicates minted, and credibility accrued"* are named frame-internal quantities.

An institution that measures its own legitimacy by its own accrued credibility is doing exactly the disqualified scoring. This is the structural reason the check must come from a different closure (Fig 6 :23: *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."*).

**Refuter:** a figure line that permits a seat to ground a verdict it benefits from, or a measured case where self-scored credibility tracks an independent ground with no shared evidence pool.

### F6 - The residual must be read per slot, not averaged (general, triangulated)

Fig 1 :19: *"The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."* Fig 1 :22: *"Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average."*

An aggregate approval statistic is the wrong instrument for this question. It can be near zero while one group's slot carries a live residual. A legitimacy study that reports only an overall mean has thrown away the only signal that says where to look.

**Refuter:** a demonstration that the effect is genuinely additive across people so that a global residual is sufficient. The figures assert the opposite, so this would require a re-census of the slot rule.

### F7 - Agreement cannot manufacture standing (general, triangulated)

Fig 7 :12-13: *"same closure, handed along; agreement compounds, evidence does not"*. Fig 7 :35: *"A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes."* Fig 13 :38-39: *"independence is how little two frames draw on the same evidence, and it is what makes their agreement mean anything."* Fig 2 :42: *"Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong."*

A larger public agreeing from one evidence pool is one frame wearing many names. It raises the vocabulary and does not add an anchor (Seat Map :283). This is why `03_public_contact_points.csv` contains institutional opt-in venues rather than a list of likely agreeers.

**Refuter:** a case where adding agreeers who share a source changes the ground's reading of standing. Since agreement is not the ground, this would need a figure re-census.

### F8 - Legitimacy is not correctness, and provenance is not a verdict (specified, triangulated)

Fig 10 :62: *"A ruling carries provenance like an atom."* Fig 10 :65: *"Provenance shows a ruling was reached legitimately. It never shows it was right: the ground does that"*. Fig 10 :66: *"a wrong ruling stays wrong however clean its record"*.

This gives the cleanest separation in the answer: a procedurally legitimate decision can be wrong, and a procedurally clean record does not make it right. Disagreement with a decision is therefore compatible with regard for the institution, because the two claims are about different things. This is triangulated by SEP *Authority* in the world sources, which states that authoritative directives are *"content-independent reasons"* grounded in the source rather than the directive's content.

**Refuter:** a figure line or a measured case in which a clean provenance record changes the ground's verdict on the decision's correctness. None was found.

### F9 - The empirical mechanism is not derivable from the figures (gap, honestly recorded)

The figures give the shape of standing: pinned, surviving outcomes, conventional, not self-scored. They do **not** say which mechanism in a person produces that standing. Candidates in the world literature include fair procedure, diffuse support, normative obligation, and content-independent reasons. Fig 8 :39: *"So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."* Fig 9 :32: *"you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself."*

After a genuine re-census of D1, D2 and D3, no figure line governs the choice among those mechanisms. Verdict: `gap`, not a silence and not a forced fit. Fig 13 :134: *"Unreachable is a verdict here, not a silence."*

**Refuter:** a figure re-census that derives one of those mechanisms from the existing lines without an import. If that succeeds, this gap closes and F9 is withdrawn.

## 4. Per-domain verdicts, kept separate and never averaged

- **D1 person's decision frame:** `triangulated` on the per-slot rule (Fig 1 :19 and Fig 9 :54, low overlap between the slot census and the union/partition rule). Disagreement is real and local; it is not a global failure.
- **D2 institution's standing:** `triangulated` on anchor plus standing (Fig 2 :29-33 and Fig 10 :61, low overlap between "does not update" and "survives the outcome"). This is the governing domain.
- **D3 community agreement:** `triangulated` on the valence of agreement (Fig 2 :40-41 and Fig 7 :35, plus Fig 13 :38-39). Agreement is a convention product, never a verdict witness.
- **Mechanism selection:** `gap`, `single-source` at best in the world literature, where several studies draw on the same survey programs and share an evidence pool.

No verdict is averaged across domains. A high D3 agreement score cannot raise a D2 standing verdict.

## 5. Pre-registered refuter

Written before the conclusion, per Seat Map :287: *"a falsifier written before the run is a design instrument, not only an honesty device."*

The shape claim is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. An institution that changes its decision in response to push on that decision **gains** regarded standing, with procedure and provenance held constant (breaks Fig 2 :30, :33, F1).
2. Regard for standing tracks each decision's outcome with no lag and no procedure signal (breaks Fig 10 :61, F4).
3. A convention that decides a contested outcome remains a convention and is checked as such (breaks Fig 2 :43, Fig 10 :47, F3).
4. An institution's self-accrued credibility tracks an independent ground with no shared evidence pool (breaks Fig 1 :27, F5).
5. A figure re-census derives one of the competing empirical mechanisms from the existing lines without an import (closes F9 and changes the verdict).

If any fires, the shape claim is withdrawn or revised in the log, not silently reinterpreted. Until one is run, no part of this may be labelled `grounded`.

## 8. Seven-question recursive derivation (summary)

1. **Is the question one question?** No. Union of disagreement and standing (Fig 9 :54). Unbundle; the standing mechanism governs.
2. **What is the standing slot?** An anchor that does not update on decision content (Fig 2 :29-33).
3. **What is the ground for this question?** Human judgment, because the question is about people (Fig 2 :36-37). Not the institution's record.
4. **Is agreement evidence?** Only for shared meaning, never for the world (Fig 2 :40-41). A room adds no anchor (Fig 7 :35).
5. **Can an institution self-certify?** No (Fig 10 :93, Fig 1 :27). The check must come from a different closure (Fig 6 :23).
6. **Is correctness in the answer?** No. Provenance shows a ruling was reached legitimately, not that it was right (Fig 10 :65-66). This is why disagreement and legitimacy are compatible.
7. **Is the mechanism available?** No. `gap` after re-census (F9). Import from the world literature is permitted only with a source gate and shadow test, and world sources here share evidence pools.

## 9. Updated verdict

The shape survives: standing is an anchor that does not update on a decision's content, it survives the outcome it was earned on, legitimacy is a convention rather than a verdict, and the ground for this question is human judgment because the question is about people. The specific empirical mechanism does **not** survive as a figure derivation; it is a `gap` recorded plainly. Strains after re-census: **none hidden**, two dissolved by re-census (§7), one live but named (F9, the unreached mechanism). The whole remains `specified`, ungrounded, and simulated.
