# Outreach draft: check the shape before you decide about the theory

**Use:** one-to-one, or as a cover letter attached to a submission through the public venues in `03_public_contact_points.csv`. **Not** for bulk send.

**Hard precondition:** do not send until you have recorded your own verdict on stage 1. If you show the theory first, the reply is no longer a check.

---

**Subject:** Check one claim about institutional legitimacy before you decide whether the framework behind it is worth reading

> Hi [name],
>
> I am writing about one narrow output, not about a theory. I am not asking you to agree with it, and I am not asking you to trust me. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The residual, in effect terms.** A person receives a decision from an institution that they think is wrong. The decision registers as a disagreement. Yet the person still treats the institution as having standing to decide. What makes the standing persist across a decision the person opposes?
>
> **The shape claim (`specified`, not `grounded`).** The standing belongs to an anchor slot, not to a decision slot. An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks; if it moves when you push on it, it is not an anchor. So an institution's standing does not get recomputed from whether a particular decision was popular. Disagreement is real and local: it lives at the decision slot. The standing slot stays pinned. The claim is about structure, not about any named institution, and not about which psychological mechanism produces the effect.
>
> Two consequences follow, and they are the parts worth checking:
>
> 1. Legitimacy is a convention, a piece of shared meaning. Agreement about a shared meaning is the product, not a corruption. But a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being a convention. So "it is legitimate, therefore this decision is correct" is a category error, not an implication.
> 2. Provenance shows a ruling was reached legitimately; it never shows it was right. That is why a person can regard an institution as legitimate while judging a particular decision wrong. The two claims are about different things.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that fair procedures are the mechanism, that any particular institution is legitimate, that legitimacy is the same as public approval, or that this settles the question. An aggregate approval score can be near zero while one group's disagreement is still live; the residual is indexed per slot, not per average. The empirical mechanism is an open gap in this derivation, and I have recorded it as such.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim** (written before this message):
> 1. an institution that changes its decision in response to push on that decision gains regarded standing, with procedure and provenance held constant;
> 2. regard for standing tracks each decision's outcome with no lag and no procedure signal at all;
> 3. a convention that decides a contested outcome remains a convention and is checked as one;
> 4. an institution's self-accrued credibility tracks an independent ground with no shared evidence pool.
>
> If any one holds, I will retract the shape claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed. **What I would like from you first:** what would you count as a failure of this claim? Please answer that before reading anything I have written about the framework.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here are the sources. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them.
> - Verbatim figure text with line numbers (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/03-FIGURES_TEXT.md`
> - Canonical usage rule (local extract): `FRAMEWORK/reference/FIGURES_README_canonical.md`
> - The eight essays: https://adventuresinml.substack.com/p/arrival-of-the-fittest-the-tether
>
> **If you want to reuse the machinery:** the formula is public. Run it on a problem you know and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result *before* you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
