## Wider literature that bears on the residual (secondary; not turned into a verdict)

- Marcel Mauss, *The Gift* (1925), and the Malinowski debate over the Kula ring. The economic-anthropology page records that a "pure gift" ideology appears with developed market ideology. That is a direct case of an economic arrangement registering as an idea about obligation.
- Chris Gregory, *Gifts and Commodities* (1982), quoted on the same page: commodity exchange between people in "reciprocal independence" versus gift exchange in "reciprocal dependence". This is the ownership and obligation slots sharing atoms under different bonds.
- Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation*, via substantivism: exchange "embedded" in kinship, religion and politics. The habitat is not a separate "economic" sphere in every regime.
- E. P. Thompson (1971) and James C. Scott (1976), *The Moral Economy of the Peasant*: changes in provisioning register as changes in what people treat as fair obligation.
- Marvin Harris, *Cultural Materialism* (1979): infrastructure as the most significant force in cultural change, with structure and superstructure explicitly **not** treated as mere reflexes. That last qualification matters, because this solution unbundles the four slots rather than averaging them.
- Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson, *Not By Genes Alone* (2008), and Joseph Henrich, *The WEIRdest People in the World* (2020), via the cultural-evolution page: institutions and cultural inheritance shape psychology. The dual-inheritance feedback claim was sent back to the domain census, not used to flip the verdict.
- Edward Deci, Mark Lepper and colleagues on intrinsic motivation and reward contingencies: a low-overlap experimental literature supporting the claim that what crosses to the individual is a changed search objective, not a copied answer.
- Ronald Inglehart and the World Values Survey: cross-national value change measures, used here only as a public dataset in the same field, not as an anchor for the figures.

Overlap declaration: the five Wikipedia pages share one editorial host but draw on distinct primary literatures (Mauss and Parry; Thompson and Scott; Harris; Boyd, Richerson and Henrich; Deci and Lepper). They are secondary summaries, so their agreement with the figures is weak corroboration. The Cultural Evolution Society and World Values Survey pages are institutional sources with different evidence pools. No world source overruled a figure.
