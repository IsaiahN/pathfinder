## Question restated as an effect

The question offers two outcomes for one choice, team composition. Option A, a cross-functional team, is claimed to produce more innovation. Option B, the same team, is claimed to produce conflict and slow decisions. In effect we are asked whether the presence of multiple functions in one room increases the production of new settled terms and/or changes the cost of reaching a settling verdict. The two outcomes are in different currencies and are governed by different figure lines, so the honest first move is to split the question rather than average the outcomes.

## What is deliberately not here

- No personal emails of named individuals.
- No ranking of people by how likely they are to agree with the claim.
- No bulk-send plan.
- No claim that this simulated derivation settles the question it reports on. Every substantive file says it is a simulation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.

Three reasons in the figures' own words:

1. A room that reaches a conclusion without the ground has agreement, not a verdict. "A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes." (Fig 7 :35)
2. Selecting recipients for receptiveness manufactures the disqualifying correlation. "High correlation is disqualifying for a verdict and efficient for a convention" is carried in the figures' usage rule; the verbatim figure line is Fig 2 :40, "agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool".
3. Asking people to trust before they check spends their independence. "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it." (Fig 10 :42)
