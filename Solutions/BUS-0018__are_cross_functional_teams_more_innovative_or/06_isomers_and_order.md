# Union vs partition, two currencies, and the order of the loops

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

The question hands us one composition variable (cross-functional versus homogeneous) and two outcomes (innovation; conflict and slow decisions). We need to know (1) whether the two outcomes are isomers, functional-group relatives, or unrelated quantities; (2) what order the derivation must run in; and (3) whether the apparent trade-off is a law or a measurement gap.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: members as frames, their closures, residuals R, mints φ, the seven bonds plus negation, an external ground, and a budget. Bonds held: union (closures differ), identity (two names for one frame), sequence (→), comparison (⋛), negation (¬). No new primitive is available without import.

Order by dependency (Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."). Refuse before mint. Split before search. Describe the residual before picking a frame.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the two outcome loops isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour." Fig 12 :123: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

- The innovation loop and the decision loop are built from the **same atoms**: the same members, the same functions, the same closures. Their difference is **which bond is active**. In the innovation loop the active relation is the union of differing closures (Fig 8 :45: "Two frames together reach what neither reaches alone."). In the decision loop the active relation is the frame ranging against an anchor (Fig 2 :45: "Frames range against the ground. They do not negotiate with it.").
- So they are **isomers in the figure's sense**: same atoms, different bond, different substance. A flat description that lists only "team composition" cannot tell them apart, which is exactly the error the question makes by asking for one answer.
- They are also members of the same **functional group**, the `frame -> bond -> ground` molecule. The group says what kind (a frame composing over a ground); the qualifier says which member (union surplus versus anchored decision).
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if either outcome can be produced without one of the shared atoms (innovation with no differing closure; a settled verdict with no ground), the isomer claim fails and the two loops are unrelated quantities.

### Q2 — Is the trade-off a law or a measurement gap?

Fig 8 :47: "Independence is one quantity read twice." Fig 8 :48-49: "How much two frames draw on the same evidence decides both whether their agreement means anything and whether their union has any surplus. Two frames that overlap almost completely are one frame wearing two names."

- Low overlap is a **single structural parameter**. Read toward the union, it predicts surplus. Read toward the room, it predicts that agreement is costly but informative; high overlap predicts cheap but evidentiary-empty agreement (Fig 2 :40-41).
- Therefore the trade-off is **structural**, not an accidental empirical regularity that a different dataset might erase. It is `general` at the shape level. What remains `specified` and unmeasured is the magnitude and the net objective in a particular habitat.
- Fig 8 :32: "no search, however long, adds an atom." Adding more of the same frames cannot convert the trade-off into a free lunch.
- **Falsifier:** demonstrate a composition change that raises surplus and lowers settling cost simultaneously, with both measured against their own grounds. That breaks Fig 8 :47 as applied here.

### Q3 — What order do the loops run in?

- Fig 9 :4-5: "are not one hard question but several well-formed ones. Split it rather than search." Splitting is step one.
- Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit." Describing each residual is step two.
- Formula step 2: REFUTED is sorted by what is wrong, and "no arrangement of the other four reaches it". If a held belief about teams is false (for example "difference causes conflict"), it owes a refusal before any new team process is minted. Step three.
- Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." A team's first-order contribution must settle against the ground before it can be composed into a second-order claim such as "this team design is better overall". Step four.
- Fig 7 :35: "A room's agreement is variation, not a verdict. Selection between rooms is where the ground votes." Step five: select between differing rooms/designs against the residual, not by averaging within one room.

**Order result:** split, describe, refuse, settle, select. A management intervention that starts at "select" (pick a popular team design) without the earlier links is "a reading of nothing" (Fig 3 :22-23: "a reading taken below the break is not a weak reading. It is a reading of nothing.").

### Q4 — What do the two currencies price, and why can they not be summed?

Fig 12 :125: "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." Fig 12 :126-127: "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds. They are in different units, so a system holding one accounting cannot compare them and will not notice the shortfall."

- The innovation side is a bits quantity: the new arrangement must cost less to state than the confusion it removes (Fig 5 :43: "A new term is worth having when stating it, plus what remains unexplained after it, costs less than what was unexplained before.").
- The decision side is an actions quantity: rounds of checking, experiments, reviews, and the time until the ground pays.
- "Summing them into one figure is the error this names, not the fix for it" (Fig 12 :130). A single "team effectiveness" score is that error.
- **Falsifier:** provide an operational conversion from actions to bits with a declared code and a ground on which the conversion is settled. Without it, the two readings stay apart.

### Q5 — Valence, order of actors, and the habitat

Fig 11 :49: "An actor's valence is relative to the residual, not a property it carries." Fig 11 :50: "The same actor is a benefactor to one residual and a malfactor to another, so mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds."

- Functions cannot be ranked in the abstract. The legal reviewer is a benefactor to a compliance residual and a malfactor to a speed residual. The order of actors is therefore a **per-residual enumeration**, read off the world (Fig 11 :48: "You do not invent the list. You read it off the world, and what you cannot perceive or measure yet is the residual.").
- Fig 11 :39: "capability is a property of agent-and-habitat, never of the agent alone." Removing a function from a team is not removing a variable from a spreadsheet; it is substituting a habitat, with actors the team did not choose (Fig 11 :66: "Isolation is not removal of the habitat. It is substitution of one habitat for another.").
- **Falsifier:** identify a function with positive valence for every residual in the habitat, with a ground on which it never becomes a cost.

## CONCLUSION

The two outcomes are **isomers** of one `frame -> bond -> ground` molecule, same atoms and different active bonds, which is why a single answer to the original question cannot be faithful. Their apparent trade-off is **structural**, one independence parameter read twice, not an empirical accident. The required order is split, describe, refuse, settle, select. The two currencies (bits of new description, actions spent settling) must be reported separately. Which function helps is a per-residual enumeration, not a standing list. All of this is a shape and a route; the net outcome in any organization is a ground reading this derivation cannot perform.
