# Kinds of connectivity as bonds, isomers and a dependency order

**Mode: `general` on the structural distinctions, `specified` on the falsifiers.** This is a
simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see
`05_what_shape_means.md` section 7).

## RESIDUAL

The question asks for "kinds" of connectivity. That word needs a figure-derived meaning before any
landscape is examined. Three sub-questions are owed:

1. What makes two connectivity arrangements the same kind or different kinds?
2. Is the familiar contrast (continuous corridor versus stepping stones) an isomer difference or a
   functional-group difference?
3. In what order must the assessment run, and which link is most likely to be missing its
   instrument?

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - What makes two arrangements the same kind?

Fig 12 :117: *"Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the
formula and decisive in the behaviour."*
Fig 12 :94: *"The arrangement is the substance."*
Fig 12 :111: *"A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."*

A landscape's patch set is an ingredient list. Two landscapes with the same patches connected in
different ways are **isomers** under Fig 12's definition: same atoms, different bond, different
substance. The "kind" of connectivity is therefore the bond arrangement, not the patch list and not
the corridor label. This is why a list of corridor types cannot answer the question: it names
ingredients and leaves the substance unrecorded.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two landscapes with identical patch sets and different
  connection patterns show no difference in either the focal residual or any harm residual, the
  isomer distinction is behaviourally empty here and the word "kind" should be dropped.

### Q2 - Continuous corridor versus stepping stones: isomer or functional group?

Fig 12 :124: *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says
what kind; the qualifier says which member."*
Fig 13 :35-36: *"adjacency is not duration, an ordering is not a pathway, a pathway is not a
direction."*

- A **continuous corridor** and a **stepping-stone chain** share the functional group (a route that
  connects two habitat patches) but differ in the substructure: one is an uninterrupted strip, the
  other adds intermediate nodes and therefore adds atoms. By Fig 12 :124 they are *members of a
  functional group*, with the qualifier doing the differentiating work.
- They are **not isomers**, because they do not share the same atom set: stepping stones introduce
  extra nodes and edges. Calling them isomers would be a strain, and the strain test sends us back
  to the census rather than to a looser reading.
- The world literature uses exactly this vocabulary: the Wildlife corridor article (opened this
  session) distinguishes *"Continuous corridors are uninterrupted strips of habitat"* from
  *"'stepping stone' corridors consist of small, separate patches of suitable habitat"*. That is a
  world-side corroboration of the functional-group reading, not a figure derivation.
- **Falsifier:** if a study shows that a stepping-stone design and a continuous corridor over the
  identical patch set (no added nodes) produce identical residuals for all actors, the functional
  group qualifier carries no information and the distinction should be collapsed.

### Q3 - Does a single undirected connectivity metric flatten distinct bonds?

Fig 12 :111 and Fig 13 :36 apply together. A graph edge records adjacency; it does not record
whether the relation is a sequence (`→`, a migration route with a required order), a production
(`⇒`, a source population seeding a sink), a comparison (`⋛`, a threshold between quantities), or a
subtraction (`−`, a barrier removing an otherwise present route). Fig 12 :112: *"A plan is a
sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."*

A single connectivity number therefore names a state (adjacency) and cannot name the route to one.
This is the structural reason the world literature finds species- and process-specific effects:
Sullivan et al. 2011 found that corridors changed biotically dispersed parasites but not
abiotically dispersed ones, i.e. two different bonds over the same structure. The ASF studies
(Goicolea 2024, Faustini 2025) use the same connectivity structures to model a pathogen's route,
which is a different bond again.

- **Falsifier:** demonstrate that one undirected connectivity metric predicts the sign and magnitude
  of every actor's residual in every landscape, with no loss of bond information. Then the
  multi-bond requirement fails.

### Q4 - What is the dependency order, and where is the missing instrument?

Fig 3 :19: *"Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."*
Fig 3 :27: *"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."*

Order for an assessment (dependency, not importance):

1. **Perception:** enumerate the habitat in contact with the residual, then outward until the
   cascade stops mattering (Fig 11 :43-44). Instrument: field survey, telemetry, surveillance.
2. **Vocabulary:** name the bonds (adjacency, sequence, production, barrier) and the actors. No new
   primitive needed.
3. **Objective:** say what would count as progress *for which residual*. This is the link that
   usually breaks, because counting structures built is frame-internal (Fig 1 :27: *"They are
   frame-internal. A frame cannot score itself with a quantity it also produces."*).
4. **Planning:** choose an intervention, remembering that introduction is available and exclusion
   is not (Fig 11 :61).
5. **Learn and carry:** monitor against the ground and record the lineage.

Effort aimed at step 4 while step 3 has no instrument is the named failure: a corridor can be
built, counted and reported while neither D1 nor D2 was ever measured.

- **Falsifier:** exhibit a connectivity programme whose objective link has a working instrument
  (a measured residual that closes or fails to close) and where adding more corridor still fails.
  Then the missing-instrument diagnosis is wrong for that programme and the failure lies elsewhere.

## CONCLUSION

"Kinds of connectivity" has a clean structural reading. A kind is a **bond arrangement**, not an
ingredient list (Q1). Continuous versus stepping-stone corridors are **functional-group members**,
not isomers, because they add or remove atoms (Q2). A single undirected metric keeps the adjacency
and loses the route, which is why valence appears to vary across species and processes (Q3). The
assessment is a **dependency chain** whose usual break is the objective link, the one that says
which residual counts (Q4).

This rescues the legitimate part of the question without naming a universally safe design. It gives
a vocabulary in which an answer could be stated and refuted, and it keeps the `gap` for the named
safe kind intact.
