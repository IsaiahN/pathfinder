# Isomers, bonds and order in transition outcomes

**Mode: `specified`.** A simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two transitions can share the same formal ingredients (elections, a new constitution, a free press, vetting, trials) and still diverge: one produces accountability, the other reproduces the old power structure. Fig 12 :5: "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording." Three questions are owed:

1. Is the question a union or a partition?
2. Are the two outcomes isomers, or merely members of a functional group?
3. What does order do, and which actors are catalysts that survive their own use?

## DOMAIN CENSUS, RESTATED FOR THIS DERIVATION

Atoms held: oversight, elections, press, courts, trials, vetting, inherited officials, security services, patronage ties, external actors. Bonds held: the seven (three symmetric, four ordered) plus negation. No new primitive is available without import. Currency: bonds and order. Anchor: the ground settles each reform; a gate is not the ground. Question type: union (two overlapping conditions), then ordered composition inside the intersection.

## Q1: Union or partition?

Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."

Fig 9 :54: "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."

The two conditions here, accountability produced and old structure reproduced, overlap. A transition can have a genuinely independent court and a captured security service at once. That is not a harder case of either; by Fig 9 :57-58 it "stops being a mixture and becomes a specific compound." A single lever clears the region where one condition holds and leaves the intersection. The right move is to unbundle the union and give the intersection its own loop with its own ground.

**Consequence for the main audit:** verdicts are reported per domain and never averaged. Averaging would turn a compound into a score, which is exactly the aggregation Fig 1 :19 forbids.

## Q2: Are the two outcomes isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

Two readings:

- **As the field usually writes them**, the accountable case and the reproduced case do not share an atom set: one has an independent court and a reformed security service, the other has a captured court and an unreformed one. By Fig 12's definition they are not isomers; they are two members of the same **functional group**, the structure "oversight plus coercion plus contestation," with different atoms in the slots.
- **The stronger and more interesting version**: hold the atom set fixed, the same officials, agencies, laws and external actors, and vary only the bond. An oversight body that ranges against evidence and one that ranges against the wishes of the powerful share every ingredient and differ in the relation. That is a genuine isomer claim: same atoms, different bond, different substance, invisible in the ingredient list and decisive in behaviour.

This is the figure-grounded reason the question is hard. The ingredient list cannot tell you which case you are in. The difference is carried entirely by the bond.

**Falsifier, written before the run (F5 in `01`):** if two transitions with the same atom set but different bonds produce no reliable difference in outcome, the isomer claim fails and only the weaker functional-group claim survives. If they share no atom set even under the stronger reading, both claims need re-census.

## Q3: What does order do?

Fig 12 :72: "A then B; B needs A to have happened."

Fig 12 :77: "A produces B; B did not exist before."

Fig 12 :111: "A vocabulary without bonds can name a state and cannot name a route to one."

`→` is sequence: B needs A to have happened. `⇒` is production: B did not exist before A fired. These are ordered, so swapping the operands changes the substance. A reform list written with `+` (conjunction, symmetric) records that all items are present and records nothing about which must precede which. That is why "elections plus trials plus vetting" is not a transition plan; it is an ingredient list with the grammar removed.

Second-order composition is legal when a settled molecule becomes an atom (Fig 12 :104). A court that has settled against the ground can become an operand for the next level of accountability; a court that has not settled cannot be composed over. That is why a newly created court is a weak building block, and why the transitional-justice literature's finding about newly formed courts versus established independent judiciaries bears directly here (world source, secondary only).

**Falsifier:** show a sequence in which the order of two reforms makes no difference to whether the anchor updates. The bond claim would then be a notation preference, not a structural finding.

## Q4: Which actors are catalysts?

Fig 12 :119: "catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."

Inherited officials are the standing candidate. A reform commission that uses former regime personnel and regenerates them into the new order is a catalyst; the same personnel dismissed and replaced are spent. The notation usually fails to record which, so two transitions that look identical in their ingredient lists differ in a quantity nobody wrote down. This is a direct, checkable question for a checker: after the transition, did the inherited actors survive their own use?

**Falsifier:** if inherited officials behave identically across uses (no regeneration effect), the catalyst reading fails and should be withdrawn as a special case of the functional-group claim.

## Q5: Negation: the reform that refuses

Fig 5 :53 states a fifth kind of gap: "refuted: a term we hold is wrong; a term is owed that says so." A transition that only accumulates (new laws, new agencies, new commissions) and cannot refuse the old claim that the new order is already accountable is a vocabulary without negation. Fig 12's eight operators include negation as a unary operator, not a bond; it joins nothing, it refuses a claim. Formula step 2 requires refusal before mint. So "the old claim is false" is a legitimate first act of a transition, and a package that cannot say it can only accumulate.

**Falsifier:** a transition that explicitly refuses the old legitimacy claim and still produces no new accountable term. Then refusal is not the missing operator here and this finding is withdrawn.

## CONCLUSION

The two outcomes are best read as a **union compound** whose intersection is a specific case with its own loop. Within the intersection they are **isomers under a fixed atom set**, a functional group when the atom sets differ. Order matters because `→` and `⇒` are ordered bonds and a flat `+` list records none of it. Inherited officials are the standing catalyst candidate, and a transition that cannot refuse the old claim is a vocabulary that can only accumulate. Every one of these claims is `specified` and carries a falsifier. None names a country.
