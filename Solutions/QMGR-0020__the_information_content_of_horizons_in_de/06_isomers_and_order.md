# Horizon boundaries as arrangements: order, finiteness, and the black-hole/de Sitter isomer claim

**Mode: `specified`.** Simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The audit in `01` establishes a shape: finite, area-bounded, per patch. Two supporting questions are owed. (1) In what order do the figure lines apply, and which link in Fig 3's chain actually binds here? (2) Are the black-hole horizon and the de Sitter horizon the same kind of thing under Fig 12's definition of an isomer, or only members of a common functional group? ## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Which link in the dependency chain binds?

Fig 3 :20-21: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces."*

- Link 1, perception: the horizon is perceived as a boundary of what is contained. Present; the world source *Cosmological horizon* says points outside *"can never be observed"*.
- Link 2, vocabulary: we have a term for the relation, "contained" (Fig 13 :10), and a term for the operation, counting. Present.
- Link 3, objective: say what would count as progress. Here it is a finite count terminating in a bounded region. Present as a `specified` objective.
- Link 4, planning: a route to a count requires an instrument that can distinguish candidate microstates. **This is the break.** There is no such instrument for de Sitter horizons in the sources opened this session.
- Link 5, learn and carry: cannot be assessed from below the break. Fig 3 :22-23: a reading taken below the break is *"a reading of nothing."*

**Consequence:** optimising the area-law coefficient (a move above the break) cannot rescue the missing counting instrument. The gap is at link 4, not at the coefficient. This is the dependency order that gives F5 its status.

### Q2 - Why finiteness comes before the count

Fig 13 :68: **"finiteness in a finite region is what makes the counting terminate"**.

The order is load-bearing. The finite region does not produce a number by itself; it makes the *counting terminate*. So "finite" is a structural property of the region and "how many" is a separate, later question that needs link 4. Reading them in the reverse order, as if a number were already implied by the area, skips the break identified in Q1.

The world source *Bekenstein bound* states the same order: information *"must be finite if the region of space and the energy are finite"*. The finiteness is conditional on the region and the energy; the count is not asserted.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** exhibit a finite-region horizon whose count does not terminate, or for which "finite" is not a property of the region. If that holds, Fig 13 :68 governs another quantity and Q2 returns to census.

### Q3 - Are the black-hole and de Sitter horizons isomers?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

Write both horizons over the same held atom set:

- Black-hole horizon: a containment boundary with an **outside** observer. The region counted is inside; the counter stands outside.
- de Sitter horizon: a containment boundary with the observer **inside**. The region counted is the observer's patch; there is no outside counter.

Same atoms (space, a containment boundary, a finite region, an observer, energy), different arrangement of the observer relative to the boundary, different behaviour (the black-hole case has a partially settled microstate count for the supersymmetric case; the de Sitter case does not). Under Fig 12's definition that is a genuine **isomer** claim: same atoms, different bond, different substance.

If the comparison is weakened to "both are horizons bounding finite regions", only the **functional group** claim survives: *"the group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* The weaker claim is safer and is the one the audit actually relies on.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the observer's position relative to the boundary makes no difference to the counting (that is, an outside counter and an inside counter are the same operation), the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. If, conversely, the two horizons can be written with entirely different atom sets, even the functional-group claim fails.
- **Mode:** `specified`, unmeasured. This is a structural comparison, not a physics result.

### Q4 - What order is the union?

The union is a **compound**, not a list (Fig 9 :57-58). Two conditions bind: a finite boundary and an accessible count. A single lever that clears one condition leaves the intersection standing, because the second condition still binds. Fig 9 :55-56: *"A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones."*

The order of operations follows Q1 and Q2: settle the boundary first (D1), then the finiteness, then attempt the count (D2). Never average the two verdicts. D1 is `specified` and corroborated; D2 is a `gap` at the ground. Averaging them would manufacture a verdict neither domain supports.

## CONCLUSION

The dependency chain binds at link 4, the missing counting instrument, not at the area coefficient. Finiteness precedes the count because it is what makes counting terminate. The black-hole and de Sitter horizons are isomers under Fig 12 if the observer's position relative to the boundary is treated as the differing bond; the weaker and safer claim is that they share a functional group. Both claims carry falsifiers written above. What this framing rescues is the structural comparison without importing the black-hole anchor into the de S case. The gap at the ground in D2 remains open.
