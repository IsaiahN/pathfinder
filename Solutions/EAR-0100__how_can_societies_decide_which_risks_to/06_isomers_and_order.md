# Prevent, prepare, accept: structure, order and analogues

**Mode: `specified` on the structural derivation, `general` on the bond typing, not `grounded`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 8).

**RESIDUAL**

The audit left three structural questions open: (1) are the three actions different bonds or one bond with different parameters, (2) what is the order of the decision chain and where does it actually break, and (3) are two different societies' triage systems isomers over a shared atom set, or merely similar in outcome? Each is answered below with a figure line and a falsifier.

## Q1: Are prevent, prepare and accept different bonds?

Fig 12 :5 "A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."
Fig 12 :46 "A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."
Operators table :58 "a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"

Write each action as an atom pair joined by a typed relation:

| action | atoms joined | bond | figure warrant |
|---|---|---|---|
| prevent | current contact + introduced measure | change of contact, not literal erasure (introduction available, exclusion not, Fig 11 :61) | Fig 11 :40 "An improvement that does not change contact changes nothing, however much it improves." |
| prepare | current capability + head start or method | head start crossing the scale membrane | Fig 4 :24 "downward: something that makes the next search cheaper. Never a replay of what worked once." |
| accept | current response set + a refusal | negation of "a response is composed here", plus a recorded reason | Operators :58; Fig 8 :40-43 on unreachable at budget |

Three different relations, and the third is not even a bond but a unary operator (Fig 12 amendment: negation "joins nothing"). Under a symmetric-bond test (Operators :15 "swap the operands. Does the meaning change? No.") none of the three is symmetric: reversing the operands gives a different claim. So they are ordered, typed, and not interchangeable.

**Falsifier for Q1:** exhibit a single bond type that generates all three actions with only parameter changes, show that the negation reading of accept is unnecessary, or show that swapping operands leaves the meaning unchanged. Any of these collapses the three into one dial and the audit's F4 falls.

**Result:** the three actions are *not* settings of one dial. This is `triangulated` across Fig 12, Fig 4, Fig 11 and the Operators table, with the specific negation reading of accept `single-source` on the figure side.

## Q2: Is "which risk is worse" a comparison bond, and what does that rule out?

Fig 12 :87 "a threshold relation between two quantities"
Operators table :51 "a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one"
Fig 12 :112 "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."

A risk register ranks risks by a comparison (⋛) of two quantities, likelihood and consequence. That is legitimate for setting a goal ("reduce the worst quantile"), but Fig 12 :112 warns that a comparison is a *goal*, not a substance. It cannot itself tell you which action composes with which slot. Treating the comparison as the decision is the flat-formula error: it records the ingredients and not the arrangement (Fig 12 :14 "the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not").

**Falsifier for Q2:** show a ranking that determines the action without an objective link or an anchor for acceptability, or show that a comparison bond carries the arrangement information by itself.

**Result:** ranking is a filter for attention and budget, not a verdict generator (Fig 9 :60). Confirms F5.

## Q3: What is the dependency order, and where does the chain break?

Fig 3 :20-21 "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."
Fig 3 :25-26 "Perception has obvious failures and obvious tests. Planning has obvious failures and obvious tests. The link between them, having terms for what a situation offers and being able to say what would count as progress, has neither, so it attracts the least attention and the fewest instruments."
Fig 3 :27 "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."

The order for one risk slot is:

1. **perceive** (D1, D2): the hazard registers against an instrument, not only against a model's own output;
2. **vocabulary** (D2, D4): there are terms for what the situation affords, including who bears the loss;
3. **objective** (D4): a stated, measurable "what would count as progress here, for whom, by when";
4. **planning** (D3): allocate the finite budget across the prepared actions and signposts;
5. **learn and carry** (D5): verify, refuse where needed, and carry only methods upward.

The break in practice is between 2 and 4, at the objective. This is why more sophisticated ranking tools (step 4 machinery) often fail to improve outcomes: they land above the break. Lempert (2025) corroborates independently: RDM starts with a decision-framing exercise in which objectives and criteria are defined before any ranking, and "No universal criterion exists."

**Falsifier for Q3:** an instrument that measures the objective per slot in the field, or evidence that the objective link is already measured for the slots in question. If either holds, the claimed break location is wrong and F6 must be amended.

**Result:** `specified`, `triangulated` (Fig 3 plus decision-science corroboration).

## Q4: Are two societies' triage systems isomers over a shared atom set?

Fig 12 :117 "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
Fig 12 :123 "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
Fig 13 :121 "Different routes can arrive at the same substance."

Define the atom set at the level of *capabilities*, not named institutions: {perception instrument, forecast frame, allocation budget, acceptability verdict seat, verification seat}. Two societies with the same five atoms but different bonds (one lets the forecast frame decide acceptability; the other keeps a separate people-anchor seat) are **isomers** under Fig 12 :117. The ingredient lists match; the behaviour differs decisively. As the record puts it elsewhere, "the arrangement is the substance" (Fig 12 :94).

If the two societies do not share the atom set, for example one lacks an independent verification seat entirely, they are not isomers at all. They are a composition plus a missing atom, so an import is owed (Fig 6 :14 "the only operator that moves the wall") rather than a re-arrangement. This distinction matters: re-arranging a society's existing institutions cannot manufacture a missing verification seat.

**Falsifier for Q4:** show that two systems with identical capability atoms and different bonds behave identically on the same residual, or that a system can add a verification seat by pure re-arrangement without importing a new actor or instrument. Either kills the isomer claim.

**Result:** `specified`, supported by the figure definition, with the fixed-atom condition named as a live constraint. The fixed-atom condition is kept visible rather than hidden, because real societies often differ in their atom sets.

## Q5: What analogue does predation risk give, and where does it stop?

Lima and Dill (1990) report that animals facing uncertain predation use simple rules, including "assume attack is likely until experience allows for a more detailed assessment of risk." Haselton and Galperin (2011) show the general logic: when false-negative errors cost more than false positives, cognitive systems bias toward the cheaper error. The parallel to the prevent / prepare / accept split is that **the bias is set by the cost asymmetry of the errors, not by the probability estimate alone**. A society with irreversible losses should bias toward preparation even where the estimated probability is low, because the false negative (unprepared for a realised catastrophe) dominates.

Where the analogue stops: an animal's ground is survival and reproduction; a society's acceptability question is constitutively about people (Fig 2 :37). The animal-behaviour source can corroborate the *mechanism shape* of asymmetric-error triage; it cannot license the acceptance verdict. Fig 4 :20 requires the anchor to be renamed when the scale changes, so the corroboration is mechanism-only.

**Falsifier for Q5:** a case where a low-probability, irreversible risk is correctly handled by pure probability ranking with no asymmetry bias.

## Q6: Order of the union, and what must not be averaged

Fig 9 :54-57 gives the union rule. The five domains are not ordered by importance but by dependency: D1 and D2 supply perception and vocabulary; D4 supplies the objective; D3 plans; D5 verifies and carries. A verdict in D4 (this residual is acceptable to those who bear it) cannot be averaged with a verdict in D1 (the residual is physically small). They are different quantities with different anchors, and averaging them is the "global risk score" failure of F1.

Fig 12 :125-126 "A composition costs in two currencies, and they do not add." "Bits price the description. Actions price finding out whether it holds." A risk register (bits) and a preparedness program (actions) are in different units. A plan that proposes "one score covering both" is summing them into one figure, which Fig 12 :129 calls "the error this names, not the fix for it."

**Falsifier for Q6:** a demonstrated common unit in which description cost and action cost legitimately add, or an anchor that settles two domains at once.
