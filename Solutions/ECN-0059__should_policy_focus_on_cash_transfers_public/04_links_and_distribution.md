## What the wider literature bears on the derivation

1. **Cash transfers: real effects, but the open question is "for whom".** J-PAL states that questions remain about "how well they work, for whom, and under what conditions," which is the per-slot residual of F2. The Cash Plus model couples transfers with optional services, which is the union reading of F1 in practice. This corroborates the shape; it does not rank instruments.
2. **Wage-setting: a part of a package, not the main tool.** Both the Low Pay Commission and Gindling (IZA) independently reach the same structural conclusion in two different labour markets (UK and developing countries). Low overlap between those two sources (different institutions, different data, different regions), so their convergence is weak corroboration of F1 and F4, not a single pooled noise.
3. **Assets are a distinct condition, not a restatement of income.** ASPE's assets-for-development perspective says assets produce effects "income alone cannot." That supports treating asset ownership as a separate condition in the union rather than as a wrapper around cash.
4. **The ground is broader than the delivered input.** PNAS shows poverty reduces the value of consumption itself, so a transfer measured only by consumption level misses a real welfare effect. This corroborates F3 (anchor on the outcome, not the input) and warns that our outcome metric needs an instrument too.

No world source conflicts with the figure verdict. The conflict rule was not triggered. All these sources are in the anti-poverty policy field except PNAS (cognitive science), which is the low-overlap witness. Most share a policy-evaluation evidence pool, so agreement among them is one pool talking; the figures decide.
