# Four instruments: functional group, union, and order

**Mode: `specified`.** A simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The question lists four instruments as if they were four answers to one question. Two structural questions are owed: (1) are the four instruments members of one kind of thing, or instances of different kinds; and (2) in what order must they be considered so that the choice is made against a measured condition rather than a preference.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 — Are the four instruments a functional group or four unrelated kinds?

Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."

All four instruments share the same functional substructure: each is a way of changing a household's position against a material shortfall. The group says what kind (anti-poverty intervention); the qualifier says which member (transfer, service, wage floor, asset account). Under this reading they are not competing theories of poverty; they are four members of one group, and the question "which one?" is a question about which member fits which slot.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if one instrument can be shown to act on a different ground entirely (for example, a wage floor that changes only employer behaviour with no household material consequence), it is not a member of this functional group and the grouping fails. This is testable against a measured outcome.

### Q2 — Are they a union or a partition?

Fig 9 :55: "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Fig 9 :56: "A single lever clears the region where only one condition holds and leaves the intersections standing, because the second condition still binds."

The *instruments* are a functional group (Q1). The *conditions they address* are a union (Q2). These are different objects, so there is no strain between Fig 12 and Fig 9. A household short of both wages and assets is a compound, not a harder wage-only case. Re-census confirms: Fig 12 governs the structure of an answer term; Fig 9 governs the condition structure of the question.

### Q3 — Are they isomers?

Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."

As usually written, the four instruments do **not** share an atom set: a transfer names cash, a service names service access, a wage floor names a wage rate, an asset policy names a stock. By Fig 12's definition they are not isomers; they are functional-group members with different atoms in the slots.

The stronger form: if all four are written over the **full shared atom set** {household, cash flow, service access, wage rate, asset stock, material outcome}, differing only in which atom is bonded to the outcome and with which bond, then they are isomers in the figure's sense: same atoms, different arrangement, different substance. Whether this stronger form holds is an empirical question about whether each instrument can be written over the same atoms without a hidden extra primitive. Mark `specified`, unmeasured.

**Falsifier:** if an instrument requires an atom none of the others require (a new primitive), the isomer claim fails and the functional-group reading stands.

### Q4 — What order do they come in?

Fig 3 :20: "The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."

Order by dependency (perception, vocabulary, objective, planning), not by political salience:

1. **Perceive the slot.** Which household, which material shortfall? Fig 1 :19: "The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated."
2. **Bind the term.** Name the condition in effect terms; describe the effect rather than the cause (Fig 9 :30-31).
3. **State the objective.** What would count as progress for this household? This is the link with no instrument in many policy shops (Fig 3 :27: "A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at.").
4. **Choose the member.** Only now pick transfer, service, wage floor, asset account, or a combination. Fig 8 :53: "Residual first, frame second."
5. **Anchor and keep reversible.** Measure the material outcome and keep every cut ranked and reversible (Fig 9 :41: "so keep every cut ranked and reversible").

### Q5 — What composes across instruments?

Fig 12 :104: "A settled molecule becomes an atom for whatever composes over it." A result that is settled against the ground for one instrument (for example, a measured cash-transfer effect on a particular slot) becomes an atom that the combined analysis can compose over. Fig 8 :45: "Two frames together reach what neither reaches alone." The combination is a second-order composition over first-order settled results, not a new primitive and not an average of the four input metrics.

## CONCLUSION

The four instruments are **members of one functional group** (Fig 12 :124), addressing a **union of overlapping conditions** (Fig 9 :55-57). They are not isomers as usually written, though a stronger isomer claim over a shared atom set is checkable and carries a stated falsifier. The order is by dependency: describe the slot and the binding condition before selecting the member; anchor on the material outcome; keep cuts reversible. The figures license this structure. They do not license picking a winner, which remains a `gap` closed by measurement (Fig 9 :32, Fig 13 :134).
