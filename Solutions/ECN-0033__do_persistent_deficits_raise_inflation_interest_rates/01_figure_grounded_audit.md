## 0. The claim under audit, stated in effect terms

> Do persistent deficits raise inflation, interest rates, or both, or can their effects remain limited in some circumstances?

R is the gap between what a forecast built on the deficit alone predicts for two observed quantities and what the data show under different conditions. The two quantities are the inflation rate (an ordering of prices over time) and the interest rate (a directed intertemporal price). "Persistent" names a flow repeated over periods, which accumulates into a stock of debt. "In some circumstances" names conditions, so the residual is conditional by construction.

**What an answer would look like, bound as an ordinary term (Formula, "Direction"):** a list of regimes, each with a sign, a magnitude and a named instrument that could refuse it. Binding this before the run makes the sought-for shape refusable by the same test as every other term.

## 1. Domain census before choosing any figure line

**R by arity, symmetry and scale.** Arity: two effect slots (inflation, interest rates) plus a condition variable, so at least three, not two. Symmetry: the two effects are not interchangeable. Swapping the price level for the cost of capital changes the claim, so the bond between deficit and each effect is ordered (Fig 12 :94: `The arrangement is the substance.`). Scale: deficit is a flow, debt is a stock, inflation is a rate of change of a price level, interest rate is a price. These are different scales, and the flow-to-stock crossing is lossy.

**One line per domain.** A domain needs actors, a currency and a nameable anchor; no anchor means it is not a domain yet.

- **Domain 1, fiscal financing.** Actors: treasury, legislature, voters, bond issuers. Currency: the budget balance as flow, accumulated debt as stock. Anchor: the government budget constraint, an accounting identity that does not move because a model predicts otherwise. Partition or union: the financing instruments (tax, money, bonds) are alternatives inside this domain, so a partition of one financing choice.
- **Domain 2, monetary and price formation.** Actors: central bank, bond market, households, firms. Currency: money, price level, nominal yields. Anchor: measured inflation and measured yields. The inflation *target* is not the anchor: it is a convention that the central bank can revise. Union: monetary regime, expectations regime and fiscal dominance overlap, so this is a compound of conditions.
- **Domain 3, empirical measurement and verification.** Actors: datasets, econometricians, historical episodes. Currency: estimates, standard errors, residuals. Anchor: the observed data, but each instrument measures only some axes. Union: studies share methods and sometimes data, so independence must be bought, not assumed.
- **Not yet a domain:** "public opinion on deficits." There is no stable anchor I can name for it here, so it is left in the habitat rather than promoted to a domain (Fig 11 :48: `You do not invent the list. You read it off the world, and what you cannot perceive or measure yet is the residual.`).

**Order by dependency (Fig 3).** Perception of the deficit and the two effects first; then vocabulary for the conditions (accommodation, currency denomination, slack, expectations regime); then the objective (a per-condition verdict); then the plan (unbundle, then search each domain with low-overlap frames); then carrying the record. Fig 3 :19: `Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not.` An average taken before the conditions are named is effort above the break.

**Partition or union.** Union (Fig 9 :56: `A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones.`). "Both" and "limited in some circumstances" overlap with the single-effect cases, so a single lever cannot clear the whole region. The compound gets its own loop and its own ground.

## 3. Findings

### F1 : The question is a union compound, and "limited in some circumstances" is the split instruction (decisive for the shape)

Fig 9 :54: `And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again.` Fig 9 :56: `A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones.` Fig 9 :60: `Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict.`

The four offered answers are not mutually exclusive outcomes. Under wartime financing, under a non-accommodating central bank, under a currency sovereign issuing in its own currency, and under a currency union with foreign-currency debt, the same deficit flow produces different readings. The world agrees: Bordo and Levy (2020) find the deficit-inflation relation `generally holds in wartime` and was absent in the 2007-2008 crisis; Catão and Terrones (2005) find a strong association among high-inflation and developing groups `but not among low-inflation advanced economies`. So the question is not one hard coefficient. It is a union, and the conditions are the answer's skeleton. Verdict: `specified` from two low-overlap figure routes (Fig 9 union rule and Fig 1 per-slot rule) plus the two empirical frames. **Triangulated.**

### F2 : The two effects are different quantities, and averaging them destroys the only signal

Fig 1 :19: `The residual is indexed by object slot, not aggregated.` Fig 1 :22: `Aggregating across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average.` Fig 13 :20: `ordering, so more and less can be said` (spectrum). Fig 13 :22: `a difference that can be spent` (gradient).

Inflation is an ordering of prices over time; the interest rate is a directed price on intertemporal exchange. "Both" does not mean "the same cause at two strengths." It means two bonds from the same financing act to two different kinds of quantity. A single headline such as "deficits raise prices and rates" averages two slots. The Plante, Richter and Zubairy estimates (NBER Digest, 2025) make the difference concrete: a 1 percentage point rise in debt/GDP raises the five-year-ahead five-year Treasury rate by about 3 basis points, roughly three quarters of it a term premium, while their summary notes that the close relation between expected rates and expected inflation `broke down in the early 2000s`. Verdict: keep the verdicts per slot, never averaged. **Triangulated** (Fig 1 plus Fig 13, plus an empirical estimate with a different instrument).

### F3 : The anchor is the measured data, not the inflation target or the model

Fig 2 :29: `An anchor is whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks.` Fig 2 :33: `The anchor must not update.` Fig 2 :35: `Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you.` Fig 2 :37 (from the same passage) says instruments `can be wrong on axes they do not measure`.

This is the place where an economics answer most often fails. The central bank's 2 percent target is a *nominal anchor* in the central-bank literature, but it is revised by the institution that holds it, so by Fig 2 :33 it is a convention, not the ground. Kim and Ulate (FRBSF, 2026) measure a sensitivity gauge of perceived credibility and conclude recent expectations are `likely to be well anchored`; that gauge is an instrument for credibility, not a measurement of fiscal causation. The ground for this question is the observed inflation and yield data, which do not move because a model predicts a coefficient. Verdict: the target coordinates expectations (efficient for a convention) but cannot settle a verdict. **Triangulated** (Fig 2 plus the FRBSF instrument, different scales).

### F4 : A threshold is a filter for the budget, never a witness for the verdict

Fig 9 :60: `Use filters for the budget and witnesses for the verdict. Never let a filter hand you a verdict.` Operators table: `a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one`.

Reinhart and Rogoff (2010) report a debt/GDP threshold near 90 percent above which median growth falls by one percent. That is a comparison, a goal-shaped cut. It is useful for deciding where to spend the next hour of search. It is not a witness that any given deficit raised inflation or rates, and the same paper reports `no apparent contemporaneous link between inflation and public debt levels for the advanced countries as a group` while `inflation rises sharply as debt increases` in emerging markets. Reading the threshold as the verdict is the named error. Verdict: filter only. **Single-source for the figure rule, corroborated by the world source.**

### F5 : "Persistent" is a flow-to-stock crossing, and the round trip is lossy

Fig 4 :30: `So take the round trip and measure the gap. Without it you are navigating by dead reckoning: confident about a position nobody checked.` `THE_FORMULA.md` step 6 defines `R_T = gap( x , (T_E ∘ T_A)(x) )`.

A deficit is a per-period flow; persistent deficits accumulate into a debt stock. Markets price the stock and the expected path of future flows, not the annual flow alone. Compressing "persistent deficit" into "more debt" throws away maturity, denomination, who holds the debt and the monetary response; bringing it back down cannot recover them. The round-trip gap `R_T` is nonzero and must be recorded rather than assumed zero. Angeletos, Lian and Wolf (2024, revised 2026) make the point precisely: their deficit-inflation result holds `provided that two testable conditions are met`, namely high marginal propensities to consume and `that deficits are not accompanied by interest rate or tax hikes in the near term`. Verdict: `specified`, with `R_T` enumerated in `11_audit.md`. **Triangulated** (Fig 4 plus the model's stated conditions).

### F6 : Agreement among studies is one pool unless independence is bought

Fig 2 :18 (from the same figure) describes two frames that `never talk, and still agree` because `the agreement is the pool talking`. Fig 2 :40: `agreement about the world is worthless when the agreeing frames share an evidence pool.` Fig 2 :42: `Buy independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong.` Fig 8 :32: `Closure is idempotent. Composing inside a closed set never leaves it, so no search, however long, adds an atom.`

Six of the eight research sources are NBER items; all are economics. Their agreement is not eight witnesses. It is one discipline with partly separated instruments: a 107-country panel (Catão and Terrones), a two-century historical narrative (Bordo and Levy), a cross-country debt dataset (Reinhart and Rogoff), a portfolio-substitutability model (Friedman), a DSGE comparison (Angeletos et al.), a high-frequency Treasury estimate (Plante et al.) and a high-frequency expectations gauge (Kim and Ulate). Low overlap in *instruments and data*, real overlap in *field*. I declare both. Verdict: triangulated on the conditional shape, not on a coefficient. **Triangulated with declared overlap.**

### F7 : No figure line supplies a coefficient: this is a `gap` for magnitude, not a silence

Fig 8 :39: `So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence.` Fig 9 :32: `you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself`. The figures README says the framework returns `a shape, not a name`.

I re-censused after F1 through F6. No figure line governs the numerical size of a deficit's effect on inflation or yields. That is not a failure to be hidden: it is the framework's stated limit. The magnitudes (about 3 basis points per point of debt/GDP; a 90 percent threshold in one dataset) are ground readings from world sources, secondary corroboration only. Verdict: `gap` on magnitude. Shape from figures. **Single-source for the limit (README + Fig 9), not a gap on the shape.**

## 4. Pre-registered refuter (written before the conclusion)

The shape claim is refuted if any of the following holds:

1. A single mechanism is shown to map the deficit to inflation and to interest rates with the same sign and comparable magnitude in every regime, with no condition doing any work (breaks F1 and Fig 9 :54-56).
2. The anchor moves: a measured relationship changes systematically when the same data is re-analysed by a frame with a declared stake in the outcome (breaks Fig 2 :33).
3. Two or more genuinely low-overlap datasets, different countries and different instruments, converge on one unconditional effect with no regime interaction (breaks F6).
4. The condition variables (monetary accommodation, currency denomination, slack, expectations regime) are shown to be post-hoc labels with no out-of-sample predictive power (breaks F1 and F5).
5. The flow-to-stock crossing is shown to be lossless for the quantities at issue, so `R_T = 0` (breaks F5 and Fig 4 :30).

If any fires, the conditional shape is withdrawn. Until one is run, nothing here may be labelled `grounded`.

## 5. Recommendation

1. Lead with the union split and the refuter, not with a coefficient (F1, F8).
2. Report inflation and rates in separate rows and never average them (F2).
3. Name the measured data as the ground and the inflation target as a convention (F3).
4. Use thresholds only to decide where to search next (F4).
5. Record the flow-to-stock `R_T` alongside any deficit number (F5).
6. Declare field overlap before claiming triangulation (F6).
7. Say `gap` on magnitudes plainly (F7). Fig 4 :25: `A recording carried upward looks like knowledge and is a description of one occasion.` Send the generator, the split and the refuter, not a headline number.

The four offered answers are a union; the effects must be reported per slot and per regime; the ground is measured inflation and yields, never the target; thresholds are filters; and magnitudes are a `gap` that only a ground reading can close.
