## QUESTION

Decide, in effect terms, what the Tether says about the claim that persistent deficits raise inflation, interest rates, or both, or that their effects remain limited in some circumstances. Option A: read the four items as competing magnitudes and select one. Option B: read them as a union of overlapping conditions, split them, and report a per-regime verdict for each effect. What each leads to: A sends one headline coefficient that hides which slot is live and which condition binds; B sends a checkable table with a refuter per row, and stops at the limit of the available instruments.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

R by arity, symmetry and scale, then one line per domain giving actors / currency / anchor, partition or union, and order by dependency.

- **R (the residual).** The gap between forecasts built on the deficit alone and the observed inflation and yield readings under different conditions. Arity: two effect slots plus a condition variable, so at least three; not the two the question appears to offer. Symmetry: the two effects are not interchangeable, since an ordering of prices is not a directed price of borrowing; the bonds are ordered. Scale: deficit is a flow, debt is a stock, inflation is a rate of change, the interest rate is a price. Fig 9 names a union compound: a case in two conditions is not a harder case of either.
- **Domain 1, fiscal financing.** Actors: treasury, legislature, voters, issuers. Currency: budget balance as flow, debt as stock. Anchor: the government budget constraint, an accounting identity that does not vary with what a model thinks. Financing instruments are a partition inside this domain.
- **Domain 2, monetary and price formation.** Actors: central bank, bond market, households, firms. Currency: money, price level, nominal yields. Anchor: measured inflation and measured yields. The inflation target revises, so it is a convention, not an anchor. Monetary regime, expectations regime and fiscal dominance overlap, so this is a union of conditions.
- **Domain 3, interest-rate formation.** Actors: bond market, issuers, term-premium traders. Currency: yields, maturity, risk premia. Anchor: observed Treasury rates. Union: term premium, expected real rates and expected inflation all contribute to the same reading, so a source-discriminating instrument is owed.
- **Domain 4, measurement and verification.** Actors: datasets, econometricians, historical episodes. Currency: estimates, standard errors. Anchor: the data, with instrument limits. Union: studies share field, method and sometimes data, so independence must be bought.
- **Order by dependency.** Perceive the deficit and both effects first; build the condition vocabulary second; set the objective as a per-condition verdict third; unbundle and search each domain with low-overlap frames fourth; carry the record fifth. Refuse the single-coefficient shape before minting (step 2); describe the residual before choosing a frame (Fig 9).
- **Not yet a domain:** public opinion on deficits. No stable anchor was nameable from the residual, so it stays in the habitat.

Strain test: the Fisher relation seemed to bond two structures directly. Re-census resolves it as two derivatives joined through the state Time, which Fig 13 permits. See `06_isomers_and_order.md` Q3. One notation choice remains open (conjunction versus comparison in the Fisher identity) and is recorded, not hidden.

## WORLD (secondary sources read)

World sources corroborate only and never overrule. All eight are economics; overlap in field is declared even where instruments differ.

- **Macroeconomic history.** Michael D. Bordo and Mickey D. Levy, "Do Enlarged Fiscal Deficits Cause Inflation: The Historical Record," NBER Working Paper 28195, 2020 (published Economic Affairs 41(1), 2021): "the relationship between fiscal deficits and inflation generally holds in wartime" and the 2007-2008 crisis is a case where expansionary fiscal and monetary policy did not raise inflation. Predicts regime dependence; corroborates the union verdict. Overlap: historical narrative, distinct from the panel and the time-series sources.
- **Cross-country panel / monetary economics.** Luis A. V. Catão and Marco E. Terrones, "Fiscal deficits and inflation," Journal of Monetary Economics 52(3), 2005, pages 529 to 554: "Yet empirical research has had limited success in uncovering this relationship" and a strong association "among high-inflation and developing country groups, but not among low-inflation advanced economies." Predicts a conditional inflation effect; corroborates Fig 9. Overlap: 107-country panel, distinct from the two-century narrative.
- **Macroeconomic history / public debt.** Carmen M. Reinhart and Kenneth S. Rogoff, "Growth in a Time of Debt," NBER Working Paper 15639, 2010 (American Economic Review 100(2), 2010): "there is no apparent contemporaneous link between inflation and public debt levels for the advanced countries as a group" while "inflation rises sharply as debt increases" in emerging markets. Predicts the advanced/emerging split and supports treating the 90 percent cut as a filter; corroborates. Overlap: long-run cross-country data, partly shared with the historical narrative.
- **Portfolio theory / public finance.** Benjamin M. Friedman, "Crowding Out Or Crowding In?", NBER Working Paper 0284, 1978 (Brookings Papers on Economic Activity 3, 1978): "Debt-financed deficits need not \"crowd out\" any private investment, and may even \"crowd in\" some." Predicts that the financing instrument and asset substitutability decide the rate effect; corroborates the per-condition verdict. Overlap: theory, no dataset shared with the empirical sources.
- **Monetary theory / DSGE.** George-Marios Angeletos, Chen Lian and Christian K. Wolf, "Deficits and Inflation: HANK meets FTPL," NBER Working Paper 33102, 2024, revised 2026: "provided that two testable conditions are met" and "that deficits are not accompanied by interest rate or tax hikes in the near term." Predicts that the response depends on accompanying policy; corroborates the flow-to-stock loss and the union rule. Overlap: model comparison, shares literature but not data with the estimates.
- **Empirical asset pricing / interest rates.** Michael Plante, Alexander W. Richter and Sarah Zubairy, "Revisiting the Interest Rate Effects of Federal Debt," NBER Working Paper 34018, summarized in NBER Digest, 1 October 2025: "A 1 percentage point increase in the US debt-to-GDP ratio raises the five-year-ahead five-year Treasury rate by about 3 basis points." Predicts a bounded rate effect, mostly term premium; corroborates the separate-slot verdict and supplies a magnitude as a ground report. Overlap: US time series, distinct from cross-country panels.
- **Expectations / central bank credibility.** Taerin Kim and Mauricio Ulate, "Are Inflation Expectations Currently Well Anchored?", FRBSF Economic Letter 2026-32, 9 November 2026: "recent U.S. market-based inflation expectations are likely to be well anchored." Predicts that the nominal anchor is a credibility instrument, not a measurement of fiscal causation; corroborates Fig 2 :33. Overlap: high-frequency expectations data, distinct from the debt/rate estimates.
- **Institutional policy.** International Monetary Fund, "Inflation Peaking amid Low Growth," World Economic Outlook Update, 31 January 2023: "Target fiscal support at those most affected by elevated food and energy prices" and "Withdraw broad-based fiscal relief measures." Predicts a targeted rather than blanket fiscal stance; corroborates the conditional shape. Overlap: policy document drawing on the same research pool.

No world source conflicts with the figure verdict. The conflict rule was not triggered. Their agreement is one economics field with several instruments, not eight independent witnesses; that overlap is recorded in `10_source_ledger.csv`.

## VERDICT (from the figures)

The shape survives; a single coefficient does not. The question is a union of overlapping conditions, so it must be split. Inflation and interest rates are two quantities and must be reported in separate rows, never averaged. The measured data are the anchor; the inflation target revises and is a convention. A debt/GDP threshold is a filter for search budget, never a witness. "Persistent" names a lossy flow-to-stock crossing whose `R_T` must be recorded. Per regime, the world says the inflation link is strong in high-inflation and emerging-market episodes and weak or absent in low-inflation advanced economies, while the rate link is real but bounded and mostly a term premium. No general magnitude is derivable from the figures; that is a `gap`, not an absence. This is a `specified`, ungrounded result.

- **cost if wrong:** bounded and locatable if the per-condition table is kept. The real cost is spending the independence of outside readers by sending a headline coefficient instead of a refuter, which Fig 2 :42 prices as buying independence in proportion to the cost of being wrong; and hiding a live slot inside an average, which Fig 1 :22 names as the way a live signal disappears. Both are avoidable by leading with the split and the refuter.
- **undoable:** yes. The per-regime verdicts can be revised as new low-overlap data arrives without touching the shape. A coefficient can be added where the ground pays for it; no primitive has to be withdrawn.
- **reversed by:** a ground reading, not agreement. A low-overlap natural experiment under a fixed monetary regime; a yield decomposition that discriminates term premium from expected real rates and expected inflation; an out-of-sample failure of the condition variables; or a demonstration that the flow-to-stock crossing is lossless (`R_T = 0`) would reverse the verdict. Until one fires, it stands as `candidate`.
