# Isomers and order: species sets, interaction bonds, cascades

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` section 6).

## RESIDUAL

Two networks may contain the same species and the same count of interactions and still differ radically in which interactions are essential for a function. Two questions are owed: (1) are such networks **isomers** under Fig 12's definition, and (2) what **order** of effects and of investigation does the derivation require?

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms held: the species set plus conditions. Bonds held: the typed interaction relations (Fig 11 :47 *"relations: pollination, predation, competition, dependency"*; the seven bonds of Fig 12, including symmetric + and ordered → and −). New primitives are not available without import. Anchor: the intervention outcome on a declared function. Currency: per-slot residual.

## DERIVATION

### Q1: Same species, different interaction type: isomers or not?

Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
Fig 12 :116: **"Two entries with an identical ingredient set are not variants of one thing."**
Fig 12 :5: **"A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information, and it is the half nothing was recording."**

- Write network A as species set S with relation set R1 (mostly mutualistic), network B as the same S with R2 (mostly antagonistic), same number of links.
- Same atoms, different bonds. By Fig 12 :117 they are **isomers**, and the difference is *"decisive in the behaviour"*: the response to removal differs even though the ingredient lists match.
- If the species sets also differ, the weaker claim is a **functional group** (Fig 12 :123): *"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."* Both networks are "interaction networks"; the qualifier (which bond types) decides the member.
- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if two networks over the same species set with different typed interaction relations are behaviourally identical under every intervention on the declared function, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands.
- **Mode: `specified`.** Triangulated across Fig 12 :117 and Fig 11 :50 (*"mutualism and parasitism are relations rather than kinds"*), low overlap between a composition rule and a valence rule.

### Q2: What order does the bond carry?

Fig 12 :46: **"A bond is the typed relation between two operands. There are seven, they are not interchangeable, and which one holds is not recoverable from the operands."**
Operators table :59: **"Symmetry decides whether an arrangement is a new thing."**
Fig 12 :74 (the `→` test): **"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."**

- Mutualism and competition are symmetric readings of an interaction: swap the two species and the description survives (+ or a symmetric comparison).
- Predation, parasitism and production are ordered: consumer and resource, parasite and host. Swapping them gives a different substance.
- − (subtraction) is the removal intervention itself: A with B absent. Fig 12 :82: **"A with B removed or absent"**. ¬ (negation) is the refusal of a claim, not the removal of an actor: Fig 12 :135 *"Negation is not one: it takes a term and yields a term, and joins nothing."* A failed intervention is not a refutation of the method.
- ⋛ (comparison) is the essentiality test: with versus without, against a threshold. Operators table :51: **"a goal is a comparison, which is why a vocabulary without it cannot state one."** Without ⋛ a vocabulary can list interactions and cannot say which ones matter for a goal.
- **Falsifier:** if the with-versus-without comparison can be replaced by a symmetric conjunction with no loss on the focal function, the ordered-bond claim fails.

### Q3: What order do effects have?

Fig 11 :52: **"And contact cascades. Scoping the habitat to direct contact is how DDT reached the eagles: first-order enumeration, second-order effect, and the residual that mattered was three steps out."**
Fig 12 :119: **"catalyst. A part that survives its own use."**
Fig 12 :121: **"cycle. The output re-enters as input."**

- First-order: the direct pairwise interaction and its immediate removal outcome.
- Second-order: the effect on a competitor or mutualist of the first pair, the classic competition-release case (Wagner 2010 documents the starfish removal producing barnacle then mussel dominance, a chain rather than a single step).
- Third-order and beyond: the habitat-modification and nutrient paths (the wolf, elk, willow, beaver, songbird chain in the same source).
- A species can be a **catalyst** in one window: it survives its own use and keeps changing the response (an engineer such as the beaver). A **cycle** makes the output re-enter as input (feedback between herbivory and plant defence). Neither can be read off a static adjacency matrix, because a flat formula records ingredients and not the route (Fig 12 :14 *"the ingredients are recorded and the arrangement is not"*).
- **Falsifier:** if for a declared function the per-slot residual ranking is unchanged when all second- and higher-order contacts are removed from the enumeration, the cascade claim fails for that system.
- **Mode: `specified`.** Single-source for the specific cascade depths (Fig 11's DDT instance); triangulated for the general cascade rule (Fig 11 plus `THE_FORMULA.md` Habitat).

### Q4: What order does the investigation have?

Fig 3 :20: **"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance. Each link consumes what the one above produces, so effort spent above the break arrives at a step that cannot use it."**
Fig 3 :27: **"A link with no instrument is not a link that works. It is a link nobody has looked at."**

1. **Perception:** does the sampling return anything for the slot, at any resolution? If no, the chain breaks here (Fig 3 :22-23: *"A step whose input never arrives cannot be diagnosed, only its predecessor can."*).
2. **Vocabulary:** do we have a typed relation for what the reading could mean (pollination, predation, dependency, or an unclassified interaction)? Fig 12 :89: **"A relation that fails all six is not a bond you are missing. It is a claim that has not been made yet."**
3. **Objective:** what counts as progress, in the frame's own terms? The focal function and the ⋛ threshold are fixed here.
4. **Planning and carry:** choose the intervention and the checking frames, record the per-slot outcome, carry the method up and the head start down (Fig 4 :22).
- The residual line itself forces steps 1 to 3; planning and carrying are candidate answers introduced by the frame, not forced by the question.
- **Falsifier:** if an essentiality ranking can be produced and validated with no vocabulary for the interaction type (an untyped binary adjacency only), the vocabulary-link claim fails.

## CONCLUSION

Over the shared species set, two networks with different typed interaction relations are Fig 12 **isomers**, not variants of one thing: same atoms, different bond, different substance. Where species sets differ, the weaker **functional-group** claim holds. Effect order is first-order direct, then second- and third-order cascade, with catalyst and cycle structures that a flat matrix cannot express. The investigation is dependency-ordered and breaks at perception under poor observation. These claims need no new primitive, no import that moves the wall, and no naming of a seventh term. They do need typed bonds, a declared function and a ground that pays.
