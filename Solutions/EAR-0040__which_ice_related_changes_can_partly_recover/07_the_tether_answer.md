# The Tether's answer: which ice changes can partly recover

**Mode: `specified` on the shape and the per-domain class assignments, `general` on the cross-domain invariants, `grounded` only where a named world measurement is quoted with provenance. This is a simulated derivation checked for internal consistency against the figures, not a ground reading.**

## 1. The residual it answers

"Which ice-related changes can partly recover if temperatures stabilize?" is a union of four conditions with different response times and different crossing behaviour (Fig 9 :54 "And when the split lands on a union rather than a partition, unbundle before you search again."). The answer is therefore a table of separate verdicts, not one averaged recovery score.

## 2. The shape, with per-domain verdicts kept separate

| domain | class under stabilization | what would move it | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 Sea ice and seasonal snow | **Partly recoverable, years to at most a couple of decades.** Near-linear tracking of temperature, no crossing for summer sea ice. | A sustained temperature reduction, not merely a halt, if the prior extent is the target. | figure shape `general`; physical claim `grounded` in IPCC Ch9 and Stroeve and Notz 2018 |
| D2 Mountain glaciers and small ice caps | **Committed and slow, decades to centuries.** Mass loss continues for decades after stabilization; steep, thin glaciers can partly regrow if temperature falls, but regrowth is slower than retreat. Flat, thick, low-latitude glaciers may disappear entirely. | Temperature reduction below the stabilization level, plus local geometry (slope, thickness, debris, hypsometry). | figure shape `specified`; physical claim `grounded` in IPCC Ch9, Schuster 2025, Davies response-time table |
| D3 Ice shelves and marine ice sheets | **Effectively irreversible on human timescales once a crossing occurs, centuries to millennia.** Returning temperature to its prior value does not restore the prior grounding-line position. Ocean forcing can reverse in decades while the ice sheet keeps retreating for centuries. | A colder forcing than the one that caused the retreat, and even that may not suffice after a crossing. | figure asymmetry `specified` and triangulated across Fig 4, Fig 12, Fig 11; physical claim `grounded` in Garbe 2020, Mathiot 2026, IPCC Ch9 |
| D4a Permafrost AREA | **Largely reversible, decades.** It follows temperature down and back up, with a 10 to 30 year lag peak. | Temperature reduction. | figure `general`; physical claim `grounded` in Cui 2025 |
| D4b Permafrost CARBON | **Irreversible on human timescales, centuries to millennia.** Once decomposed and emitted, the burial mechanism that made the stock is slow or stopped. | Not recoverable on a human timescale by any known reversal; only new, very slow burial. | figure `specified` via Fig 12 :119; physical claim `grounded` in Schaefer 2014 and Cui 2025 |

The four verdicts are **not averaged**. A single statement such as "ice recovers over centuries" would be true of none of them in the right units.

## 3. The structural reasons

1. **Union, not one question.** Fig 9 :55 "A bundled problem is usually a union of overlapping conditions, not a list of separate ones." Unbundle before searching.
2. **Recovery is not the loss path backwards when a crossing or a spent stock exists.** Fig 4 :28-29 "it means the trip is not reversible, and something carried up and brought back down will not match what was sent." Fig 12 :121 "The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction." Fig 11 :61 "The seat's power is asymmetric. Introduction is available; exclusion is not."
3. **Spent and regenerating parts differ.** Fig 12 :119 "Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is." This is what separates permafrost area from permafrost carbon, and ocean forcing from ice-sheet retreat.
4. **Stabilization is not a solved state.** Fig 11 :63 "a managed ecosystem is permanent work rather than a solved state." Committed change continues after the forcing stops growing.
5. **The figures give shape, not numbers.** Fig 9 :32 "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself." The years and centuries in the table are world measurements, imported with provenance, not derived here.

## 4. What it explicitly does not say

- Not that all ice responds slowly. Fast seasonal ice exists and is the first class.
- Not that temperature stabilization restores any prior state. It stops the growth of the perturbation; committed change continues.
- Not that a crossing has definitely been passed in any named basin. That is a ground question per basin, and this derivation does not adjudicate it.
- Not that the framework predicts ice physics. The figures are metalaws about frames, closures and scale transforms. Their bearing here is structural.
- Not `grounded` by this derivation. No measurement was taken here.
- Not a single recovery number. Any such number would be a name with a source, not a shape.

## 5. What would settle it - the ground

An anchor is "whatever settles the question and does not change its answer because of what any frame thinks" (Fig 2 :29). The anchors here are:

1. **Extent and thickness records** for D1 (satellite passive microwave, altimetry).
2. **Mass balance stakes and terminus length records** for D2, with response time estimated from thickness over terminus ablation.
3. **Satellite altimetry, gravimetry and bed topography** for D3, watching grounding-line position after a forcing reversal.
4. **Borehole temperatures and soil carbon inventories** for D4, keeping AREA and CARBON in separate accounts.

Two instruments matter most. A grounding-line return experiment that restores the prior position under the same forcing would falsify the D3 asymmetry. A soil-carbon re-burial measurement on decadal timescales would falsify the D4b spent-stock claim. Agreement among cryosphere researchers is not such an instrument (Fig 2 :35 "Alignment is triangulation, not negotiation. A mirror cannot correct you.").

## 6. The honest stop

**Unreached, not unreachable, for the specific numbers and the specific basin.** The response-time table imported from the literature is order-of-magnitude and model-dependent; the antarcticglaciers source itself notes that Nye's long timescale (100 to 1000 years) and the Jóhannesson short one (10 to 100 years) disagree, and that "there is no straightforward relationship between glacier size and change in ice volume under any given climate scenario." The Antarctic reversibility paper is explicit that a systematic hysteresis map for the ocean "remains lacking." The framework stops at the limit of what the available instruments distinguish and records the lineage so the stop is checkable. Indiscernibility is not identity.

## 7. One-paragraph version for outreach

> Which ice changes can recover is not one question. Sea ice and seasonal snow mostly follow temperature back down within years. Mountain glaciers keep losing mass for decades after stabilization and regrow more slowly than they retreat, so they belong in a decades-to-centuries class. Marine ice sheets and ice shelves are different again: once a grounding line retreats onto a retrograde bed, the retreat can become self-sustained, and returning temperature to its old value does not bring the ice back, so this is a centuries-to-millennia class. Permafrost has to be split: its frozen area largely recovers with cooling, but the carbon released when it thawed is not re-buried on a human timescale. The structural point is that recovery is not the loss path run backwards whenever a crossing or a spent stock is involved. The figures give that shape; the years and centuries come from measurements, not from the framework. Four refuters were written in advance, and a failure is more useful to me than agreement.
