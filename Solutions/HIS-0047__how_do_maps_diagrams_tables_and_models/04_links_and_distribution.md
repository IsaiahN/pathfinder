## What in the wider literature bears on this question

1. **Informational versus computational equivalence (Larkin and Simon 1987, as reported in SEP).** Two representations can express the same content yet differ in which inferences are "easily and quickly" drawn. This is the world-facing twin of Fig 12 :5 ("A set of ingredients is not one. The bond is the rest of the information"), and it supplies the experimental design that would move the general claim from `specified` toward `grounded`. SEP records the specific claim: "the great utility of the diagram arises from perceptual enhancement, the fact that it makes explicit the relative positions of the equilibrium points, so that the conclusion can be read off with the help of simple, direct perceptual operations" (Larkin and Simon 1987, p.95).
2. **Not all formats help equally (Cheng 2004, as reported in the search index).** Participants solved pulley problems with diagrammatic, tabular or sentential representations. "Solutions with the diagrams were up to six times easier than informationally equivalent sentential representations" while "the tabular representation was not better overall than sentential representation." This blocks the lazy conclusion that any reformatting helps, and matches Fig 12 :99 "A bond costs what it could have been, and nothing more."
3. **Formal observational advantage (Stapleton, Jamnik and Shimojima 2016).** The theory of observation characterizes when one representation lets a reader observe more than another, and is used to show that Euler diagrams have observational advantages over set theory. This is a precise account of "which bond is carried" and corroborates Fig 12 :116.
4. **The periodic table as a functional group at scale (SEP, citing Knight 1996).** "Grouping and classifying chemical elements in terms of a visually clear table 'transformed the science'." This is the strongest table example and it is figure-licensed as a functional group (Fig 12 :123), not as a new primitive.
5. **The Snow case splits display from ground.** The revisionist Lancet reading, the Science History Institute account, the Tulchinsky table and Whitehead's primary narrative together show that the map confirmed a held theory and that the ground was settled by other means (cesspit excavation, water-company rate comparison). This is Fig 2 :31 and Fig 10 :18 in a real historical record. SEP also records the general caution that formal diagrammatic results "refuted a widely-held assumption that diagrams are inherently misleading", so the answer is neither "charts always help" nor "charts always deceive".

**Stage 1:** validate the shape claim against the reader's own ground. **Stage 2, opt-in only:** read the framework that produced it. **Stage 3:** use the formula on a problem with a checkable ground, with a self-written refuter.

Stage 1 before stage 2 is Fig 10 :52 and Fig 2 :45. Stage 2 is opt-in because once a reader holds the framework's interiors their agreement stops being evidence (Fig 2 :40). Stage 3 sends a method, which is permitted: Fig 4 :22 "Only methods cross up. Only head starts come down."
