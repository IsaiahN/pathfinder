**Subject:** Check one claim about the gender pay gap decomposition, no theory required

> Hello,
>
> I am working on a figure-grounded derivation and have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read the framework first and I am not asking you to agree with it. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`specified`, not `grounded`):** the question "how much of the gender pay gap is occupation and hours, and how much is discrimination and unequal caregiving?" asks for a partition of a single aggregate number. The derivation says the four conditions named in it form a **union of overlapping conditions** with a shared fraction that cannot be assigned to any one of them, not a partition with additive shares. It also says that a variance share is a filter for deciding where to collect the next instrument, never a witness for which condition changed a worker's pay. This is a claim about the *shape* of a defensible answer, not a measurement and not a new percentage.
>
> Three things follow that are checkable without the framework:
>
> 1. A worker who is in a part-time occupation, after a birth, with an employer who discounts mothers is a case in four conditions at once. That is a specific compound, not a mixture of four percentages.
> 2. "Discrimination" is not the label for the unexplained remainder of a regression. An unexplained remainder moves with the model that produces it. It becomes an evidence-bearing claim only when an instrument makes differential treatment readable at equal observed productivity, as in a callback audit or a blind audition.
> 3. Occupation, hours, and caregiving are conditions around the worker, not fixed properties of the worker. Their effect reverses with the question asked: a part-time schedule helps one worker's residual and harms another's. So a single fixed sign and a single fixed share are both the wrong objects.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** that any particular percentage is wrong, that discrimination does not exist, that caregiving is not important, that the figures measure a wage gap, or that this derivation settles the empirical literature. I am not claiming a universal number. The honest verdict on a universal number is `gap`, and a gap is a verdict, not a silence.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim (written down before this email was sent):**
> 1. A decomposition whose per-condition shares are invariant to adding an overlapping condition and to changing the reference category, with an assignable shared part.
> 2. One instrument that discriminates all four conditions at the worker-slot with no cross-condition remainder.
> 3. Randomizing or matching occupation, hours, and caregiving leaves the pay gap unchanged where the shape predicts a change.
> 4. A universal percentage that replicates across datasets with non-overlapping instruments and survives a new overlapping condition.
>
> If any holds, I will retract or narrow the claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** here is what I actually read. The figures, not my summary, are the authority.

> - Simpson's Paradox, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/paradox-simpson/

> - Kleven, Landais, and Søgaard, "Children and Gender Inequality: Evidence from Denmark": https://www.nber.org/papers/w24219
