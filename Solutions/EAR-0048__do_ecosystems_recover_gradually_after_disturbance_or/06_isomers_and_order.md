# Are the two recovery regimes isomers? Order and structure

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

The recovery question asks whether the same system can end in two different stable states. Two supporting questions are owed: (1) can gradual return and alternative settlement be written as **isomers** under Fig 12, and (2) what **order** do the bonds impose on the answer? If they are isomers, the difference is in the arrangement, not in the ingredients; if not, a weaker claim must stand.

## DERIVATION

### Q1 - Are they isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: "isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."
- Fig 12 :124: "functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."
- Fig 12 :121: "The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."
- Fig 13 :121: "Different routes can arrive at the same substance."

Reading: two ecosystems with the same species pool but different feedback relations are **isomers**. The species list is the formula; the bond arrangement is the missing half. A gradual-return system is a chain: the output does not re-enter with a closing sign. An alternative-state system is a cycle whose sign lets it close. Same atoms, different bond, different substance, and the difference is invisible if you only count species.

If the two systems do not share a species pool, the weaker reading applies: they are two members of the same **functional group** (a recovery route with a comparison against a reference state), with different atoms in the slots. The functional group fixes the kind; the qualifier fixes the member.

- **Falsifier, written before the run:** if the same species set under every bond arrangement returns to the same state, the isomer claim fails and only the functional-group claim stands. Equivalently, if two ecosystems sharing all species and differing only in feedback sign show identical return trajectories, Fig 12 :117 is not governing this case and the domain must be re-censused.
- **Status: triangulated** across Fig 12 (isomer definition) and Fig 13 :121-125 (different routes, same substance), with the ecology-facing check still unmeasured.

### Q2 - What order does the answer have?

- Fig 12 :112: "A plan is a sequence. A goal is a comparison. Progress is a subtraction."
- Fig 9 :27-28: "describe it before you go looking, or any frame you pick will seem to fit"
- Fig 3 :19: "Optimising a link that already works cannot rescue one that does not."

The answer must be ordered as follows:

1. **Sequence (`→`):** observe the state after disturbance and trace the trajectory. Without a sequence you can name a state and cannot name a route to one (Fig 12 :111).
2. **Comparison (`⋛`):** compare the settled state with S0. "Recovered" is a threshold relation, not a presence. If S1 differs from S0 at the same driver value, the comparison fires and the alternative-state regime is admitted.
3. **Subtraction (`-`):** progress is R(t) - R(0), per slot (Fig 1 :19). This is why an averaged resilience index cannot carry the answer.
4. **Cycle:** check the sign of the loop that re-enters. This decides whether the state closes into a self-maintaining basin (Fig 12 :121).
5. **Negation (`¬`):** a refusal is owed when a held regularity is false. If "all ecosystems return to S0" is held and the measurement contradicts it, the framework must be able to refuse the claim, not just accumulate exceptions. `THE_FORMULA.md`: "a vocabulary without ¬ can only accumulate."

Order by dependency (Fig 3): perception of state, vocabulary for what the habitat affords, objective (what counts as recovery), trajectory or intervention, learn and carry. A link with no instrument is not a link that works (Fig 3 :27).

### Q3 - Why a chain of gradual recoveries is not the same as a regime shift

- Fig 7 :32: "one long room: motion, no progress"
- Fig 7 :36: "A chain of mirrors is one long room. The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced."
- Fig 6 :23: "No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."

A sequence of small recoveries inside one basin is motion, not a crossing. A regime shift is selection of a different room, driven by the residual the last one surfaced. The two are categorically different, not points on one scale. This is the structural reason the original binary question has to be unbundled (Fig 9 :54).

### Q4 - What the isomer claim does not give

- Fig 9 :32: "you get a kind of thing, never the answer itself"
- Fig 8 :39: "So a search that finds nothing is never a proof of absence."

The isomer framing gives the shape (same atoms, different arrangement, different outcome) and a falsifier. It does not name which arrangement a given ecosystem has. That still needs the hysteresis measurement.

## CONCLUSION

Gradual return and alternative settlement are **isomers** when they share a species pool and differ in feedback arrangement; otherwise they share only a **functional group**. The order of the answer is sequence, comparison, subtraction, cycle sign, and refusal, with perception and vocabulary upstream of intervention. The isomer claim carries a stated falsifier and needs no new primitive and no import that moves the wall. The particular basin remains unmeasured.
