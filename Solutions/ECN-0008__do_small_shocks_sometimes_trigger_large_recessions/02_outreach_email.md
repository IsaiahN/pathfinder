**Subject:** One checkable claim about small shocks and large recessions

> Hi [name],
>
> I have one specific output I would like you to check against what you already know. I am not asking you to read a framework first, and I am not asking you to agree with anything. If the output does not survive, there is nothing else to look at.
>
> **The output (`general` structurally, `specified` as a mechanism, not `grounded`):** the question bundles two different conditions, a tipping point and a feedback loop. I treat them separately. The figures say:
>
> 1. A feedback loop is a cycle whose output re-enters as input, and the sign of the loop decides whether it amplifies or damps. A chain cannot close by construction. A positive loop gain above one is the condition for a small disturbance to grow.
> 2. A tipping point is a threshold comparison between two quantities, not a new kind of thing. It names the crossing, not what pushed the system to it.
> 3. A remainder compounding through an amplifying chain is how a small input becomes a large output, structurally.
> 4. A live signal is indexed by slot, not by the aggregate. Averaging across slots is how a live signal disappears into an average, so an aggregate model can be nearly right while one sector carries the signal that matters.
>
> So the shape I offer is: a shock lands in a slot, a chain there amplifies rather than damps it, a threshold switches regime, and contact cascades carry the effect several steps out. The aggregate is a lossy view of that, not the thing itself.
>
> **What I am not claiming:** I am not claiming this derivation shows that actual economies have those threshold values or loop gains. I am not claiming it settles whether small shocks sometimes trigger large recessions, because that is a question for recession data, impulse responses and measured balance sheets. I have not measured any of those. I am not claiming a butterfly analogy is evidence, and I am not claiming a consensus of economists is an anchor.
>
> **What would refute the shape claim:**
> 1. A documented case with a large response to a small shock where every measured loop gain is at or below one and no threshold crossing occurred.
> 2. A case where averaging across agents preserved the live slot signal exactly, so the aggregate carried all the information.
> 3. A mechanism called a feedback loop whose output does not re-enter as input, or whose sign does not decide closure.
> 4. A transition called a tipping point that is not a threshold relation between two quantities.
>
> I wrote these down before sending this. If any holds, I withdraw the shape claim. A one-line "this fails because..." is more useful to me than agreement. If you would rather not engage, no reply is needed.
>
> **If it survives, and only then:** I can send the figure extract and the worked audit. The figures are the authority, not my summary of them. The exact lines are in the packet.
>
> **If you want to reuse the method:** the eight-step loop is public. Run it on a problem you know well and can check against a ground. Write down what would refute your result before you run it, and state the mode of what you get. A reported failure is more useful to me than a reported success.
>
> Thanks,
> [name]

## Two-stage discipline

1. **Stage 1:** send only the shape, its mode and its refuter. Ask what the reader would count as a failure. Record that verdict **before** showing any framework material.
2. **Stage 2, opt-in only:** if they are curious, send the three figure lines and the audit. Fig 10 :42: "A verifier that can reconstruct the claim cannot verify it." Once a recipient has absorbed the frame, their agreement stops being evidence.
3. **Stage 3:** offer the formula as a reusable method, with the instruction that each user names their own ground and writes their own refuter.

Later agreement is never pooled as corroboration (Fig 2 :40). A failure is more useful than an endorsement.
