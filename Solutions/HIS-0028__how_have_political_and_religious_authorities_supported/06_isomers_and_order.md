# The two operations as bonded structures: isomers, order and a falsifier

**Mode: `specified`.** This is a simulated derivation checked against the figures, not a ground reading (see `05_what_shape_means.md` §6).

## RESIDUAL

`01` returns two operations, support (S) and limit (L), as different relations of an authority to a residual. This file asks the supporting structural question: are they different **arrangements** of the same atoms, so that Fig 12's isomer rule applies, or do they require different atoms? If they are isomers, the answer is a bond difference, which is checkable in the archive; if not, the union in `01` §1.3 was drawn at the wrong level.

## DOMAIN CENSUS

Atoms in view: the authority A, the inquiring community C, the ground G, an introduced structure M (instrument, method, office, translation), and a decree D. Bonds held from Fig 12 and the Operators table: `→` sequence (*"A then B; B needs A to have happened"*, Fig 12 :72), `⇒` production (*"A produces B; B did not exist before"*, Operators :36), `≡` identity (*"two names, one referent"*, Fig 12 :67), `⋛` comparison (*"a threshold relation between two quantities"*, Fig 12 :87), `¬` negation (the unary refusal, Operators :53-58). ## DERIVATION

### Q1: What is the bond structure of support?

Support introduces M between C and G:

```
C → (M) → G        with  A ⇒ M
```

The authority produces an atom that was not in C's library (Fig 6 :21: *"MINT composes inside the closure and can never add an atom"*, so the added atom must be an import from a closure that differs; Fig 6 :23: *"No frame escapes its own closure. The bound moves only on contact with a frame whose closure differs."*). M then sits in the middle as a real operand that can be tested separately from A. The sequence bond matters: Fig 12 :71-74 says sequence is ordered and the test is *"swap them. Does it become a different thing? Yes."* If the order is reversed, so that G is reached before M is introduced, there is no support, only an assertion.

Fig 12 :119 names the risk: *"catalyst. A part that survives its own use. Spent and regenerating parts behave differently across uses, and nothing records which a term is."* A patronage office is a catalyst while it keeps regenerating new readings; it becomes a spent part when it only reproduces its own past success. F8 in `01` is this claim at the institutional scale.

### Q2: What is the bond structure of limit?

Limit does not introduce an operand. It collapses the distinction between the decree and the settlement:

```
A ≡ G        (declared, not settled)
```

with the result that C's loop ranges against D rather than against G. Fig 10 :49: *"Declaring a meaning does not ground it."* Fig 10 :47: *"a convention that decides an outcome has stopped being one."* The `≡` bond is the right glyph because identity means *"two names, one referent"* (Fig 12 :67): the authority's word and what the world returns are declared to be the same thing. The bond is misapplied, not merely absent. That is why Fig 2 :34 says the failure is hard to notice: *"If it does, panel 3 turns into panel 1: the anchor becomes another frame, everything drifts together, and the drift is coherent, which is why nobody notices."*

There is also a second form, the self-closing room: *"cycle. The output re-enters as input. The sign of the loop decides whether it closes, and a chain cannot close by construction."* (Fig 12 :121). A body that certifies only its own previous certifications is a cycle whose sign closes. Fig 7 :36: *"A chain of mirrors is one long room. The next room must differ from the last, and match the residual it surfaced."*

### Q3: Are S and L isomers?

- Fig 12 :117: **"isomer. Same atoms, different bond, different substance. The difference is invisible in the formula and decisive in the behaviour."**
- Fig 12 :124: **"functional group. A substructure whose presence fixes the outcome. The group says what kind; the qualifier says which member."**

S uses `{A, C, G, M}` with `A ⇒ M` and `C → M → G`. L uses `{A, C, G, D}` with `A ≡ G` and a closed cycle back to C. They do **not** share the same atom set if M and D are counted as different atoms. So the strict isomer reading fails as written.

The stronger and more interesting reading is to hold the atom set fixed at `{A, C, G}` plus the two operator roles, and ask what differs. Under that reading:

- S bonds A to C through an introduced M that ranges against G. A stays outside the anchor slot.
- L bonds A directly onto G by a declared identity, collapsing the mediating slot.

Same three core atoms, different bond, different substance. Both are legal molecules under Fig 12's operator set; one is a production-and-sequence arrangement, the other is an identity-and-cycle arrangement. The behaviour differs decisively: in S a new reading can arrive that nobody predicted; in L only readings consistent with the decree can arrive. That is the isomer claim worth testing.

**Falsifier, written before the run:** if either operation requires an atom the other does not, so that no common three-atom core can be held fixed, the isomer claim fails and the weaker **functional-group** reading stands (both are instances of `authority → mediation → ground`, with different qualifiers). Concretely: show a limit operation that cannot be described without a fourth atom that support never uses, or a support that cannot be described without one that limit never uses.

### Q4: What is the order of the union?

Fig 3 :20: *"The chain is ordered by dependency, not by importance."* The dependency order for a case is:

1. perception: what did the instrument return? (anchor reachable?)
2. vocabulary: does the community have a term for what the authority affords?
3. objective: is progress more ground contact or more compliance?
4. planning: which intervention is chosen?
5. learn and carry: does the method cross up as a method, not a recording (Fig 4 :22)?

A limit operation typically breaks at link 3: the objective is silently replaced by compliance, so the chain runs in place even though links 1 and 2 still work. That is why F8 in `01` matters: the break is invisible from above it (Fig 3 :22-23).

### Q5: Is a refusal part of the structure?

The Operators table :58: *"a refutation is a negation, which is why a vocabulary without it can only accumulate"*. A community that can only record "the authority supported this" and "the authority limited that" has a vocabulary of nouns and no way to write that one of its own held terms is false. `01` §4 lists four refuters precisely so the shape can be refused rather than merely accumulated. Formula step 2 routes a failed prediction to REFUTED before any new term is minted: *"a claim the record holds is false; what is owed is a refusal, not an explanation"* (Seat Map appendix).

## CONCLUSION

Under a fixed three-atom core `{authority, inquirers, ground}`, support and limit are **isomers**: same atoms, different bonds, different behaviour. The support arrangement is production plus sequence with an importable mediator; the limit arrangement is a declared identity that seats the authority where the ground should be, optionally closed as a cycle. Under the looser counting where the mediator and the decree are distinct atoms, the claim degrades to a functional group, and the falsifier in Q3 decides which reading holds. The dependency order puts the break at the objective link, which is where a decree replaces ground contact without disturbing the visible instruments.

This framing rescues what is checkable in the "supported while limiting" observation without turning it into a moral ranking of institutions. No new atom, no import that moves the wall and no seventh operator are required.
