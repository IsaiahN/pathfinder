## Ecology and Biodiversity: 100 Unresolved Questions

### 1. Biodiversity, stability, and resilience
1. Does greater species diversity generally make ecosystems more stable, or does its effect depend on which species are present?
2. Which kinds of stability matter most: resistance to change, recovery after disturbance, or persistence of ecosystem functions?
3. Can biodiversity preserve ecosystem function when species identities and interactions are continually changing?
4. How does variation among species within the same functional group affect ecosystem resilience?
5. Are ecosystems with many rare species more resilient, or can rarity make them especially vulnerable to change?
6. Does biodiversity stabilize ecosystems mainly through differences among species, or through the presence of particular influential species?
7. How do diversity and stability relationships change across spatial and temporal scales?
8. Can ecosystem resilience be measured in ways that work across forests, oceans, grasslands, and other systems?
9. When does a diverse ecosystem become less stable because interactions among species amplify fluctuations?
10. How much biodiversity is required to maintain essential ecosystem functions under rapid environmental change?

### 2. Species interactions and ecological networks
11. Which species interactions—predation, competition, mutualism, parasitism, or facilitation—most strongly shape ecosystem resilience?
12. How do ecological networks change when species interactions vary with season, life stage, or environmental conditions?
13. When do highly connected species stabilize ecological networks, and when do they make those networks vulnerable to collapse?
14. How can researchers identify which interactions are essential when many species and relationships remain poorly observed?
15. Do ecological networks become more robust when interactions are weak, or can strong interactions be necessary for stability?
16. How do indirect effects propagate through food webs, and when do they outweigh direct interactions?
17. Can the loss of one species trigger cascading extinctions, and what determines the scale of a cascade?
18. How do mutualistic networks respond when one partner declines or changes its behavior?
19. When do ecological networks reorganize after species loss, and when do they lose important functions?
20. Can network structure predict which species or interactions should be prioritized for conservation?

### 3. Species loss, extinction, and community change
21. How does the order in which species disappear affect the functioning and stability of an ecosystem?
22. Which traits make species especially vulnerable to extinction in rapidly changing environments?
23. How do local extinctions differ in their ecological consequences from global extinctions?
24. Can communities compensate for species loss through changes in the abundance or behavior of remaining species?
25. How long can ecosystems continue functioning after key species have become too rare to perform their roles reliably?
26. Which changes in species abundance are early signs of a coming ecological collapse?
27. How do extinction risks change when species depend on one another for food, pollination, habitat, or reproduction?
28. Can the loss of a species alter evolutionary trajectories enough to cause further losses?
29. How should conservation weigh the loss of a unique species against the decline of many widespread species?
30. When should restoration aim to bring back historical species, and when should it prioritize future ecological function?

### 4. Climate change, disturbance, and ecological thresholds
31. How will changing temperature and rainfall alter species interactions, not just the ranges of individual species?
32. Can ecosystems adapt to climate change quickly enough through migration, evolution, or shifts in species composition?
33. How do heat waves, droughts, fires, storms, and floods interact to produce ecological change?
34. What determines whether an ecosystem recovers after disturbance or shifts into a new state?
35. Can ecological tipping points be predicted reliably before changes become difficult to reverse?
36. How often do early-warning indicators of ecosystem collapse provide false alarms or fail to appear?
37. How do repeated disturbances affect ecosystems differently from a single disturbance of the same total intensity?
38. When does disturbance maintain biodiversity, and when does it push ecosystems toward degradation?
39. How do climate-driven shifts in species ranges create new competition, disease risks, or food-web connections?
40. Can ecosystems that have crossed a threshold be restored, or are some changes effectively irreversible?

### 5. Ecosystem functions and services
41. Which species and interactions are most important for pollination, soil formation, nutrient cycling, water regulation, and carbon storage?
42. How reliably can researchers predict ecosystem functions from species counts alone?
43. When do different species perform interchangeable ecological roles, and when is that apparent redundancy misleading?
44. How do multiple ecosystem functions respond when biodiversity declines?
45. Can ecosystems maintain services humans depend on while losing much of their biodiversity?
46. How should conservation balance measurable human benefits against ecological values that are difficult to quantify?
47. What determines whether an ecosystem service can recover after species loss or habitat degradation?
48. How do trade-offs among food production, carbon storage, water quality, and biodiversity emerge across landscapes?
49. Can managing one ecosystem service undermine other ecological functions that are less visible or slower to change?
50. How should societies value ecosystem functions whose benefits are diffuse, long-term, or shared across borders?

### 6. Habitat, landscapes, and connectivity
51. How much habitat must remain for populations and ecological interactions to persist over the long term?
52. When does habitat fragmentation isolate vulnerable species, and when can it create new opportunities for diversity?
53. What kinds of landscape connectivity help species move without also facilitating disease, invasive species, or other harms?
54. How do habitat corridors affect gene flow, species interactions, and the spread of ecological disturbances?
55. Can small protected areas maintain viable populations when the surrounding landscape is heavily altered?
56. How do urban environments reshape ecological communities and their relationships with people?
57. Which landscape patterns best support biodiversity under changing climate conditions?
58. How do roads, fences, dams, and other infrastructure alter the movement of organisms and ecological processes?
59. Can restoring connectivity reconnect ecosystems without disrupting established local communities?
60. How should conservation planning account for species that move across administrative and national borders?

### 7. Evolution, adaptation, and ecological change
61. How quickly can species evolve traits that reduce their vulnerability to changing environments?
62. When does adaptation help a species survive but make it more harmful to other species or ecosystem functions?
63. How do ecological interactions influence which genetic traits spread through a population?
64. Can evolution occur quickly enough to prevent ecosystem collapse under rapid climate change?
65. How do changes in the behavior of predators, prey, or pollinators reshape ecological networks?
66. When does hybridization help threatened species adapt, and when does it threaten their distinctiveness?
67. How do species interactions constrain or enable evolutionary responses?
68. Can ecological restoration succeed if it ignores the capacity of organisms to adapt and change?
69. How does human-driven evolution—through harvesting, pollution, or habitat change—alter ecosystem relationships?
70. What evidence can distinguish genetic adaptation from behavioral flexibility or movement to a new habitat?

### 8. Invasive species, disease, and biological change
71. What determines whether an introduced species becomes invasive and transforms an ecosystem?
72. How do ecological networks change when a newly arrived species has no local predators or competitors?
73. When can an introduced species fill a lost ecological role, and when does it intensify ecosystem disruption?
74. How do climate change and human transport interact to increase the spread of invasive species?
75. Can biological control reduce an invasive species without creating new ecological risks?
76. How do diseases move through ecological networks that connect wildlife, livestock, and people?
77. Which changes in biodiversity increase disease transmission, and under what conditions can biodiversity reduce it?
78. How do wildlife trade and habitat disruption change the emergence and spread of infectious diseases?
79. Can ecosystems recover after invasive species or pathogens have altered their interactions and food webs?
80. How should managers respond when eradication is impossible and continued suppression has ecological side effects?

### 9. Human activity, conservation, and restoration
81. Which conservation strategies best protect ecological relationships as well as individual species?
82. How can conservation programs protect biodiversity while respecting the rights and knowledge of local communities?
83. When do protected areas conserve ecosystems effectively, and when do they displace pressures elsewhere?
84. How should conservation account for Indigenous knowledge without treating diverse communities as having a single perspective?
85. Can restoration recreate ecological interactions, or does it mainly rebuild physical habitat and species lists?
86. How should restoration goals change when climate conditions no longer resemble historical conditions?
87. When does active management strengthen resilience, and when does it prevent ecosystems from adapting on their own?
88. How can conservation prioritize actions when ecological outcomes are uncertain and resources are limited?
89. What should count as successful conservation: species persistence, ecosystem function, evolutionary potential, or human well-being?
90. How can human communities and ecosystems adapt together without shifting environmental costs onto less powerful groups?

### 10. Measurement, prediction, and ecological theory
91. How can researchers observe complex ecological networks when many interactions are rare, hidden, or difficult to measure?
92. Which measures of biodiversity best capture ecological difference, evolutionary history, and functional importance?
93. How can ecological models represent changing species interactions without becoming too complex to test?
94. Why do models that perform well in one region or period often fail under different ecological conditions?
95. How can researchers distinguish natural fluctuations from early signs of long-term ecosystem change?
96. What kinds of experiments can reveal ecosystem-level effects when controlled manipulation is impractical or unethical?
97. How should field observations, experiments, remote sensing, Indigenous knowledge, and models be combined to improve ecological prediction?
98. Can a general theory explain when ecological networks are resilient, or does resilience depend too strongly on context?
99. How can researchers connect changes in local species interactions to global patterns of biodiversity loss and ecosystem function?
100. What evidence would show that an ecosystem remains resilient—not merely intact today, but able to adapt as species, relationships, and conditions change?
