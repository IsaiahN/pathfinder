## Cognitive Science and Anthropology: 100 Unresolved Questions

### 1. Universals and cultural variation
1. Which features of human cognition are shared across all known societies, and which vary substantially across cultures?
2. How can researchers distinguish a cognitive universal from a widespread pattern caused by similar environments or institutions?
3. Are there basic cognitive capacities that develop similarly everywhere but are used differently across cultures?
4. How much variation in attention, memory, and reasoning reflects cultural learning rather than biological differences?
5. Do people in different societies categorize the world using fundamentally different mental structures, or mainly different learned categories?
6. How can researchers avoid treating the cognitive norms of industrialized societies as a universal baseline?
7. Which patterns of human behavior remain stable across historical periods, and which change when social conditions change?
8. Are there limits to cultural variation imposed by shared human biology, and how can those limits be identified?
9. How do researchers distinguish genuine cultural differences from differences produced by translation, testing, or sampling?
10. Can a general theory of human cognition accommodate deep cultural variation without reducing it to individual differences?

### 2. Perception, attention, and learning
11. How does culture shape what people notice, ignore, or treat as important in their surroundings?
12. To what extent does language influence perception, and under what conditions are its effects strongest?
13. How do environments and daily practices train people to attend to different kinds of visual or social information?
14. Do cultural differences in perception persist when people perform the same task under the same conditions?
15. How do children learn which features of their environment are worth attending to?
16. How do formal schooling and informal learning shape people’s expectations about evidence and explanation?
17. Are cultural differences in memory caused by different strategies, different social goals, or different forms of experience?
18. How do oral traditions and written records influence what people remember and how they organize the past?
19. When does repeated exposure produce shared knowledge, and when does it produce divergent interpretations?
20. How do people learn to distinguish reliable signals from noise in environments with different risks and information sources?

### 3. Concepts, categories, and language
21. How do languages and cultural practices jointly shape the categories people use to understand the world?
22. Which concepts are difficult to learn without a particular language, and which can be acquired through nonlinguistic experience?
23. How do cultures differ in how they divide up time, space, nature, and social relationships?
24. Can people reason effectively about concepts that their language does not routinely name?
25. How do metaphor and analogy shape thought across different languages and traditions?
26. How do children learn culturally specific categories such as personhood, kinship, ownership, or responsibility?
27. When do translation choices distort comparisons of concepts across cultures?
28. How do new technologies and institutions create new categories that alter everyday thought?
29. Are there universal foundations for concepts such as agency and causation, or are these concepts substantially culturally learned?
30. How do people reconcile categories that are useful in everyday life but conflict with scientific or bureaucratic classifications?

### 4. Reasoning, causation, and knowledge
31. Do people across cultures use the same basic forms of reasoning but apply them to different problems?
32. How do cultural practices influence what counts as a convincing explanation?
33. Are some reasoning strategies more common in particular institutional settings, such as markets, schools, or courts?
34. How do people infer cause and responsibility when outcomes arise from many interacting factors?
35. What shapes whether a person explains events through individual choices, social relationships, institutions, or spiritual forces?
36. How do people decide when evidence is sufficient to act, especially when waiting for certainty is costly?
37. Can researchers identify cross-cultural differences in logical reasoning without confusing test familiarity with reasoning ability?
38. How does access to formal education change people’s use of abstraction, statistics, and hypothetical reasoning?
39. How do local expertise and scientific expertise differ in the evidence they rely on and the questions they ask?
40. What makes knowledge credible within a community, and how do those standards change over time?

### 5. Selfhood, emotion, and motivation
41. How do cultures shape the experience and interpretation of emotions without determining them entirely?
42. Are emotional categories such as anger, shame, or grief experienced similarly across societies, or do they vary in important ways?
43. How do social norms influence which emotions people express, conceal, or recognize in others?
44. How do cultural models of the self affect motivation, decision-making, and personal responsibility?
45. Is the distinction between individual and collective identity universal, or does its importance depend on social organization?
46. How do people learn what kinds of desires and ambitions are acceptable or admirable?
47. How do cultures shape the relationship between emotion, bodily sensation, and explanation?
48. When do people experience personal choice as self-directed, and when as shaped by obligations or relationships?
49. How do ideas about character and moral worth influence how people interpret their own behavior?
50. How do social change and migration alter people’s sense of identity, belonging, and emotional life?

### 6. Cooperation, trust, and social learning
51. How do people learn whom to trust when social networks and institutions differ?
52. Which conditions support cooperation among strangers, and how do those conditions vary across societies?
53. How do kinship, friendship, reputation, and formal rules interact to sustain cooperation?
54. Why do people sometimes cooperate even when they expect no direct reward or punishment?
55. How do children learn local norms of sharing, reciprocity, fairness, and obligation?
56. Do people across cultures share similar intuitions about fairness, or do they apply different standards to different relationships?
57. How do communities enforce norms when formal authorities are weak or absent?
58. When does social learning preserve useful knowledge, and when does it spread ineffective or harmful practices?
59. How do status and authority affect whose knowledge is copied or trusted?
60. How do cooperation strategies change when people move from small communities into large, anonymous institutions?

### 7. Morality, religion, and meaning
61. Are there moral concerns that appear across all societies, and how do cultures interpret them differently?
62. How do local histories and institutions shape judgments about fairness, harm, loyalty, and authority?
63. How do people learn which actions are morally wrong, socially disapproved, or merely unusual?
64. What roles do religion and ritual play in creating shared beliefs, emotional experiences, and social obligations?
65. Can religious and spiritual beliefs be explained through general cognitive tendencies, or do institutions and histories do more explanatory work?
66. How do people decide whether misfortune has social, natural, moral, or supernatural causes?
67. What makes a ritual feel meaningful or effective to participants?
68. How do stories and collective memories shape moral identity and political belonging?
69. When do moral disagreements reflect different values, and when do they reflect different factual beliefs or social interests?
70. How do societies change moral norms while maintaining a sense of continuity with their past?

### 8. Childhood, development, and socialization
71. How do caregiving practices shape children’s attention, emotional regulation, and expectations of other people?
72. Which developmental patterns are robust across cultures, and which depend on family structure, schooling, or local work?
73. How do children acquire social norms without explicit instruction?
74. How do different childhood responsibilities affect learning, independence, and cooperation?
75. When do developmental theories based on formal schooling generalize to children raised in other settings?
76. How does growing up multilingual influence attention, memory, identity, and social understanding?
77. How do children learn to navigate conflicts between family norms, peer norms, and institutional rules?
78. How do differences in play, work, and storytelling shape children’s learning and social imagination?
79. What influences the timing and form of children’s understanding of agency, intention, and responsibility?
80. How do major changes in family life, technology, and education reshape development across generations?

### 9. History, institutions, and cultural change
81. How do institutions such as schools, markets, states, and religious organizations reshape habitual ways of thinking?
82. Can researchers identify how a specific institution caused a cognitive or behavioral change rather than merely accompanying it?
83. How do technologies change people’s memory, attention, communication, and sense of what can be known?
84. Why do some cultural practices persist for generations while others change rapidly?
85. How do colonialism, migration, and unequal power shape which forms of knowledge are preserved or dismissed?
86. How do economic systems influence ideas about choice, obligation, ownership, and personal success?
87. When do institutional rules change people’s values, and when do people follow rules without adopting their underlying assumptions?
88. How do collective memories of conflict or displacement shape present-day reasoning and trust?
89. Can changes in everyday cognitive practices be reconstructed from historical records, and what biases limit that reconstruction?
90. How do people combine inherited traditions with new information and institutions without simply replacing one with the other?

### 10. Methods, comparison, and theory
91. How can studies compare cognitive processes across cultures without imposing a single culture’s assumptions about what counts as intelligence or rationality?
92. What evidence is sufficient to establish that a cultural difference is real rather than an artifact of measurement?
93. How should researchers combine laboratory experiments, ethnography, historical evidence, and population-level data?
94. How can researchers test whether a cognitive pattern is caused by culture, ecology, institutions, or their interaction?
95. What methods best capture variation within a culture rather than treating each society as internally uniform?
96. How can studies account for the effects of migration, urbanization, media, and schooling on the populations they compare?
97. How should researchers address the risk that study participants change their responses because they know they are being studied?
98. Can theories of cognition make useful predictions about unfamiliar societies without reducing their practices to familiar categories?
99. What would a genuinely cross-cultural science of the mind look like in its methods, concepts, and standards of evidence?
100. How can researchers distinguish what is universal about human minds from what people learn through culture, history, and institutions?
