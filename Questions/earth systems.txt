Here are **100 unresolved Earth-system science questions**, grouped into ten modules. They treat Earth as a coupled system: changes in the atmosphere, oceans, ice, land, ecosystems, and human systems can interact, sometimes through feedbacks or abrupt shifts. A “tipping point” is a useful research concept, but its meaning and application vary across systems; thresholds, timing, and interactions are often uncertain, so the questions below don’t assume every major change is a tipping point. <citation src="1,4,5"></citation>

## 1. Earth as a coupled system

1. Which interactions among atmosphere, oceans, ice, land, and ecosystems most strongly shape Earth’s long-term behavior?
2. Which Earth-system processes are still studied too separately to understand their combined effects?
3. How do fast processes—storms, fires, and seasonal cycles—interact with slow processes such as soil formation, ice-sheet change, and ocean circulation?
4. How much can local changes in land or water use affect regional and global climate?
5. Which feedbacks stabilize the Earth system, and which amplify change?
6. Can researchers identify the most important links in the Earth system without oversimplifying the rest?
7. How do human activities alter natural feedbacks, rather than simply add external pressure?
8. Which Earth-system interactions are strongest at local, regional, continental, or global scales?
9. How do climate variability and long-term human-driven change combine to produce observed trends?
10. What does it mean to define a “safe” or “stable” Earth system when natural conditions have always varied?

## 2. Climate sensitivity, variability, and extremes

11. How much will global temperature rise in response to a given increase in greenhouse gases?
12. How will climate sensitivity change as ice, clouds, forests, and oceans respond to warming?
13. Why do climate models differ in how they represent clouds and their effects on warming?
14. How will warming change the frequency and intensity of compound extremes, such as heat combined with drought?
15. Can researchers reliably distinguish human-caused changes in extreme events from natural variability?
16. How will regional rainfall patterns shift, and how confident can projections be at the scale communities need?
17. How will climate change alter the strength, location, and timing of monsoons?
18. Can a region experience long-term drying even while global average rainfall increases?
19. How do aerosol pollution and greenhouse gases interact to affect regional climate?
20. Which climate extremes are poorly represented in models because they are rare, local, or compound?

## 3. Oceans and circulation

21. How will global warming and freshwater from melting ice affect major ocean circulation systems?
22. How stable is the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, and what observations best track its long-term changes?
23. How will changes in ocean circulation affect rainfall, temperatures, and ecosystems far from the ocean?
24. What determines whether an ocean circulation change is gradual, abrupt, or reversible?
25. How will warming, acidification, and declining oxygen interact in different ocean regions?
26. How will changing ocean currents redistribute heat, nutrients, and carbon?
27. How much carbon can the ocean continue to absorb as it warms and its chemistry changes?
28. Could changes in plankton and marine food webs substantially alter the ocean’s role in the carbon cycle?
29. How will sea-level rise vary regionally as ocean circulation, gravity, and land movement change?
30. Which ocean changes are approaching thresholds, and which are better described as continuing trends?

## 4. Ice, sea level, and frozen regions

31. How quickly will Greenland and Antarctic ice loss raise sea levels under different warming pathways?
32. Which processes control the stability of ice shelves that restrain glaciers?
33. Can small changes in ocean heat trigger large or persistent changes in ice-sheet flow?
34. How much sea-level rise is already committed because ice sheets respond slowly?
35. How will thawing permafrost release carbon dioxide and methane, and how will those releases vary by place?
36. Could thawing permafrost destabilize buildings, roads, pipelines, and other northern infrastructure faster than expected?
37. How will shrinking sea ice affect Arctic ecosystems, weather, and human activity?
38. What observations would give early warning of a major change in a glacier or ice sheet?
39. How will mountain glaciers’ retreat affect water supplies for communities and agriculture?
40. Which ice-related changes can partly recover if temperatures stabilize, and which would take centuries or longer to reverse?

## 5. Forests, ecosystems, and biodiversity

41. How will heat, drought, fire, pests, and land conversion interact to affect forest survival?
42. Under what conditions can forests shift from absorbing carbon to releasing it?
43. How close are major forests, including the Amazon and boreal forests, to persistent shifts in structure or function?
44. How do biodiversity losses affect ecosystem resilience, carbon storage, and recovery after disturbance?
45. Which species or ecological relationships are essential for maintaining ecosystem functions?
46. How quickly can species move or adapt as climate zones shift?
47. How will climate change alter fire regimes, and how much can land management influence them?
48. Do ecosystems recover gradually after disturbance, or can they settle into a different stable state?
49. How can scientists tell whether an ecosystem’s decline is driven chiefly by climate, direct human pressure, or their interaction?
50. Which ecological changes are reversible through restoration, and which require different expectations or adaptation?

## 6. Soils, water, and land systems

51. How will warming and changes in rainfall affect soil carbon storage?
52. How quickly can soil degradation reduce crop productivity, water retention, and ecosystem resilience?
53. When does drought become a persistent shift in water availability rather than a temporary event?
54. How do groundwater depletion and changing rainfall interact to affect rivers and wetlands?
55. Can soil restoration meaningfully increase carbon storage while also improving food production and water quality?
56. How will climate change alter erosion, landslides, and sediment flows?
57. Which water systems are most vulnerable to simultaneous pressure from warming, extraction, pollution, and population growth?
58. How will changes in snowpack affect river flow and water supply across seasons?
59. Can wetlands and peatlands remain carbon sinks as temperatures rise and hydrology changes?
60. How should water be managed when past patterns no longer reliably predict future supply?

## 7. Food, agriculture, and land use

61. How will heat, drought, floods, pests, and soil change combine to affect major crops?
62. Which crops and farming systems are most likely to adapt successfully to future climate conditions?
63. How can agriculture reduce emissions without shifting environmental harm to other regions?
64. Could changes in crop yields trigger land conversion that worsens biodiversity loss and climate change?
65. How do irrigation, fertilizer use, and agricultural runoff affect regional water and climate systems?
66. How resilient are global food supplies to simultaneous harvest failures in major producing regions?
67. Would diversified crops and farming systems improve resilience enough to offset possible yield or cost trade-offs?
68. How will changes in pollinators, pests, and soil organisms affect food production?
69. What land-use changes can reduce climate risk while protecting food security and ecosystems?
70. How should food systems prepare for climatic extremes that are too rare to estimate confidently from historical records?

## 8. Tipping points, thresholds, and early warning

71. What precise evidence should qualify a change as a tipping point rather than a gradual trend or abrupt event?
72. How can researchers estimate thresholds in systems that cannot be experimentally pushed through them?
73. Can early-warning signals—such as slower recovery after disturbances—reliably indicate an approaching threshold?
74. How often do real Earth systems show those warning signals, and when do they fail?
75. Can a threshold move as the climate, ecosystem, or human pressures around a system change?
76. How should researchers estimate tipping risks when observations are short and geological evidence is incomplete?
77. Could one major shift trigger changes in other parts of the Earth system?
78. Are tipping cascades physically plausible at the global scale, and which links in proposed chains are well supported?
79. Can a system cross a threshold gradually, with impacts appearing only much later?
80. How should decision-makers act when a potentially irreversible risk is plausible but its timing and probability are uncertain? <citation src="1,4,5"></citation>

## 9. Human infrastructure, society, and adaptation

81. How will climate extremes interact with power grids, transport, water, communications, and health systems?
82. Which infrastructure failures could cascade across regions or sectors?
83. How should cities adapt when heat, flooding, water stress, and sea-level rise occur together?
84. Which adaptation measures reduce risk in the long term, and which may create new vulnerabilities?
85. How do housing, income, health, and access to services shape who bears climate impacts?
86. Can early warning systems and local knowledge reduce harm even when forecasts remain uncertain?
87. How should communities plan for climate conditions unlike anything in their historical experience?
88. When does moving away from high-risk locations become safer than continued protection or reconstruction?
89. How can climate adaptation avoid shifting risk to neighboring communities or future generations?
90. What kinds of governance can coordinate local, national, and international responses to Earth-system risks?

## 10. Observation, modeling, and decision-making

91. Which missing observations most limit understanding of interactions among atmosphere, ocean, ice, land, and ecosystems?
92. How can satellites, field measurements, historical records, and Indigenous and local knowledge be combined responsibly?
93. Which Earth-system processes are most poorly represented in global models?
94. How can models represent feedbacks between ecosystems, climate, and human land use without losing useful detail?
95. How should researchers compare model projections when they disagree about the probability, timing, or severity of change?
96. Can machine learning reveal useful patterns in Earth-system data, and how can scientists guard against misleading correlations?
97. What observations would most improve forecasts at the scales where infrastructure and land-use decisions are made?
98. How should uncertainty be communicated so it is neither overstated nor mistaken for lack of risk?
99. What indicators should be monitored to detect changes early enough for practical action?
100. How can societies decide which risks to prevent, which to prepare for, and which to accept when resources are limited?
