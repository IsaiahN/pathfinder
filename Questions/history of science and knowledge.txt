## History of Science and Knowledge: 100 Unresolved Questions

### 1. Evidence and standards of proof
1. How have different societies defined convincing evidence, and what explains the differences?
2. When do standards of evidence change because of new discoveries, and when do they change because of political or institutional pressures?
3. How can historians distinguish a genuine shift in evidential standards from a change in the kinds of records that survived?
4. Why do some forms of evidence—measurement, testimony, experiment, or tradition—gain authority in particular contexts?
5. How have instruments changed what people could observe and what they considered real?
6. When does replication strengthen a claim, and when do different methods make direct replication impossible?
7. How have scientific communities handled evidence that is reliable but difficult to reproduce?
8. What makes an observation count as a fact rather than an artifact of a method or instrument?
9. How do background assumptions shape which evidence researchers notice, record, or disregard?
10. Can evidence be evaluated independently of the institutions and communities that produce and interpret it?

### 2. Trust, authority, and expertise
11. How have societies decided who is qualified to produce or interpret knowledge?
12. What makes some forms of expertise trusted in one historical period and rejected in another?
13. How do social status, credentials, gender, class, race, and ethnicity influence whose claims are believed?
14. When does public trust in experts reflect careful evaluation, and when does it rely mainly on institutional reputation?
15. How do experts earn authority beyond formal training and professional titles?
16. Why do some communities rely more on local practitioners, witnesses, or elders than on credentialed specialists?
17. How have conflicts between academic, professional, religious, and traditional authorities been resolved—or left unresolved?
18. When does skepticism toward experts improve knowledge, and when does it make reliable knowledge harder to establish?
19. How do mistakes by respected institutions change public trust in knowledge more broadly?
20. Can institutions earn durable trust while acknowledging uncertainty and disagreement?

### 3. Institutions and organization of knowledge
21. How have universities, academies, laboratories, museums, libraries, and government agencies shaped what could be known?
22. Why have some institutions encouraged open criticism while others discouraged challenges to established claims?
23. How do funding systems shape which questions researchers ask and which findings receive attention?
24. When do professional rules improve the reliability of knowledge, and when do they protect established interests?
25. How have journals, peer review, and scientific societies changed the circulation and evaluation of claims?
26. How do bureaucracies transform uncertain findings into rules, statistics, or official categories?
27. What determines whether institutions correct errors quickly or allow them to persist?
28. How have political and religious authorities supported scientific inquiry while also limiting it?
29. Can institutions preserve specialized expertise without isolating experts from public needs and criticism?
30. How do rival institutions compete to define authoritative knowledge?

### 4. Knowledge, power, and exclusion
31. How have political power and economic interests shaped which questions could safely be asked?
32. Which forms of knowledge have been suppressed, appropriated, or excluded from official histories of science?
33. How have colonialism and empire influenced the collection and classification of knowledge?
34. When has scientific research challenged unequal power, and when has it helped justify or strengthen it?
35. How have people excluded from formal institutions created and preserved knowledge?
36. What evidence can reveal the contributions of researchers whose work was uncredited or erased?
37. How do classifications of people and nature reflect the interests of those who create them?
38. When does sharing knowledge across cultures produce genuine exchange, and when does it become extraction?
39. How have laws, patents, and ownership rules shaped access to knowledge and control over its use?
40. Can histories of knowledge correct past exclusions without treating communities or traditions as internally uniform?

### 5. Categories, facts, and measurement
41. How have new measurement systems changed the categories societies use to describe people, nature, and social life?
42. When do classifications reveal meaningful patterns, and when do they impose divisions that obscure complexity?
43. How have statistics changed what governments, institutions, and publics regard as social problems?
44. What makes a measurement valid when the thing being measured—such as intelligence, health, or poverty—is contested?
45. How do changes in units, instruments, or definitions alter apparent trends across time?
46. Can historical data be compared reliably when the people, categories, and methods used to collect it have changed?
47. How do maps, diagrams, tables, and models shape reasoning rather than merely display results?
48. When do numerical measures increase accountability, and when do they encourage people to optimize the measure rather than the underlying goal?
49. How do metaphors and analogies influence scientific classification and explanation?
50. What determines whether a new category becomes widely accepted and used?

### 6. Disagreement, controversy, and consensus
51. Why do some scientific disagreements lead to better methods and stronger explanations while others remain entrenched?
52. How can historians distinguish productive scientific controversy from disputes driven mainly by status or institutional rivalry?
53. What causes communities to converge on a shared conclusion despite persistent disagreement about its interpretation?
54. When does consensus reflect strong evidence, and when can it reflect conformity or exclusion?
55. How have minority positions within science influenced later research, even when they were initially rejected?
56. What kinds of evidence or argument can change the minds of experts committed to competing theories?
57. How do public controversies alter scientific research agendas and standards of proof?
58. Can disagreement improve collective reasoning when participants do not share standards of evidence?
59. Why do some controversies persist after key factual questions have been settled?
60. How can societies distinguish legitimate uncertainty from strategically manufactured doubt?

### 7. Methods, technologies, and ways of knowing
61. How have experiments changed the relationship between observation, intervention, and explanation?
62. Why have some research methods become authoritative while others remained marginal?
63. How have new technologies created forms of evidence that older theories could not easily interpret?
64. When do computational models extend human reasoning, and when do they conceal assumptions behind complexity?
65. How have research practices changed when observations became large-scale, automated, or remote?
66. How can researchers compare knowledge produced through controlled experiments with knowledge gained through long-term observation or practice?
67. What makes a method portable across different places and populations?
68. How do ethical rules change what can be studied and what counts as an acceptable method?
69. When does reliance on instruments increase objectivity, and when does it create new forms of dependence and uncertainty?
70. How have technologies for recording and communicating information changed what can be preserved as knowledge?

### 8. Transmission, education, and public knowledge
71. How have schools and universities shaped what societies regard as basic, authoritative, or useful knowledge?
72. Why do some ideas spread widely while others remain confined to specialist communities?
73. How have translation and language differences changed the circulation of scientific ideas?
74. What gets lost when knowledge moves from local practice into formal textbooks, databases, or policy documents?
75. How do public demonstrations, museums, exhibitions, and popular writing shape beliefs about science?
76. When does simplifying an explanation make knowledge accessible, and when does it distort the underlying uncertainty?
77. How do communication technologies change the speed, reach, and credibility of competing claims?
78. Why do educational systems preserve some inherited ideas while encouraging students to question others?
79. How have oral, written, and digital forms of knowledge altered what communities can remember and share?
80. Can public engagement improve research questions, rather than only improve public acceptance of research?

### 9. Knowledge outside formal science
81. How can historians compare scientific knowledge with practical, Indigenous, religious, and craft traditions without forcing them into the same categories?
82. What makes practical knowledge reliable when it is transmitted through apprenticeship rather than explicit theory?
83. How do communities test and revise knowledge that is tied to place, identity, or long experience?
84. When do formal scientific methods overlook patterns that local practitioners can detect?
85. How have different knowledge systems influenced one another, including through unequal relationships?
86. Can knowledge be useful and dependable without fitting the forms of proof favored by scientific institutions?
87. How do practices such as healing, farming, navigation, and building preserve knowledge across generations?
88. What responsibilities arise when researchers study, publish, or commercialize knowledge held by communities?
89. How do societies decide which inherited practices to retain, modify, or reject?
90. Can plural knowledge systems cooperate when they disagree about causes, evidence, or what counts as success?

### 10. Historical explanation and contemporary relevance
91. How can historians determine whether an idea changed society or merely reflected changes already underway?
92. What evidence is needed to trace how a claim moved from observation to theory, institution, and public policy?
93. How should histories of science account for chance discoveries, failed projects, and abandoned research programs?
94. Why do some scientific ideas recur after having been rejected, and what explains their later acceptance?
95. How do historical narratives of scientific progress shape present-day expectations about research and innovation?
96. Can studying past scientific errors help distinguish correctable mistakes from enduring limits of knowledge?
97. How should histories of scientific harm inform current decisions without treating all research as inherently untrustworthy?
98. What can past episodes of public disagreement teach about communicating uncertainty today?
99. How do societies decide which unanswered questions deserve public resources and long-term attention?
100. What historical conditions help people disagree about knowledge while remaining open to evidence, criticism, and revision?
