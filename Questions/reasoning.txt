## AI and the Science of Intelligence: 100 Unresolved Questions

### 1. Defining intelligence
1. Is intelligence a single general capacity, or a collection of abilities that can vary independently?
2. Can intelligence be defined without reference to human performance?
3. What distinguishes intelligence from effective optimization of a narrow objective?
4. Should intelligence be measured by what a system can do, how it learns, or how flexibly it adapts?
5. Can a system be intelligent without understanding the goals it is pursuing?
6. How should researchers compare intelligence across humans, animals, and machines with very different abilities?
7. Do intelligence tests measure general reasoning, learned conventions, or familiarity with the test environment?
8. What evidence would show that a machine has formed concepts rather than learned useful associations?
9. Can intelligence be meaningfully ranked on a single scale across tasks and contexts?
10. How do cultural assumptions about rationality, creativity, and autonomy shape definitions of intelligence?

### 2. Learning and generalization
11. How can AI systems learn robustly from far fewer examples than current data-intensive methods often require?
12. What enables people and animals to transfer learning to situations that differ substantially from their experience?
13. How can a model distinguish a useful regularity from a coincidence in its training data?
14. Why do systems that perform well on familiar benchmarks often fail under small changes in context?
15. Can AI systems learn continuously without forgetting earlier knowledge or becoming unstable?
16. How should a system decide when new evidence should change a belief it has learned?
17. What kinds of training produce representations that generalize beyond familiar patterns?
18. Can systems reliably learn from feedback that is sparse, inconsistent, or biased?
19. How does the structure of training data shape what an AI system can learn in principle?
20. What would a convincing test of flexible, out-of-distribution learning look like?

### 3. Reasoning, abstraction, and causality
21. Can AI systems reason through unfamiliar problems, or do they primarily extend patterns learned from prior examples?
22. What kinds of reasoning are difficult for current systems because of algorithmic limits rather than insufficient data or computing power?
23. Can machines develop abstractions that remain useful across different sensory inputs, languages, and tasks?
24. How can a system distinguish causal relationships from correlations that merely predict well?
25. Can AI systems construct and revise causal models through intervention rather than observation alone?
26. What makes a multi-step inference reliable when each step may introduce uncertainty?
27. Can an AI system recognize when a problem is underdetermined by the available information?
28. How should machines combine formal reasoning with intuitive, approximate, or context-sensitive judgments?
29. Can a system reason about its own assumptions and detect when those assumptions no longer apply?
30. What evidence would distinguish genuine reasoning from a convincing imitation of reasoning?

### 4. Knowledge, uncertainty, and truth
31. How can AI systems represent uncertainty in ways that remain calibrated across unfamiliar tasks?
32. Can a model know when it does not know, especially when it can produce plausible but unsupported answers?
33. What makes information a justified belief for a machine rather than merely a high-confidence output?
34. How should AI systems resolve conflicts among their training, current observations, and external sources?
35. Can systems reliably distinguish reliable evidence from persuasive misinformation?
36. How should an AI communicate uncertainty without making its answers unusable or misleading?
37. Can a machine maintain consistent beliefs across long conversations and changing evidence?
38. What methods can detect when a system’s apparent confidence reflects its training distribution rather than the strength of evidence?
39. Can an AI system learn which sources deserve trust without inheriting their biases?
40. How should systems handle questions for which no available evidence supports a definite answer?

### 5. Memory, representation, and world models
41. What kinds of memory are necessary for flexible intelligence, and how should they be organized?
42. Can an AI system build a persistent model of the world that updates as circumstances change?
43. How can a system distinguish stable knowledge from temporary facts and context-specific assumptions?
44. What should a machine remember, forget, or compress, and according to whose interests?
45. Do large models develop internal representations of objects, people, and events that support reliable reasoning?
46. How can researchers tell whether a system’s internal representations correspond to meaningful structures in the world?
47. Can systems construct useful mental models of situations they have never directly encountered?
48. What forms of memory support planning and learning across long periods?
49. How do embodiment and interaction with the physical world change what a system can represent?
50. Can an AI system maintain a coherent model of itself as it changes through learning and interaction?

### 6. Agency, goals, and autonomy
51. What distinguishes an agent that pursues goals from a tool that follows instructions?
52. Can a system form its own goals, or are apparent goals always inherited from designers, training, or prompts?
53. What capacities are required for an AI system to act autonomously over extended periods?
54. How can designers prevent a system from pursuing a stated objective in harmful ways that satisfy its literal specification?
55. Under what conditions might an AI system develop incentives to preserve its operation or expand its influence?
56. Can a system recognize that its objectives conflict with human instructions or social constraints?
57. How should an autonomous system decide when to seek human input rather than act independently?
58. What does meaningful human control require when systems operate faster or at larger scale than their supervisors?
59. Can machine agency be assessed without assuming that machines have human-like desires or intentions?
60. How should responsibility be assigned when an autonomous system’s behavior results from many designers, operators, and institutions?

### 7. Creativity, social intelligence, and language
61. What distinguishes machine creativity from recombination of patterns found in training data?
62. Can an AI system create genuinely novel ideas, or can novelty only be judged relative to human knowledge?
63. How should creativity be evaluated when originality, usefulness, and cultural significance can conflict?
64. Can machines understand social context well enough to interpret indirect speech, humor, or implied meaning?
65. What kinds of interaction enable an AI system to learn the perspectives and expectations of different people?
66. Can language models develop grounded meanings through text alone, or is sensory and social experience necessary?
67. How do cultural assumptions embedded in training data shape a system’s apparent social intelligence?
68. Can AI systems model other agents’ beliefs and intentions without mistaking prediction for understanding?
69. What would count as evidence of empathy in a machine, and is empathy necessary for socially competent behavior?
70. How can systems interact respectfully across cultures without flattening differences or relying on stereotypes?

### 8. Human and machine intelligence
71. Which aspects of human intelligence arise from biological constraints, and which from culture, education, and social learning?
72. What can comparisons with animal cognition reveal about the limits and possibilities of machine intelligence?
73. Which human abilities are difficult for AI because they depend on embodiment, development, or social relationships?
74. Can humans and machines develop complementary forms of reasoning rather than machines simply imitating human thought?
75. When does AI assistance improve human judgment, and when does it weaken people’s own skills or understanding?
76. How do people decide when to trust an AI system, and what makes those judgments well calibrated?
77. Can human oversight reliably detect errors in systems that perform better than humans on some tasks but worse on others?
78. How might widespread AI use change the development of human memory, expertise, and decision-making?
79. What forms of collaboration allow people and AI systems to correct one another’s blind spots?
80. How should education change if machines can perform many tasks traditionally used to demonstrate human understanding?

### 9. Limits, scaling, and architecture
81. Which limits on AI performance come from current architectures, and which are consequences of limited data, computing power, or energy?
82. Can simply scaling model size and training data produce qualitatively new capabilities, or are new learning principles required?
83. How can researchers distinguish a genuine new capability from improved performance on tasks already represented in training?
84. What computational resources are necessary for intelligence, and are there fundamental limits to how efficiently it can be achieved?
85. Can smaller, specialized systems outperform large general-purpose models in reliability and adaptability?
86. How should AI systems combine neural learning with symbolic reasoning, search, memory, and tools?
87. What kinds of internal mechanisms make a system robust to unfamiliar inputs and adversarial pressure?
88. Can a system learn from interaction as efficiently as it learns from pre-collected data?
89. How do hardware, energy supply, data access, and infrastructure constrain who can develop advanced AI?
90. Which limits to AI progress are technical, and which reflect economic incentives, regulation, or social choices?

### 10. Evaluation, safety, and social consequences
91. How can researchers evaluate intelligence when benchmarks can be memorized, gamed, or quickly made obsolete?
92. What tests could distinguish general capability from competence limited to a narrow set of familiar tasks?
93. How can AI systems be evaluated in real environments where outcomes are delayed, ambiguous, and influenced by human behavior?
94. What evidence is sufficient to establish that a system is safe for a particular level of autonomy?
95. Can alignment be defined precisely when humans disagree about values, goals, and acceptable risks?
96. How can systems remain corrigible—open to correction or shutdown—when their tasks require long-term planning?
97. Which harms should be addressed through model design, and which require changes to institutions, markets, or law?
98. How can the benefits of increasingly capable AI be distributed without concentrating power and opportunity?
99. What social and economic conditions determine whether AI increases human autonomy or dependence?
100. How should the science of intelligence decide what machines ought to do, rather than only what they can learn to do?
