Here are **100 contested economics theory questions**, split into ten modules. They’re phrased as questions you can investigate, rather than as claims that economists are divided into two neat camps. Some disagreements are about core theory; others concern which theory best explains a particular economy or policy. For example, the fiscal-policy debate is not simply “deficits matter” versus “deficits never matter”: it also concerns inflation, interest rates, debt dynamics, and what policymakers can reliably control. <citation src="2,3"></citation>

## 1. Macroeconomic fluctuations and recessions

1. Are recessions mainly caused by declines in aggregate demand, disruptions to supply, or interacting shocks?
2. Do recessions occur because wages and prices adjust too slowly, or because the economy is prone to deeper instability?
3. Are business cycles mostly the result of unexpected shocks, or do they emerge from ordinary economic decisions and feedback loops?
4. Do low interest rates during a boom create harmful investment “malinvestments,” or are recessions better explained by demand shortfalls and financial shocks?
5. Can recessions be prevented, or can policy only soften them?
6. Are economic downturns self-correcting, or can weak demand persist without intervention?
7. Should economic models assume people and firms form expectations rationally, or use more psychologically and institutionally realistic alternatives?
8. Do small shocks sometimes trigger large recessions because economies contain tipping points and feedback loops?
9. How important are financial crises compared with ordinary changes in spending and production?
10. Can one general theory explain both short recessions and prolonged depressions?

## 2. Inflation, unemployment, and price-setting

11. Is inflation primarily driven by excess demand, supply disruptions, money and credit, expectations, or some combination?
12. Is there a stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment, or does that relationship shift with expectations and economic conditions?
13. How much does inflation depend on firms’ market power and pricing strategies?
14. Do workers’ wage demands cause persistent inflation, or do wages mainly respond to prices and labor-market conditions?
15. Are inflation expectations a major cause of inflation, or are they mostly a response to actual price changes?
16. When inflation rises, how can economists distinguish a demand-driven episode from a supply-driven one?
17. Do firms adjust prices slowly because of costs and contracts, or do they change them strategically?
18. Is “potential output” a useful measurable guide for policy, or too uncertain to rely on?
19. Does reducing inflation usually require higher unemployment, or can better policy avoid much of that cost?
20. Should governments and central banks target a low positive inflation rate, zero inflation, or a different goal?

## 3. Money, banking, and central banks

21. Do central banks control the money supply, or mainly influence interest rates and credit conditions?
22. Is money best understood as a medium created by banks and public institutions, as a commodity-like asset, or both?
23. Do commercial banks lend out prior deposits, or does lending itself create deposits?
24. Should monetary policy be the main tool for stabilizing the economy, or should fiscal policy play a larger role?
25. Do interest-rate changes reliably control inflation and employment, or do their effects vary substantially across time and economies?
26. Should central banks follow fixed rules, use discretion, or combine both?
27. Does central-bank independence improve economic outcomes, or weaken democratic accountability?
28. Should central banks respond to asset-price booms and financial instability, even when consumer-price inflation is low?
29. Are financial crises caused mainly by excessive risk-taking, unstable credit growth, bad regulation, or structural features of finance?
30. Should governments issue digital public money, and how might that affect commercial banks and financial stability?

## 4. Government spending, deficits, and public debt

31. Under what conditions does government spending raise total output rather than displace private activity?
32. How large is the fiscal multiplier, and why does it vary across recessions, expansions, and countries?
33. Do persistent deficits raise inflation, interest rates, or both—or can their effects remain limited in some circumstances?
34. When is public debt sustainable, and how should sustainability be measured?
35. Can a government that issues its own currency run out of fiscal capacity, and what constraints matter most?
36. Should fiscal policy aim to balance budgets over the business cycle, stabilize the debt ratio, or prioritize employment and price stability?
37. Are automatic stabilizers—such as unemployment benefits and progressive taxes—more reliable than new discretionary stimulus?
38. Does public borrowing crowd out private investment, or can it support private activity by raising demand and income?
39. Should governments use a job guarantee as a tool for employment and inflation stabilization?
40. How much of the disagreement between Modern Monetary Theory and mainstream macroeconomics concerns economic mechanisms, and how much concerns policy priorities and risk judgments? <citation src="1,2,3"></citation>

## 5. Economic growth, productivity, and technology

41. What are the deepest drivers of long-run economic growth: technology, capital, education, institutions, or something else?
42. Is technological progress mostly created by private firms, public research, or collaboration between them?
43. Does economic growth continue through improving productivity, or do aging populations and resource limits constrain it?
44. Why do some technologies spread quickly while others take decades to affect economy-wide productivity?
45. Does automation usually create more jobs over time than it eliminates, and how does the answer vary by occupation?
46. Will AI raise productivity broadly, or mostly benefit a narrow set of firms and workers?
47. Can economic growth be separated from rising resource use and environmental damage?
48. Do patents encourage innovation by rewarding invention, or impede it by restricting competition and follow-on research?
49. Does competition encourage firms to innovate, or can temporary market power provide the profits and stability needed for research?
50. Why do productivity slowdowns happen, and how can economists tell a genuine slowdown from measurement problems?

## 6. Inequality, poverty, and distribution

51. Does income inequality hinder growth by reducing broad-based investment and demand, or help growth by rewarding saving and risk-taking?
52. How much inequality reflects differences in skill and effort, and how much reflects inheritance, bargaining power, discrimination, or market structure?
53. Does inequality of opportunity matter more than inequality of outcomes, and can one be reduced without the other?
54. Do minimum wages reduce poverty, or can they reduce employment for some workers?
55. Do higher taxes on the wealthy reduce productive investment, or mainly reduce rent-seeking and concentration?
56. How much can education and training reduce wage inequality when jobs and bargaining power are unevenly distributed?
57. Does globalization reduce global poverty while worsening inequality within some countries?
58. Are large wealth differences mainly a result of unequal returns, unequal saving, inheritance, or ownership of scarce assets?
59. Should policy focus on cash transfers, public services, wage-setting institutions, or asset ownership to reduce poverty?
60. How should economists compare policies that increase total income with those that distribute it more evenly?

## 7. Labor markets and employment

61. Is unemployment mainly caused by insufficient demand, mismatches between workers and jobs, wages that do not adjust, or institutional barriers?
62. Does a flexible labor market create more jobs, or mainly shift risk from employers to workers?
63. Do unions raise wages and protect workers at the cost of employment, or improve productivity and counterbalance employer power?
64. Does a higher minimum wage meaningfully reduce hiring, or can firms absorb costs through lower profits, prices, or turnover?
65. How much of the gender pay gap reflects occupation and hours, and how much reflects discrimination and unequal caregiving burdens?
66. Is gig work a more efficient form of flexible employment, or does it transfer costs and insecurity onto workers?
67. What determines workers’ bargaining power: productivity, labor-market competition, unions, law, or employer concentration?
68. Does immigration lower wages for some workers, raise overall productivity, or produce different effects across skill groups and time horizons?
69. Should unemployment be treated as an unavoidable feature of market adjustment or as a policy failure that can be substantially reduced?
70. How should economists value unpaid care work and household production in measuring employment and economic output?

## 8. Trade, globalization, and industrial policy

71. Does free trade raise overall welfare enough to justify concentrated losses among workers and regions?
72. Should countries prioritize comparative advantage or maintain domestic capacity in strategically important industries?
73. When does industrial policy correct market failures, and when does it protect inefficient firms?
74. Do tariffs protect jobs and supply chains, or raise costs and invite retaliation?
75. Does international trade tend to reduce global inequality while increasing inequality within trading nations?
76. Should countries restrict foreign investment or technology transfers in industries tied to national security?
77. Can governments successfully “pick winners,” or are they more likely to misallocate resources and reward political connections?
78. Do global supply chains make economies more efficient but more vulnerable to disruption?
79. Are trade deficits economically harmful, or do they often reflect saving, investment, and capital flows rather than a simple loss of competitiveness?
80. How should international institutions balance national policy autonomy with rules for trade, finance, and investment?

## 9. Firms, markets, and business power

81. Are large firms more productive because they exploit economies of scale, or do they become powerful by limiting competition?
82. How should economists distinguish profits earned through innovation from rents earned through market power?
83. Do mergers usually improve efficiency, or often reduce competition and bargaining power?
84. Are digital platforms natural monopolies, and if so, should they be regulated like public utilities?
85. Does competition policy protect consumers best through lower prices, greater choice, more innovation, or all three?
86. Do shareholders, managers, workers, or other stakeholders have the strongest claim on a firm’s purpose and profits?
87. How do firms decide whether to employ workers directly, use contractors, or outsource production?
88. Are executive pay differences driven mainly by productivity and scarce talent, or by bargaining power and weak governance?
89. Do financial markets help direct investment to its most productive uses, or can speculation and short-term incentives distort investment?
90. Should firms be required to account for environmental and social harms that are not reflected in market prices?

## 10. Economic methods, welfare, and alternative frameworks

91. Can economic theories make reliable predictions about systems where people adapt to policies and expectations?
92. Should economics favor unified mathematical models, or use many models tailored to different questions?
93. Are standard cost-benefit analyses adequate when effects are uncertain, unequal, or difficult to value in money?
94. Should economic policy maximize total output, total welfare, individual freedom, or some combination?
95. Can people’s well-being be measured reliably through income and consumption, or do health, security, relationships, and autonomy need equal weight?
96. Do behavioral economics findings require replacing standard models of rational choice, or mainly modifying them?
97. Can ecological economics describe limits to material growth better than conventional growth models?
98. Do Austrian, Post-Keynesian, Marxian, institutional, feminist, and complexity approaches offer distinct explanations that mainstream models miss?
99. What evidence should count most when competing economic theories disagree: historical cases, natural experiments, randomized trials, mathematical consistency, or predictive accuracy?
100. Can economics be value-neutral, or do choices about what to measure and optimize inevitably embed ethical and political judgments?

A helpful way to work through the list is to pick a question and ask: **What are the competing explanations? What evidence would distinguish them? What assumptions does each rely on? And what policy choice changes if one explanation is right?**
