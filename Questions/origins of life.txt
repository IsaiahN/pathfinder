Here are **100 unresolved research questions about the origin of life**, organized into ten modules. They span prebiotic chemistry, geology, planetary science, and evolutionary theory. The big competing research programs include **RNA-first**, **metabolism-first**, **membrane/protocell-first**, and hybrid scenarios—but these are not always mutually exclusive. A central challenge is explaining how chemistry could link together replication, energy use, and compartmentalization, not just how one potentially useful molecule formed. <citation src="2"></citation>

## 1. What counts as “life,” and what counts as its origin?

1. What minimum properties must a system have to count as alive?
2. Did life begin in one identifiable transition, or through many gradual steps from chemistry to biology?
3. What is the simplest system that can undergo Darwinian evolution?
4. Must the first life have had genes, metabolism, a boundary, or some combination?
5. Did the first evolving system consist of individual molecules, chemical networks, or compartments?
6. Can a system evolve before it has genes in the modern sense?
7. How can researchers distinguish a genuinely evolving chemical system from a reaction that merely repeats?
8. Is there a clear scientific boundary between prebiotic chemistry and the first life?
9. Did “life” begin only when a system could reproduce independently, or earlier when chemical selection began?
10. Could there be multiple scientifically reasonable definitions of life, each useful for a different question?

## 2. Early Earth: where and when?

11. When did Earth first have environments capable of supporting prebiotic chemistry?
12. How soon after Earth formed could the first life have appeared?
13. Was the early Earth’s surface continuously habitable, or did impacts repeatedly disrupt it?
14. What were the atmosphere’s composition and pressure during the relevant period?
15. How much oxygen, if any, was present before oxygen-producing life evolved?
16. Were early oceans acidic, alkaline, or chemically varied from place to place?
17. Which settings lasted long enough for complicated chemistry to build up?
18. Did life emerge in deep-sea hydrothermal systems, shallow ponds, volcanic land, ice, or another setting?
19. Could life have begun in more than one environment, with only one lineage surviving?
20. What geological evidence could distinguish these proposed settings?

## 3. Prebiotic ingredients and chemical supply

21. How were amino acids, sugars, nucleobases, and membrane-forming molecules produced on early Earth?
22. Which ingredients could have formed locally, and which may have arrived on meteorites or comets?
23. Were the necessary molecules present in the same place at the same time?
24. How could useful molecules become concentrated instead of remaining dilute in water?
25. Could minerals or rock surfaces have helped assemble and organize prebiotic molecules?
26. How important were ultraviolet light, lightning, heat, or chemical gradients as energy sources?
27. Could meteorite-delivered organic compounds have supplied a meaningful share of life’s building blocks?
28. How did prebiotic chemistry avoid producing complex mixtures dominated by unwanted or harmful compounds?
29. Which chemicals would have been stable enough to accumulate on early Earth?
30. Can laboratory experiments reproduce a plausible sequence of natural conditions rather than a single isolated reaction?

## 4. RNA-first and genetic information

31. Could RNA have been the first molecule able to store information and catalyze reactions?
32. Could the building blocks of RNA form naturally under realistic early-Earth conditions?
33. How could RNA nucleotides link into long strands without enzymes?
34. Could short RNA strands join or copy one another often enough to support evolution?
35. How could an early RNA replicator copy itself accurately enough to persist but inaccurately enough to evolve?
36. How would RNA replication avoid being overwhelmed by defective or parasitic sequences?
37. Could a small RNA system sustain multiple generations of replication under natural conditions?
38. Did RNA emerge directly, or was it preceded by a simpler genetic polymer?
39. If another polymer came first, what chemical properties would it need to pass useful information to RNA?
40. Did RNA-based evolution require a membrane or some other form of compartment from the start?

RNA-first research faces a key gap between showing that particular RNA reactions can occur in the lab and establishing that a complete, sustained RNA-based evolutionary system could arise under plausible environmental conditions. <citation src="2"></citation>

## 5. Metabolism-first and chemical networks

41. Could self-sustaining chemical networks have formed before genetic molecules?
42. What is the smallest chemical network capable of harvesting energy and maintaining itself?
43. Could iron, sulfur, or other minerals catalyze early metabolic reactions?
44. Did the first metabolism resemble any modern pathway, or was it fundamentally different?
45. Could naturally occurring chemical gradients power primitive metabolic networks?
46. How might a network of reactions become inherited from one generation of compartments to another?
47. Can metabolism evolve before genes encode enzymes?
48. How could a primitive metabolic network avoid losing essential reactions when conditions changed?
49. Did early metabolism produce the building blocks needed for genetic polymers, or did it depend on those polymers?
50. Can researchers experimentally create a chemical system that grows, maintains itself, and undergoes heritable change without modern enzymes?

## 6. Membranes, compartments, and protocells

51. What kinds of simple molecules could form the first cell-like boundaries?
52. Could early membranes grow and divide without complex cellular machinery?
53. How could a membrane retain useful molecules while still allowing nutrients and waste to pass?
54. Did compartments form before replicating molecules, or did molecules become enclosed afterward?
55. Could simple protocells compete and reproduce differently from one another?
56. How could genetic molecules, catalysts, and energy-producing chemistry become gathered in the same compartment?
57. Would a primitive membrane survive heat, salt, acidity, or other plausible early-Earth conditions?
58. How could compartments divide without losing all their contents or genetic information?
59. Could mineral pores or rock structures have acted as compartments before free-floating membranes evolved?
60. What experimental result would demonstrate that a protocell can undergo sustained Darwinian evolution?

A central unresolved problem is how replication, metabolism, and boundaries became coupled: a replicating molecule alone is not yet a cell, while a simple compartment alone does not automatically reproduce information. <citation src="2"></citation>

## 7. Energy, environment, and chemical cycles

61. What energy source powered the earliest self-maintaining chemical systems?
62. Could natural heating and cooling cycles concentrate molecules and encourage reactions?
63. Could wet–dry cycles help build long polymers from smaller molecules?
64. Could freeze–thaw cycles support copying or assembly of genetic molecules?
65. Were hydrothermal chemical gradients strong and stable enough to drive early chemistry?
66. How could early systems capture energy without modern enzymes and membranes?
67. Did early metabolism rely on sunlight, chemical reactions, or multiple sources?
68. How could chemical cycles persist despite dilution, degradation, and changing environmental conditions?
69. Could environmental cycles provide a kind of “external memory” that helped organize reactions?
70. Which geochemical settings combine the right energy, concentration, stability, and chemical ingredients?

## 8. From chemical evolution to cells

71. How did early genetic systems begin to produce peptides or proteins?
72. Why did the modern genetic code arise, and could alternative codes have worked?
73. How did translation—converting genetic sequences into proteins—begin?
74. Why do modern organisms use DNA for long-term information storage and RNA for many intermediate roles?
75. When and why did DNA replace RNA as the main hereditary material?
76. How did early catalysts become genetically encoded?
77. What were the first cellular systems like before the last universal common ancestor?
78. Did early life evolve in a network of gene-sharing communities rather than as cleanly separated lineages?
79. How much can scientists infer about the earliest life from features shared by modern organisms?
80. Could the first cells have been radically unlike any organisms alive today?

## 9. Evidence, experiments, and competing explanations

81. What evidence would most strongly favor RNA-first over metabolism-first—or a hybrid model?
82. How should experiments balance chemical plausibility against the need to produce a complete, evolving system?
83. Which prebiotic experiments rely on conditions that may have been unrealistic for early Earth?
84. Can researchers reconstruct a plausible sequence of connected steps rather than demonstrate isolated reactions?
85. How can laboratory results be scaled from tiny, carefully controlled systems to a geologically realistic environment?
86. What ancient rocks or chemical signatures might preserve evidence of prebiotic chemistry?
87. How can scientists tell a biological signature from a nonbiological chemical process?
88. What does the oldest surviving evidence of life actually establish—and what does it leave unknown?
89. How should researchers compare theories when each explains some stages but not others?
90. What finding would cause researchers to abandon or substantially revise a leading origin-of-life model?

## 10. Life beyond Earth and broader implications

91. If life arose on Mars, Venus, or an icy moon, how could scientists determine whether it shares ancestry with Earth life?
92. Could the same basic origin-of-life pathway work on planets with very different oceans, atmospheres, and minerals?
93. Is life likely to emerge wherever conditions are suitable, or could its origin require a very rare chain of events?
94. Which planetary environments are most likely to support prebiotic chemistry today?
95. What would a second, independent origin of life in our Solar System reveal about how common life is?
96. Could alien life use different genetic molecules, cellular boundaries, or energy systems from those on Earth?
97. How can a spacecraft distinguish living organisms from complex nonliving chemistry?
98. What measurements should a mission prioritize to test for past or present life?
99. If Earth life began elsewhere and arrived here, what evidence could distinguish that from an Earth-based origin?
100. How would discovering independent life beyond Earth change scientific estimates of how often life emerges?

A useful way to narrow this list is to pick one bottleneck—**the first replicator, the first metabolism, the first protocell, or the transition to genetic encoding**—and map the competing hypotheses, supporting experiments, and missing links for that specific problem.
